## Christian response

In the Gītā a person sins "though reluctant," because desire has taken a seat even in reason.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="3.36–40" /> That is close to Augustine's mind that "commands itself, and is resisted."<Cite source="augustine-confessions" locator="VIII.9.21" /> For Rāmānuja, good and evil deeds consist in "pleasing or displeasing the Supreme Person,"<Cite source="thibaut-ramanuja-sribhashya" locator="II.2.3, p. 487" /> and a person's sins may be too many for any rite to expiate.<Cite source="govindacharya-gita-ramanuja" locator="18.66, pp. 564–565" />

Two school-specific points follow. First, Advaita's root ignorance is not ignorance of duties. It is the Self's misidentification with body and mind, an error "which has its cause in wrong knowledge."<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra" locator="I.1.1, intro., pp. 4, 9" /> From the false "I" and "mine" come fear and grasping.<Cite source="sep-shankara" locator="§3.4" /> One can know a prohibition and still break it for a misconceived self; knowing wrongdoing fits the diagnosis. The Christian disputes the order, not the phenomenon. Augustine traces evil to a will that made itself "a kind of end to itself."<Cite source="augustine-city-of-god" locator="XIV.13" /> Love comes first; the false self-image serves it. Error that yields to discipline and insight favours Advaita's order; resistance born of preferring the flattering self favours the Christian's. Advaita's requirement of dispassion before knowledge<Cite source="sep-shankara" locator="§4.1" /> can be read either way. The dispute concerns an ultimate premise.

Second, theistic Vedānta has a personal Lord and real guilt; the question is its removal. For Rāmānuja, the devotee's loving knowledge propitiates the Lord and destroys his displeasure.<Cite source="thibaut-ramanuja-sribhashya" locator="IV.1.13, pp. 722–723" /> Or the Lord, "demanding no price," frees whoever takes refuge.<Cite source="govindacharya-gita-ramanuja" locator="18.66, p. 565" /> The Reformed premise is that justice must be satisfied, not only waived. Pardon rests on Christ's "full satisfaction to his Father's justice,"<Cite source="wcf" locator="11.3" /> a propitiation God himself put forward.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 3:25" /> A Vedāntin can reply that the Lord may justly remit his own displeasure, and ask why another's suffering is needed. This is a Christian theological disagreement, not a demonstrated deficiency.

## Pressure questions

- The Gītā places desire in reason itself. If that is so, what keeps the knowledge or discipline offered as the cure from being steered by the same desire?
- For Advaita: if liberating knowledge makes past actions impotent and shows that individual agency was superimposed, is a wrong done to another person still something someone answers for at the ultimate level, or only at the conventional one?
- For Rāmānuja and Madhva: the Lord allots the fruits of action justly. When he removes his displeasure at sin, whether through devotion or by free mercy, what makes that removal just as well as merciful?

## Deep dive

#### A Christian note on school differences (optional)

The Christian should also notice how close Rāmānuja comes to Paul. In Rāmānuja, those resolved on what displeases the Lord are given "a delight in such actions" as their punishment.<Cite source="thibaut-ramanuja-sribhashya" locator="II.3.41, pp. 557–558" /> Paul says that God "gave them up" to their desires.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 1:24, 28" /> Both see escalating sin as judgment, not only as habit. For this reason the first point above does not apply to Rāmānuja or Madhva. Both trace wrongdoing to a dependent agent's own effort under a personal Lord, and for Madhva liberation depends on Viṣṇu's grace.<Cite source="iep-madhva" locator="§3" /> With these schools the disagreement concerns the ground of forgiveness, not whether sin is personal.
