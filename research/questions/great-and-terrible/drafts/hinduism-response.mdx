## Christian response

The Christian recognizes much here. In the Gītā a person sins "though reluctant," because desire has taken a seat even in reason.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="3.36–40" /> That is close to Augustine's mind that "commands itself, and is resisted."<Cite source="augustine-confessions" locator="VIII.9.21" /> Rāmānuja goes further. Good and evil deeds consist in "pleasing or displeasing the Supreme Person,"<Cite source="thibaut-ramanuja-sribhashya" locator="II.2.3, p. 487" /> and a person's sins may be too many for any rite to expiate.<Cite source="govindacharya-gita-ramanuja" locator="18.66, pp. 564–565" />

The Christian presses two school-specific points.

First, in Advaita the deepest root is cognitive. Śaṅkara calls superimposition, the root of evil, an error "which has its cause in wrong knowledge."<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra" locator="I.1.1, intro., pp. 4, 9" /> Knowledge is therefore the cure, and it renders past actions "impotent."<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="4.37, comm., pp. 134–135" /> Nyāya and Yoga likewise put ignorance first. The Christian reverses the order. Paul describes people who knew God yet "suppress the truth,"<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 1:18–21" /> and Calvin holds it "false to say" that the sinner "sins only through ignorance."<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="II.2.22" /> On this view, ignorance is driven by a will that has made itself "a kind of end to itself."<Cite source="augustine-city-of-god" locator="XIV.13" /> This disputes an ultimate premise and its explanatory adequacy; it is not a charge of incoherence.

Second, theistic Vedānta has a personal Lord and real guilt, so the question there is how guilt is removed. For Rāmānuja, the devotee's loving knowledge propitiates the Lord and destroys his displeasure.<Cite source="thibaut-ramanuja-sribhashya" locator="IV.1.13, pp. 722–723" /> Alternatively, the Lord, "demanding no price," frees whoever takes refuge in him.<Cite source="govindacharya-gita-ramanuja" locator="18.66, p. 565" /> The Christian asks how this mercy upholds justice. Scripture's answer is that God himself put forward the propitiation, so that he is "just and the justifier."<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 3:25–26" /> Christ made "full satisfaction to his Father's justice."<Cite source="wcf" locator="11.3" /> This concerns redemptive adequacy, argued from revelation.

## Pressure questions

- The Gītā places desire in reason itself. If that is so, what keeps the knowledge or discipline offered as the cure from being steered by the same desire?
- For Advaita: if liberating knowledge makes past actions impotent and shows that individual agency was superimposed, is a wrong done to another person still something someone answers for at the ultimate level, or only at the conventional one?
- For Rāmānuja and Madhva: if sin's power lies in the Lord's displeasure, what makes his removal of it, whether through the devotee's devotion or by free mercy, consistent with his justice as the one who allots the fruits of action?

## Deep dive

#### A Christian note on school differences (optional)

The Christian should also notice how close Rāmānuja comes to Paul. In Rāmānuja, those resolved on what displeases the Lord are given "a delight in such actions" as their punishment.<Cite source="thibaut-ramanuja-sribhashya" locator="II.3.41, pp. 557–558" /> Paul says that God "gave them up" to their desires.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 1:24, 28" /> Both see escalating sin as judgment, not only as habit. For this reason the first point above does not apply to Rāmānuja or Madhva. Both trace wrongdoing to a dependent agent's own effort under a personal Lord, and for Madhva liberation depends on Viṣṇu's grace.<Cite source="iep-madhva" locator="§3" /> With these schools the disagreement concerns the ground of forgiveness, not whether sin is personal.
