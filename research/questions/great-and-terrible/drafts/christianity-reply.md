## 1. Revised `## Strongest objection` (≈ 258 words)

```mdx
## Strongest objection

The objection does not deny the darkness; it denies that the Reformed mechanism explains it. Kant grants a propensity to evil that is universal and "radical" because "it corrupts the ground of all maxims."<Cite source="kant-religion-greene-hudson" locator="Bk. I, §III" /> Yet guilt, he argues, must be self-incurred: a person "must make or have made himself" whatever he is morally, "for otherwise he could not be held responsible for it."<Cite source="kant-religion-greene-hudson" locator="Bk. I, General Observation" /> John Taylor pressed this against federal headship: that another should so represent me "that when he is guilty I am to be reputed guilty," and "all this before I am born," is "unreasonable."<Cite source="taylor-original-sin-supplement" locator="pp. 108–109" /> The result is a dilemma. If inherited corruption leaves us unable to do good, the cruelty it explains is misfortune, not guilt; one who sins from a principle "never in his Power to command" would "be no Sinner at all."<Cite source="taylor-original-sin" locator="Part III, p. 222" /> If we are truly guilty, the guilt must be our own, and Adam's imputed guilt does no work.

The greatness side presses the same way. "The injunction that we ought to become better men resounds unabatedly in our souls; hence this must be within our power."<Cite source="kant-religion-greene-hudson" locator="Bk. I, General Observation" /> Unbelievers reason well, love sacrificially and die bravely; George Turnbull set against history's crimes "the exceeding generous and brave actions with which history shines."<Cite source="edwards-works-hickman-1" locator="Original Sin I.1.1" /> So either the capacity for good is intact and "inability" is false, or, once qualified by common grace and a civil–spiritual distinction, it no longer predicts anything we observe. Few philosophers now defend original guilt.<Cite source="sep-sin-christian" locator="§4.3" /> A mixed creature with a self-incurred bent to evil seems to explain the same facts more simply.
```

## 2. `## Christian reply` (≈ 299 words)

```mdx
## Christian reply

The reply distinguishes two kinds of inability. Edwards grants that natural inability, a lack of faculties or opportunity, excuses; for it, "ought implies can" holds. Moral inability is different: it "consists in the opposition or want of inclination," so that nothing is lacking "but a will."<Cite source="edwards-works-hickman-1" locator="Freedom of the Will I.4" /> Commands address the will itself, so a will that cannot love God because it does not love him is not excused by that fact.<Cite source="edwards-works-hickman-1" locator="Freedom of the Will III.4" /> Against Taylor, Edwards argues that if sin must always flow from a prior sinful choice, the first such choice could not itself be sin.<Cite source="edwards-works-hickman-1" locator="Original Sin IV.1" /> Culpability belongs to what the will is, not only to how it began. What remains is a disputed ultimate premise, which neither side demonstrates: whether responsibility requires self-origination.

On imputation, Edwards holds that a person's inborn corrupt disposition is a real consent of heart to the first sin, with guilt following that disposition "as it was in the case of Adam himself."<Cite source="edwards-works-hickman-1" locator="Original Sin IV.3" /> Not all Reformed theologians accept his account of identity, and they also differ on whether guilt is imputed immediately or through inherited corruption. The main argument is from Scripture: Paul sets one man's disobedience beside another's obedience.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 5:18–19" /> The Reformed draw the corollary that an objection to representation in principle would also count against the imputation of Christ's righteousness. That is argued from revelation, not demonstrated from neutral premises.

The empirical strand targets a narrower claim than it supposes. The inability confessed concerns "spiritual good accompanying salvation,"<Cite source="wcf" locator="9.3" /> and the Confession allows that unbelievers' works may be "of good use both to themselves and others."<Cite source="wcf" locator="16.7" /> Calvin calls such virtues "special gifts of God."<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="II.3.4" /> What the doctrine predicts is not the absence of virtue but natural virtue such as pity and gratitude, which falls short of love to God.<Cite source="edwards-works-hickman-1" locator="True Virtue ch. 7" /> How testable that prediction is remains disputed.
```

Self-check against the brief and the coordinator's instructions:
- Engages the objection as stated: both horns (misfortune vs. own guilt), Kant's "ought implies can",
  and the empirical strand.
- Concessions stated plainly: (a) self-origination is a disputed ultimate premise; (b) the justice of
  imputation is argued from Scripture and the Adam–Christ parallel, not from neutral premises;
  (c) Reformed theologians differ on Edwards's identity metaphysics and on mediate vs. immediate
  imputation; (d) the testability of the civil/spiritual prediction is disputed.
- No claim that the objection "fails". The kind of disagreement left is named: a disputed premise
  about responsibility, plus an argument from revelation.
- The Adam–Christ corollary is given as the Reformed argument, without a citation that would wrongly
  attribute it to a particular author. Witsius makes the parallel (I.8.31) but does not frame it as
  "if one is unjust, so is the other". That framing is the Reformed argument in general, so it is not
  attributed to him.
- Strands considered but left out for length: general revelation and the image (they are covered in
  "The Christian view" and the Deep dive); corruption rather than annihilation (II.2.12, also in the
  view section).

## 3. Optional Deep-dive addition (≈ 222 words)

To be appended to the existing `## Deep dive` (after "Where Reformed theologians differ" or before it).

```mdx
#### Edwards against Taylor

Edwards wrote *Original Sin* as a reply to Taylor. Two of its moves bear on the objection beyond what the reply can carry. First, he argues from fact before theory: every descendant of Adam comes into the world with no real prospect except sinning once he is able to act morally. Either God treats the race as one with its first father, or he subjects people regarded as wholly innocent to the same certain ruin, which "will not mend the matter."<Cite source="edwards-works-hickman-1" locator="Original Sin IV.3" /> On this argument, views that keep inherited corruption but drop inherited guilt still face the justice question, now as innocent people born with an inevitable bent to sin.

Second, he reads moral facts as evidence of a tendency. A disposition is known "by observing what is constant or general in event," especially when the effect persists against much opposition, and the tendency of a nature is what it would produce "were it not that the free mercy and kindness of God interposes."<Cite source="edwards-works-hickman-1" locator="Original Sin I.1.1" /> That is why the doctrine of restraint is not, on his view, a post hoc patch: the claim concerns the will's direction, not how often crimes occur.

Edwards's own answer to the identity question, that all created identity depends on God's constitution, is distinctive and is debated among Reformed theologians. The confessional doctrine does not depend on it.
```
