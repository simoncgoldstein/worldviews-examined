## Christian response

Christianity shares much here: one good Creator, the image of God in every person, the Hebrew Scriptures, real responsibility, covenant, and a God who forgives those who return to him. The disagreements concern how the heart is renewed and on what ground sin is forgiven.

The first is a Christian theological disagreement. Both traditions affirm real change now. Complete *teshuvah* means meeting the same temptation and refraining,<Cite source="maimonides-mishneh-torah-touger" locator="Teshuvah 2:1" /> the penitent is again "beloved and desirable" before God,<Cite source="maimonides-mishneh-torah-touger" locator="Teshuvah 7:4" /> and God helps the one who struggles.<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Sukkah 52b" /> Both also expect a further change. Nachmanides reads the promised circumcision of the heart<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Deuteronomy 30:6" /> as messianic, linked to Jeremiah's new covenant and Ezekiel's "new heart". Until then, he holds, choice remains and people need instruction.<Cite source="ramban-torah-chavel" locator="on Deuteronomy 30:6" /> Reformed theology likewise holds that corruption remains in believers until glory.<Cite source="wcf" locator="13.2" /> Nachmanides concedes only that evil desire persists until then. The difference lies in who begins the change. For Nachmanides, God assists whoever "comes to purify himself".<Cite source="ramban-torah-chavel" locator="on Deuteronomy 30:6" /> Reformed theology holds that Ezekiel's new heart is given now, in regeneration,<Cite source="wcf" locator="13.1" /> and that a person is "altogether passive" until the Spirit renews him.<Cite source="wcf" locator="10.2" />

The second, also a Christian theological disagreement, concerns atonement. The shared Scriptures do not tie every pardon to a slain animal. A poor person's flour offering brings forgiveness,<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Leviticus 5:11–13" /> and Solomon asks God to forgive repentant exiles who pray toward the Temple.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="1 Kings 8:46–50" /> The Mishnah teaches that repentance, Yom Kippur and death atone.<Cite source="mishnah-kulp" locator="Yoma 8:8" /> The Christian agrees that God forgave penitent Israelites.<Cite source="wcf" locator="7.5" /> He argues that every such pardon rests on one sacrifice,<Cite source="wcf" locator="8.6" /> that of the servant whose soul makes "an offering for guilt".<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Isaiah 53:10" /> Rashi reads that servant as Israel.<Cite source="rashi-tanakh-judaica-press" locator="on Isaiah 53:3" /> Identifying him with Jesus rests on the New Testament's witness, not on the Tanakh alone.

## Pressure questions

- Rabbinic sources describe real change now, through complete *teshuvah* and God's help, and a further change in the messianic age, when good becomes natural. What will the circumcised heart give that complete *teshuvah* does not? In present change, is the first move the penitent's or God's?
- Within the tradition's own sources, the evil inclination belongs to creation's "very good", yet "even its Creator calls it evil", and God will one day slaughter it. Is it best understood as a created good to be ruled, or as a disorder to be removed?
- Where Jews and Christians differ: the shared Scriptures know forgiveness through animal sacrifice, a poor person's flour offering, and the prayer of repentant exiles far from the altar. Since the Temple's destruction, are the sacrifices best understood as one God-given means of a mercy that never depended on them alone? Or are they replaced by prayer and repentance, fulfilled in another form, or awaiting restoration?

#### Optional Deep-dive paragraph (Christian side): Shared text, rabbinic reading, Christian claim

Three layers should be kept apart. The Hebrew Bible is shared ground. Its verdict on the heart (Gen 6:5; Jer 17:9) and its promise of a new heart (Deut 30:6; Ezek 36:26) belong to Jews and Christians alike. Rabbinic Judaism developed its own reading of those texts after the Second Temple: Torah study, *teshuvah* and Yom Kippur in the present, and the circumcised heart in the messianic age. The specifically Christian claim is that the new covenant has already begun in Jesus. It rests on the New Testament's testimony to his death and resurrection. Reformed theology holds that the covenant of grace is "one and the same" across both testaments.<Cite source="wcf" locator="7.6" /> On that view the Christian is not claiming that Israel's faith was graceless. The claim is that its promises have been kept. Note that Jeremiah 31:31–34 in English Bibles is 31:30–33 in Jewish editions.
