## Christian response

The Christian recognizes the analysis of craving and self-grasping as exact. Mahānāma understands that greed corrupts the mind and is still overtaken by it.<Cite source="majjhima-nikaya-sujato" locator="MN 14.2–4" /> Augustine found the same in himself: a perverse will hardened into habit, and "custom not resisted became necessity."<Cite source="augustine-confessions" locator="VIII.5.10" /> Śāntideva's teaching that the world's joy comes from wanting others to be happy<Cite source="shantideva-bodhicaryavatara" locator="8.129" /> stands close to Augustine's two loves.<Cite source="augustine-city-of-god" locator="XIV.28" />

The Christian does not argue that Buddhism lacks real wrongs against others. Its practice asks one to recognize a transgression, make amends and properly accept another's confession.<Cite source="anguttara-nikaya-sujato" locator="AN 3.4" /> A monk who refuses a fellow monk's confession is called a fool.<Cite source="samyutta-nikaya-sujato" locator="SN 11.24" /> Rāhula is told to confess a harmful deed to a teacher or companion and to restrain himself afterward.<Cite source="majjhima-nikaya-sujato" locator="MN 61.11" /> No-self, rightly understood, keeps a conventional doer who acts and reaps.<Cite source="buddhaghosa-path-of-purification" locator="XIX.19–20" /> Conscience rests on self-respect and respect for others.<Cite source="buddhaghosa-path-of-purification" locator="XIV.142" />

The Christian addition is narrower: every wrong is also an offence against God. Scripture puts the root in refusal: people "knew God" yet did not honour him.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 1:21" /> "Against you, you only, have I sinned"<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Psalm 51:4" /> names an answerability that, the Christian argues, no human party's forgiveness discharges. This is a claim of explanatory adequacy resting on a disputed ultimate premise: that a personal God exists to whom persons answer.

That shapes the remedy (redemptive adequacy). If wrongdoing offends God as well as harming others, it needs God's forgiveness as well as cleansing.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="1 John 1:9" /> Gradualist schools cure ignorance by cultivated knowledge.<Cite source="nagarjuna-mmk" locator="26.11" /> Shinran instead relies on Amida's vow, made for the evil person.<Cite source="shinran-tannisho-cws" locator="3" /> That is close to grace. The remaining disagreement is theological: whether the one who saves is also the God who was wronged, so that deliverance is personal forgiveness and not only release.

## Pressure questions

- Buddhist practice treats wrongs against others as real and answerable through confession and amends. If someone has made amends and been forgiven by everyone they harmed, yet still feels a guilt that no human party can lift, how should that remainder be understood?
- Does a person's worth rest on the rare opportunity of human birth, on a buddha-nature shared by all beings, or on something belonging to this particular person? Which best explains the honour owed to someone who squanders the opportunity?
- If latent tendencies outlast understanding, what finally uproots them: cultivated insight, the power of practice, or another's vow? What reason does each school give for thinking that power is sufficient?

#### Aspiration, buddha-nature and dignity: a Christian reading

The *Ratnagotravibhāga* reads moral aspiration as evidence of the buddha-element in all beings.<Cite source="ratnagotravibhaga-johnston" locator="I.40–41" /> The Christian offers a parallel reading: aspiration and conscience are remnants of the image of God and of an ineradicable sense of deity.<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="I.3.1" /> The two readings differ over the ground. Theravāda sources locate the value of human life chiefly in its rare opportunity.<Cite source="samyutta-nikaya-sujato" locator="SN 56.47–48" /> Buddha-nature texts locate it in a nature all beings share, which Dōgen refuses to treat as a self.<Cite source="dogen-shobogenzo-ja" locator="Busshō" /> The Christian argues that the image belongs to each person and grounds honour owed even to the unworthy and the enemy.<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="III.7.6" /> Tiantai holds that evil is inherently included in buddhahood.<Cite source="sep-buddhism-tiantai" locator="§4.1" /> Here the disagreement sharpens, because for Augustine evil is a privation of good, not a constituent of what is real.<Cite source="augustine-enchiridion" locator="11" /> These are Christian theological disagreements, not demonstrations.
