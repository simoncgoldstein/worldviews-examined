## Christian response

The Christian can grant most of this account. Its mechanisms, from Hume's mixed constitution and Darwin's remorse to Kitcher's "incomplete psychological altruists" and two kinds of aggression, are real, and Christian theology has no stake in denying them. Calvin traced truth, wherever found, to the Spirit, "the only fountain of truth."<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="II.2.15" /> The disagreement lies at two deeper points.

The first concerns explanatory adequacy. The phenomenon includes a conscience that accuses the wrongdoer himself.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 2:14–15" /> Mackie, a naturalist, held that ordinary moral judgments claim this categorical authority and that naturalist analyses leave it out;<Cite source="mackie-ethics" locator="p. 33" /> he concluded that the claim is an error.<Cite source="mackie-ethics" locator="p. 35" /> Railton's realism is stronger: rightness is what would be approved if everyone's interests counted equally.<Cite source="railton-moral-realism" locator="p. 190" /> But Railton grants that his account does not give every rational agent a reason to be moral regardless of his desires.<Cite source="railton-moral-realism" locator="p. 201" /> His "sensible knave" can admit that his conduct is unjust and ask, "But what is that to me?"<Cite source="railton-moral-realism" locator="p. 169" /> The Christian argues that obligation binds the knave too, because it is owed to God, whose law convicts people "on their own testimony."<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="II.2.22" />

The second is a premise the account presupposes rather than grounds. Railton says his criterion "presupposes" a substantive moral theory,<Cite source="railton-moral-realism" locator="p. 190" /> and capacity-based views make dignity a matter of degree. Scripture grounds equal worth in a status, not a capacity: murder is forbidden "for God made man in his own image."<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Genesis 9:6" /> Infants and people with dementia hold that status fully. Neither point denies that naturalists know right from wrong or hold equal dignity firmly. The claim is that this moral seriousness outruns what the account grounds.

## Pressure questions

- If someone fully understands that an act is cruel and simply does not care, does he still have a reason not to do it? If not, in what sense is he obligated?
- Is the equal worth of every person a discovery about human beings, a commitment we have chosen, or the best product of a contingent history? Does the answer change anything for those who cannot press their own claims, such as infants or people with advanced dementia?
- If remorse is a lasting social instinct reasserting itself, what makes it accurate rather than merely strong?

#### Railton's analogy with logic

Railton answers the knave with an analogy. A person may have no reason to purge every contradiction from his beliefs, yet logic is not arbitrary. Likewise moral evaluation is not arbitrary, because moral conduct "is rational from an impartial point of view."<Cite source="railton-moral-realism" locator="p. 202" /> The Christian finds the analogy apt, since both standards bind whether or not one wants them. But it moves the question rather than settling it: why should the impartial standpoint have authority over someone who rejects it? Railton notes that people have sacrificed even their lives for religions and ideologies that claimed to express "the universal" justificatory standpoint.<Cite source="railton-moral-realism" locator="p. 202" /> The Christian reads that pervasive concern as the work of the law written on the heart.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 2:14–15" /> That reading is argued from Scripture, not demonstrated from neutral premises.
