## The view

In mainstream Sunni thought, one created nature explains both human greatness and human wickedness. God honoured every child of Adam and favoured them above much of creation.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="17:70" /> Humanity alone took on the Trust that the heavens, the earth and the mountains refused, and the same verse calls humanity "inept and foolish".<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="33:72" /> Every child is born on the *fitra*, the original nature, which many scholars read as an orientation toward the Creator, and is then shaped by upbringing.<Cite source="bukhari-sahih-khan" locator="1385" /> God formed the soul and inspired it to know "its own rebellion and piety".<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="91:8" />

Al-Ghazali's psychology shows how one root bears both kinds of fruit. The "lordly" element in the heart is what longs to know all things, and it is also what craves mastery and despotism. The discernment that raises humans above the beasts is what makes cunning and deceit possible.<Cite source="ghazali-marvels-heart" locator="pp. 29–30" /> Evil, then, is created power turned to the wrong end, and each soul answers for its own deeds.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="6:164" /> The remedy is purification of the soul.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="91:9–10" /> It comes through repentance and guidance, under a mercy that forbids despair.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="39:53" />

## What this explains well

The account treats vices as good powers in disorder. Al-Ghazali says anger is needed for self-defence and appetite for life itself, so the aim is to govern them, not to destroy them.<Cite source="ghazali-disciplining-soul" locator="pp. 27–28" /> This explains why great gifts and great dangers so often travel together.

It explains why everyone falls while keeping responsibility personal. Desire matures before intellect,<Cite source="ghazali-repentance-stern" locator="p. 43" /> and habit gradually crusts over the heart.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="83:14" />

It gives a precise map of knowingly doing wrong. Al-Ghazali traces an act through inclination, a judgment that sets modesty and fear aside, and resolve, and places responsibility at that judgment.<Cite source="ghazali-marvels-heart" locator="pp. 118–119" />

It holds universal dignity<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="17:70" /> together with real moral ranking.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="49:13" />

And it offers grounded hope. Character can change,<Cite source="ghazali-disciplining-soul" locator="p. 25" /> and God delights in repentance.<Cite source="bukhari-sahih-khan" locator="6309" />

## Deep dive

#### Scope

This entry represents mainstream Sunni thought. Its main voice is al-Ghazali's *Revival of the Religious Sciences*, which combines Ash'ari theology with Sufi moral psychology and addresses this question more directly than any other classical Sunni work. Maturidi theology, Ibn Taymiyya's traditionalism and the philosophical and mystical strands are noted where they change the answer.

#### Honour, trust and the angels' question

The Qur'an's first scene of human origins already holds the paradox. God announces a successor (*khalifa*) on earth. The angels ask how He can place there "someone who will cause damage and bloodshed". God answers, "I know things you do not".<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="2:30" /> He then displays Adam's knowledge of the names, which the angels lack.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="2:31–33" /> Commentators disagree whether *khalifa* means a deputy of God or one generation succeeding another. Either way, the same creature is both the one who sheds blood and the one who knows.

Iblis supplies the pattern of pride: "I am better than him".<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="7:12" /> But Satan can only call. On the Day of Judgment he tells those who followed him to "blame yourselves".<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="14:22" />

#### Fitra and personal responsibility

Adam and his wife confess, "we have wronged our souls".<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="7:23" /> Afterwards God "brought him close, accepted his repentance, and guided him".<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="20:122" /> Adam's lapse is answered by his own repentance, and each of his descendants is born on the *fitra*. In a hadith in which God speaks, He says He created His servants inclined to worship Him, "but it is Satan who turns them away".<Cite source="muslim-sahih-siddiqui" locator="2865a" />

Ibn Taymiyya develops this further than anyone. On his reading, the *fitra* is the religion of Islam. It exists at birth only as a potency, and it becomes actual knowledge of God as the child's capacities of knowing and willing mature, unless something impedes it.<Cite source="hoover-theodicy" locator="p. 41" /> He compares it to an infant's drive for its mother's milk and calls it stronger still. The same nature loves justice and hates injustice. In Jon Hoover's summary, a willing being "necessarily has a god toward which it directs its love".<Cite source="hoover-theodicy" locator="p. 42" /> Satans, parents, false doctrines and pride can corrupt it.<Cite source="hoover-theodicy" locator="p. 43" /> The prophets were sent, Ibn Taymiyya writes, "to perfect the natural constitution and firmly establish it, not to replace it and change it".<Cite source="hoover-theodicy" locator="p. 44" />

On this view, devotion and idolatry are one capacity for love, aimed rightly or wrongly. The Qur'an names the wrong aim: the one "who has taken his own desire as a god".<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="45:23" />

#### Al-Ghazali's anatomy of the heart

For al-Ghazali, the real human being is the heart, a subtle "lordly" reality that knows and is held responsible.<Cite source="ghazali-marvels-heart" locator="p. 6" /> Man stands between the beasts and the angels. What sets him apart is knowledge of the real nature of things.<Cite source="ghazali-marvels-heart" locator="p. 25" />

Four kinds of quality meet in every heart. There is appetite, there is anger, and there is a demonic cunning that "sets forth evil as though it were good". The fourth is a lordly element which, left undisciplined, "claims lordship for himself and loves mastery … and despotism".<Cite source="ghazali-marvels-heart" locator="pp. 29–30" /> So inside one skin live "a pig, a dog, a demon, and a sage".<Cite source="ghazali-marvels-heart" locator="p. 30" /> When the intellect serves appetite, the result is "the possessor possessed, the lord lorded over". When the order is reversed, the heart becomes a home for knowledge, wisdom and certainty.<Cite source="ghazali-marvels-heart" locator="p. 31" /> Each sin darkens the heart's mirror like smoke, and obedience and repentance polish it clean.<Cite source="ghazali-marvels-heart" locator="pp. 32–33" />

#### How one knowingly does wrong

The Qur'an names the phenomenon directly: "How can you tell people to do what is right and forget to do it yourselves?"<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="2:44" /> The Prophet defined vice as "what rankles in your heart" and what you would not want others to know.<Cite source="muslim-sahih-siddiqui" locator="2553a" />

Al-Ghazali traces an act through four stages:
- an involuntary thought;
- a natural inclination;
- a judgment that the thing should be done, reached once inhibitions such as modesty or fear have been set aside, sometimes by reflection;
- resolve.

Accountability begins with the judgment.<Cite source="ghazali-marvels-heart" locator="pp. 118–119" /> One of his types of moral condition is the person who "knows that he is not acting as he should" because desire has mastered his better judgment, yet who can still be reformed.<Cite source="ghazali-disciplining-soul" locator="p. 26" /> What finally moves such a person, al-Ghazali argues, is knowledge that has become certainty "overwhelming his heart".<Cite source="ghazali-repentance-stern" locator="pp. 31–32" />

Ibn Taymiyya is more intellectualist. "The root of what makes people fall into evil deeds is ignorance." He means not knowing that a deed brings greater harm, or thinking that it brings greater profit. Hoover notes the resulting tension with his view that only knowing, deliberate disobedience deserves full punishment.<Cite source="hoover-theodicy" locator="pp. 201–202" />

#### School differences

In Ash'ari theology, God creates every human act, disobedience included. Nothing a human acquires lies outside His will.<Cite source="ashari-ibana-klein" locator="p. 104" /> As Jon McGinnis summarizes the school, moral obligation rests on revelation rather than on properties that reason detects.<Cite source="sep-arabic-islamic-religion" locator="§7.2" />

Maturidi theology describes a person as material nature plus intellect. Nature desires what intellect rejects, so the human test is passed by rational enquiry.<Cite source="harvey-transcendent-god" locator="p. 27" /> Interpreters differ on how far al-Maturidi trusts unaided moral reason. He affirms real human choice (*ikhtiyar*) within God's creation of acts.

Ibn Taymiyya traces sin to a lack. In his words, the "existent sins … are his punishment for his not doing what God created him to do and what He constituted him naturally to do".<Cite source="hoover-theodicy" locator="pp. 202–203" />

#### Hard texts on rank and destiny

The tradition's own record includes hard verses. Classical commentators read them in different ways, but they belong to the answer:
- Humanity's acceptance of the Trust comes with the verdict "inept and foolish".<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="33:72" />
- The human created "in the finest state" is reduced "to the lowest of the low", except those who believe and do good deeds.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="95:4–6" />
- Many jinn and humans "destined for Hell" are described as "like cattle, no, even further astray", with hearts they do not use.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="7:179" />
- Al-Ash'ari's creed states that God gives grace to believers and "causes the infidels to err". It also says He could make them believers but wills that they remain unbelievers.<Cite source="ashari-ibana-klein" locator="pp. 51–52" />

On these readings, the honour of 17:70 belongs to every child of Adam, while final standing before God follows mindfulness of Him and God's decree.

#### Philosophical and mystical strands

In Dimitri Gutas's account, Ibn Sina locates human greatness in the rational soul. Its real happiness is perfection through knowledge, which it must work to attain.<Cite source="sep-ibn-sina" locator="§4" /> In William Chittick's presentation of Ibn Arabi's contested but influential metaphysics, humans are made in the form of the all-comprehensive divine name and are "compelled to be free". Only because they can choose wrong can they realize the full range of divine qualities.<Cite source="sep-ibn-arabi" locator="§5.3" />

#### Remedy

The remedy matches the diagnosis. If character could not change, al-Ghazali argues, "there would be no value in counsels, sermons and discipline".<Cite source="ghazali-disciplining-soul" locator="p. 25" /> Repentance has three parts: knowledge, remorse, and a resolve that turns into changed conduct.<Cite source="ghazali-repentance-stern" locator="p. 31" /> Angels hold to pure good and devils to unrepented evil, "but he who rectifies evil by returning to good, is, in truth, human".<Cite source="ghazali-repentance-stern" locator="pp. 30–31" /> The Prophet taught that no one is saved by deeds alone, only by God's mercy.<Cite source="bukhari-sahih-khan" locator="6463" />
