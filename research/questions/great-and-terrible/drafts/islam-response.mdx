## Christian response

The Christian shares a good deal with this account. Humans are creatures wholly dependent on God and made to worship. They can deny what their own souls acknowledge.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="27:14" /> Ibn Taymiyya's thesis that a willing being necessarily has a god<Cite source="hoover-theodicy" locator="p. 42" /> is close to Calvin's sense of deity engraved on every heart.<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="I.3.1" />

The first disagreement is about explanatory depth. Al-Ghazali grants that passion prevails over intellect in every human being, so repentance is required of all.<Cite source="ghazali-repentance-stern" locator="p. 43" /> He explains this by sequence: desire matures before intellect, in a heart originally receptive to angel and demon "with no preponderance".<Cite source="ghazali-marvels-heart" locator="p. 80" /> The Christian argues that a fall without exception is better read as a tendency of the nature itself, known, as Edwards puts it, "by observing what is constant or general in event".<Cite source="edwards-works-hickman-1" locator="Original Sin I.1.1" /> On that reading the root is a will that suppresses truth it already knows,<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 1:18–21" /> one "opposite to all good".<Cite source="wcf" locator="6.4" /> This is a claim about explanatory adequacy, not a charge of incoherence. Al-Ghazali's rusted mirror is itself a serious account of hardening.

The second disagreement is about redemptive adequacy. The tradition teaches that no one is saved by deeds, only by God's mercy.<Cite source="bukhari-sahih-khan" locator="6463" /> The Christian asks how that forgiveness is also just. On the Ash'ari view as McGinnis summarizes it, the ethical rules that bind humans do not apply to God.<Cite source="sep-arabic-islamic-religion" locator="§5" /> Christians hold that God forgives so "that he might be just and the justifier",<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 3:26" /> because Christ made "a proper, real, and full satisfaction" to divine justice.<Cite source="wcf" locator="11.3" /> The traditions disagree at a basic level about whether justice requires satisfaction.

## Pressure questions

- If the heart begins with "no preponderance" between angel and demon, why does passion, as al-Ghazali himself affirms, come to prevail over intellect in every human being without exception?
- Ibn Taymiyya traces evil deeds to ignorance of their harm, yet the Qur'an describes people who denied the signs "even though their souls acknowledged them as true" (27:14). Is knowing wrongdoing finally a failure of knowledge, or of love?
- When God forgives the repentant, is anything done about the wrong itself, or is it remitted by sovereign mercy alone? Do the Ash'ari, Maturidi and Hanbali answers differ here?

## Deep dive

#### Mercy that turns first (Christian response, optional)

The Qur'an itself places God's turning before the sinner's return. Of the three who stayed behind, "He turned to them in mercy in order for them to return".<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="9:118" /> Adam, too, "received some words from his Lord" before his repentance was accepted.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="2:37" /> Al-Ghazali adds that what finally moves a person to repent is knowledge that has become certainty "overwhelming his heart".<Cite source="ghazali-repentance-stern" locator="pp. 31–32" /> So Islamic teaching on repentance does not rest on self-reform alone, and the Christian should not present it that way. Two differences remain. The first is whether God's turning renews the will at its root, so that sinners come "most freely, being made willing by his grace".<Cite source="wcf" locator="10.1" /> The second is whether pardon needs a ground beyond repentance. The Confession denies that repentance is "any satisfaction for sin, or any cause of the pardon thereof".<Cite source="wcf" locator="15.3" />
