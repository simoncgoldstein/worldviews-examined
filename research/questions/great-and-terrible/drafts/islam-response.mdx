## Christian response

Al-Ghazali's developmental account is a real explanation, and universality alone does not defeat it. If desire matures before intellect and habit then crusts over the heart, passion should prevail in everyone, as he says it does.<Cite source="ghazali-repentance-stern" locator="p. 43" /> That fact fits his account and inherited corruption equally well.

The Christian presses a narrower point. The Qur'an describes people who rejected the signs "in their wickedness and their pride, even though their souls acknowledged them as true".<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="27:14" /> Paul describes those who knew God, did not honour him, and "suppress the truth".<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 1:18–21" /> Both texts describe adult refusal aimed at God, which a lag in maturation does not obviously explain. The Christian reads it as corruption in the will's orientation toward God, "opposite to all good".<Cite source="wcf" locator="6.4" /> A Muslim can reply that early rust and knowledge short of certainty explain it too. The evidence underdetermines the choice. The Reformed reading rests finally on revelation, so this is a Christian theological disagreement.

On the remedy, Islamic teaching does not pass over the wrong done to others. A wrongdoer must seek pardon from the one he wronged; otherwise his good deeds go to his victim at judgment.<Cite source="bukhari-sahih-khan" locator="2449" /> The "bankrupt" arrives with prayer and fasting and loses them to those he abused, robbed and beat.<Cite source="muslim-sahih-siddiqui" locator="2581" /> The Christian's question concerns the other claim, the offence against God himself. Reformed theology holds, as a premise, that God's justice requires satisfaction for that offence, so that he is "just and the justifier".<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 3:26" /> It finds that satisfaction in Christ.<Cite source="wcf" locator="11.3" /> The premise is distinctively Christian; Muslim theologians ground pardon in God's own will and mercy. The early al-Ash'ari, for instance, held that the ethical rules binding humans do not apply to God.<Cite source="sep-arabic-islamic-religion" locator="§5" /> This too is a theological disagreement, not a demonstrated deficiency.

## Pressure questions

- Al-Ghazali's sequence explains why passion gains a head start in childhood. Does it also explain the adult refusal of 27:14, made "in their wickedness and their pride" against what the soul acknowledges, or does that refusal need a further root?
- Ibn Taymiyya traces evil deeds to ignorance of their harm. When someone sins fully aware of the harm, what is missing: knowledge, certainty, or love?
- Wrongs against other people are settled at judgment (Bukhari 2449). For the offence against God himself, does justice require anything beyond sincere repentance and God's free pardon, such as satisfaction? Do Ash'ari, Maturidi and Hanbali theologians answer differently?

## Deep dive

#### Mercy that turns first (Christian response, optional)

The Qur'an itself places God's turning before the sinner's return. Of the three who stayed behind, "He turned to them in mercy in order for them to return".<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="9:118" /> Adam, too, "received some words from his Lord" before his repentance was accepted.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="2:37" /> Al-Ghazali adds that what finally moves a person to repent is knowledge that has become certainty "overwhelming his heart".<Cite source="ghazali-repentance-stern" locator="pp. 31–32" /> So Islamic teaching on repentance does not rest on self-reform alone, and the Christian should not present it that way. Two differences remain. The first is whether God's turning renews the will at its root, so that sinners come "most freely, being made willing by his grace".<Cite source="wcf" locator="10.1" /> The second is whether pardon needs a ground beyond repentance. The Confession denies that repentance is "any satisfaction for sin, or any cause of the pardon thereof".<Cite source="wcf" locator="15.3" />
