## The view

Hindu traditions read the human being as an embodied self (ātman) whose own nature is conscious. In theistic Vedānta that self also belongs to God: Rāmānuja reads the Bhagavad Gītā's "higher Nature" and "portion of Myself" as the real, eternal soul.<Cite source="govindacharya-gita-ramanuja" locator="7.5; 15.7" /> A Vedic forest-text, the Aitareya Āraṇyaka, marks the human as "most endowed with knowledge," the creature who "knows what is to happen to-morrow" and "by means of the mortal ... desires the immortal."<Cite source="muller-upanishads-1" locator="Aitareya Āraṇyaka II.3.2.4" />

Wrongdoing arises when that self, bound in a body-mind made of three strands (guṇas), is overrun by desire. Arjuna asks why a person sins "though reluctant, as if constrained by force." Krishna answers that desire and wrath, born of rajas, veil wisdom through the senses, the mind and even reason.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="3.36–40" /> Advaita Vedānta, especially Śaṅkara, traces the root to a beginningless superimposition of body and mind onto the Self.<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra" locator="I.1.1, intro., pp. 3–4" /> For Rāmānuja the soul's innate knowledge is "contracted" by karma, and the soul stays answerable for its own choices.<Cite source="thibaut-ramanuja-sribhashya" locator="I.1.1, p. 63; II.3.41, p. 557" /> For both, greatness and wickedness are two states of one self: its native light, and that light obscured. Madhva's Dvaita differs, spreading them partly across innately different souls. Their remedies range from liberating knowledge (Advaita) to devotion and surrender to God (Rāmānuja, Madhva).

## What this explains well

The Gītā names the precise puzzle of doing what one knows is wrong and gives it a psychology. Desire takes up residence even in reason, so knowing better is not enough. Śaṅkara adds that desire is the enemy of the wise, who see in advance where it leads and are "ever miserable."<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="3.39, comm." /> The guṇa analysis explains why civilization and domination share a root: rajas drives productive work like "husbandry" and also insatiable craving.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="14.7–10, comm." /> Habit and escalation are built in, since dwelling on objects leads by stages to wrath, delusion and ruin.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="2.62–63" /> And because the self's worth lies in its nature rather than its record, high dignity and severe diagnosis stand together. Even one "of flagitiously wicked ways" who turns to God "speedily becometh ... a holy soul."<Cite source="govindacharya-gita-ramanuja" locator="9.30–31" />

## Deep dive

#### The shared text: Gītā 3, 14 and 16

The Bhagavad Gītā is common ground for the Vedānta schools. Arjuna's question at 3.36 concerns someone who does not want to do wrong yet is "dragged on." Desire, which turns into wrath when blocked, is "all-devouring, all-sinful." It covers wisdom "as fire is surrounded by smoke, as a mirror by rust," and its seats are "the senses, mind, and reason." So reason is no neutral judge standing outside desire. The remedy is to restrain the senses and, knowing the Self "superior to reason," to subdue "the self by the self."<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="3.36–43" /> Chapter 14 sets this in a cosmology of three guṇas. Sattva brings lucidity and happiness, rajas brings "thirst and attachment," and tamas brings heedlessness that "covers the judgment."<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="14.6–9, comm." /> Chapter 16 sets a "divine endowment" (harmlessness, truth, compassion to creatures, forgiveness) beside a "demonic" one, whose world is "without a Lord ... brought about by lust." It names "lust, wrath, and greed" as the "gate to hell."<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="16.1–8, 16.21" /> The Gītā also insists on agency: "he alone is the friend of himself, he alone is the enemy of himself."<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="6.5–6" />

#### Advaita: superimposition and the anxious self

Śaṅkara defines superimposition (adhyāsa) as "a natural procedure" with "its cause in wrong knowledge," by which we merge Self and not-Self and say "That am I," "That is mine."<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra" locator="I.1.1, intro., pp. 3–4" /> Men, "although distinguished by superior intelligence," proceed like animals in their ordinary ways of knowing and acting. This error is "the cause of all evil" (Sanskrit *anartha*: harm or calamity).<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra" locator="I.1.1, intro., pp. 7–9" /> It breeds fear and lack, so people seek wholeness in wealth, status and pleasure, which "are perpetually bound to fail."<Cite source="sep-shankara" locator="§3.4" /> At the conventional level, karma, agency and moral discipline are fully real. Śaṅkara explains why beings' lots are unequal by their own merit and demerit over a beginningless saṃsāra.<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra" locator="II.1.34–36, pp. 357–360" /> Ultimately, though, only nondual Brahman is real. Śaṅkara therefore reads Gītā 15.7's "ray of Myself" as "only an imaginary portion."<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="15.7, comm., pp. 361–362" /> Virtue and selfless action purify the mind, but liberation comes by knowledge alone, and it dissolves the sense of individual agency.<Cite source="sep-shankara" locator="§§4.1, 4.3" />

#### Viśiṣṭādvaita: contracted knowledge, permitted freedom, surrender

For Rāmānuja the soul is an eternal knower whose knowledge is "in itself unlimited" but "capable of contraction." In embodied life it is contracted "owing to the influence of work (karman)."<Cite source="thibaut-ramanuja-sribhashya" locator="I.1.1, p. 63" /> God, the inner Ruler, acts only by granting "permission (anumati)" to the soul's own "volitional effort," so the fruit "properly belongs to him only." Those resolved on what displeases God are given "a delight in such actions" as their punishment, which explains why wickedness escalates.<Cite source="thibaut-ramanuja-sribhashya" locator="II.3.40–41, pp. 556–558" /> Lust, he says, is "sharpened ... by past habits" and, when blocked, turns into wrath and violence.<Cite source="govindacharya-gita-ramanuja" locator="3.37" /> On Gītā 18.66 he allows that accumulated sins may be too many to expiate. God then offers himself as "the Most Merciful ... demanding no price whatever for admission."<Cite source="govindacharya-gita-ramanuja" locator="18.66, pp. 564–565" /> Scholars debate how far the later doctrine of surrender (prapatti) is Rāmānuja's own.<Cite source="iep-ramanuja" locator="§5" />

#### Dvaita: dependent agents and graded souls

Madhva holds that God alone is independent; souls are real, eternal and dependent.<Cite source="iep-madhva" locator="introduction" /> The soul is a genuine agent, like a carpenter under a master. But its "capability of action is derived ... from the perfect Lord," who impels it "according to ... his previous actions and his effort (or aptitude)."<Cite source="subbarau-madhva-brahmasutra" locator="II.3.40–42, pp. 145–146" /> According to Madhva's tradition, souls form a hierarchy of innate virtues and faults, and karma is "the mechanism by which a given soul is propelled" toward its destiny.<Cite source="iep-madhva" locator="§3" /> Madhva's commentary cites a Purāṇic vision of three classes of beings, including some "for ever immersed in absolute misery." This doctrine is contested and sensitive.<Cite source="subbarau-madhva-brahmasutra" locator="III.1.21, p. 178" /> Liberation depends on Viṣṇu's grace.<Cite source="iep-madhva" locator="§3" />

#### Dignity across the schools

For Advaita the dignity is the Self itself, the same "in a dog and in a dog-eater" as in a learned Brahmin.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="5.18" /> For Rāmānuja, souls are "fundamentally alike and ... equal" as knowers, and "wholly subservient" to God.<Cite source="iep-ramanuja" locator="§5" /> For Madhva, dignity is real but dependent and graded. Many texts call human birth precious: Śaṅkara calls it the means to life's aims and "very hard to attain."<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="9.33, comm." /> The classical record also ranks persons by caste. Śaṅkara argues that śūdras are not qualified for Vedic knowledge of Brahman, though he grants that knowledge they gain otherwise still bears fruit.<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra" locator="I.3.34–38, pp. 223–229" /> The Gītā promises the supreme goal to women, vaiśyas and śūdras who take refuge. In the same verse, the phrase "sinful birth" is read by translators either as describing these groups or as naming a further class.<Cite source="govindacharya-gita-ramanuja" locator="9.32" />

#### Yoga, Nyāya and Mīmāṃsā

Patañjali's Yoga Sūtra names five afflictions (kleśas): ignorance, the feeling of personality, passion, aversion and the will to live. Ignorance is the soil of the others, and the will to live "exists ... even in the wise."<Cite source="woods-yoga-sutra" locator="YS 2.3–9" /> Nyāya's world is a chain running from misapprehension to faults, activity, birth and pain. Of the faults, delusion is "the worst," because without it affection and aversion do not arise.<Cite source="vidyabhusana-nyaya-sutras" locator="NS 1.1.2; 4.1.6" /> Mīmāṃsā, through Kumārila, explains why moral knowledge needs revelation. Dharma has "Injunction for its sole authority," because perception and inference cannot reach a good that does not yet exist.<Cite source="jha-slokavarttika" locator="Codanā-sūtra 1, 16–18" /> And one enduring self must be both doer and enjoyer: "it is always the doer that enjoys the result of the action."<Cite source="jha-slokavarttika" locator="Ātmavāda 29" />
