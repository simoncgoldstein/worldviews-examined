## The Christian view

Reformed theology explains the doubleness of human beings by one history of one nature: created good, fallen, preserved, and restored in Christ. God made humanity in his image, "endued with knowledge, righteousness, and true holiness," with his law written on the heart.<Cite source="wcf" locator="4.2" /> By Adam's sin the race fell from original righteousness and communion with God and became "wholly defiled in all the parts and faculties of soul and body."<Cite source="wcf" locator="6.2" /> Yet the faculties were not destroyed. Calvin, following Augustine, holds that the natural gifts were corrupted while the supernatural gifts were withdrawn: reason and will remain, damaged and misdirected.<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="II.2.12" /> So fallen people still discover truths, practise arts and frame just laws, which Calvin traces to God's Spirit as "the only fountain of truth,"<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="II.2.15" /> while God's providence restrains corruption from breaking out to its full extent.<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="II.3.3" /> Wickedness is not a second nature beside the good one; it is good powers ruled by a love of self "even to the contempt of God."<Cite source="augustine-city-of-god" locator="XIV.28" /> Because the disorder lies in what the will loves, the remedy must reach the will itself: renewal "after the image of its creator."<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Colossians 3:10" />

## What this explains well

The account affirms both halves of the phenomenon at full strength. Human dignity is real and permanent, and wrongdoing can be knowing and willing as well as mistaken: Calvin notes that some see the better course and follow the worse.<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="II.2.23" /> It explains why the worst evils feed on the best capacities. Augustine observes that the same inventiveness that produced building, music and astronomy also devised poisons and "engines of destruction."<Cite source="augustine-city-of-god" locator="XXII.24" /> It accounts for universality together with variation, because a corruption common to all is restrained and gifted unequally.<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="II.3.4" /> And because dignity rests on God's image rather than on performance, it survives the worst conduct: after the Flood, the prohibition of murder is grounded in the image.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Genesis 9:6" />

## Strongest objection

The objection does not deny the darkness; it denies that the Reformed mechanism explains it. Kant grants a propensity to evil that is universal and "radical" because "it corrupts the ground of all maxims."<Cite source="kant-religion-greene-hudson" locator="Bk. I, §III" /> Yet guilt, he argues, must be self-incurred: a person "must make or have made himself" whatever he is morally, "for otherwise he could not be held responsible for it."<Cite source="kant-religion-greene-hudson" locator="Bk. I, General Observation" /> John Taylor pressed this against federal headship. That another should so represent me "that when he is guilty I am to be reputed guilty," and "all this before I am born," is "unreasonable."<Cite source="taylor-original-sin-supplement" locator="pp. 108–109" /> The result is a dilemma. If inherited corruption leaves us unable to do good, the cruelty it explains is misfortune, not guilt; one who sins from a principle "never in his Power to command" would "be no Sinner at all."<Cite source="taylor-original-sin" locator="Part III, p. 222" /> If we are truly guilty, the guilt must be our own, and Adam's imputed guilt does no work.

The greatness side presses the same way: "the injunction that we ought to become better men resounds unabatedly in our souls; hence this must be within our power."<Cite source="kant-religion-greene-hudson" locator="Bk. I, General Observation" /> Unbelievers reason well, love sacrificially and die bravely. George Turnbull set against history's crimes "the exceeding generous and brave actions with which history shines."<Cite source="edwards-works-hickman-1" locator="Original Sin I.1.1" /> So either the capacity for good is intact and "inability" is false, or, once it is qualified by common grace and a distinction between civil and spiritual good, it no longer predicts anything we observe. Few philosophers now defend original guilt.<Cite source="sep-sin-christian" locator="§4.3" /> A mixed creature with a self-incurred bent to evil seems to explain the same facts more simply.

## Christian reply

The reply distinguishes two kinds of inability. Edwards grants that natural inability, a lack of faculties or opportunity, excuses, and for it "ought implies can" holds. Moral inability "consists in the opposition or want of inclination": nothing is lacking "but a will."<Cite source="edwards-works-hickman-1" locator="Freedom of the Will I.4" /> Commands address the will itself, so a will that cannot love God because it does not love him is not excused by that fact.<Cite source="edwards-works-hickman-1" locator="Freedom of the Will III.4" /> Against Taylor, Edwards argues that if sin must always flow from a prior sinful choice, the first such choice could not itself be sin.<Cite source="edwards-works-hickman-1" locator="Original Sin IV.1" /> Culpability belongs to what the will is, not only to how it began. What remains is a disputed ultimate premise that neither side demonstrates: whether responsibility requires self-origination.

On imputation, Edwards holds that the inborn corrupt disposition is a real consent of heart to the first sin, with guilt following it "as it was in the case of Adam himself."<Cite source="edwards-works-hickman-1" locator="Original Sin IV.3" /> Not all Reformed theologians accept his account of identity, and they differ on whether guilt is imputed immediately or through inherited corruption. The main argument is from Scripture: Paul sets one man's disobedience beside another's obedience.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 5:18–19" /> The Reformed draw the corollary that an objection to representation in principle would also count against the imputation of Christ's righteousness. That case is argued from revelation, not demonstrated from neutral premises.

The empirical strand targets a narrower claim than it supposes. The confessed inability concerns "spiritual good accompanying salvation,"<Cite source="wcf" locator="9.3" /> and the Confession allows that unbelievers' works may be "of good use both to themselves and others."<Cite source="wcf" locator="16.7" /> The doctrine expects such goods. The image remains, the faculties are corrupted rather than annihilated,<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="II.2.12" /> and God's providence restrains sin and distributes "special gifts" to the unregenerate.<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="II.3.4" /> What it predicts is natural virtue, such as pity and gratitude, that falls short of love to God.<Cite source="edwards-works-hickman-1" locator="True Virtue ch. 7" /> Its claim concerns the will's settled direction, not the frequency of crimes.<Cite source="edwards-works-hickman-1" locator="Original Sin I.1.1" /> How far that prediction can be tested remains disputed.

## Deep dive

#### Scope

This answer represents confessional Reformed theology as stated in the Westminster Standards and expounded by Calvin, with Augustine as the patristic source Calvin explicitly follows. Federal headship, set out below, is the Confession's position.

#### A ruined image, not a replaced one

Calvin defines the image by what Christ restores: knowledge, righteousness and holiness. After the fall, he holds, it was "not utterly effaced and destroyed" but survives as a ruin, partly renewed in believers and complete only in heaven.<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="I.15.4" /> The corruption is real but not substantial. Man is "corrupted by a natural viciousness, but not by one which proceeded from nature."<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="II.1.11" /> Augustine put the same point metaphysically. Vices are privations of natural good,<Cite source="augustine-enchiridion" locator="11" /> and since evil can exist only in something good, "an evil man" is "an evil good."<Cite source="augustine-enchiridion" locator="13" />

The Confession states the corruption without softening: "From this original corruption, whereby we are utterly indisposed, disabled, and made opposite to all good, and wholly inclined to all evil, do proceed all actual transgressions."<Cite source="wcf" locator="6.4" /> The Standards then gloss that sentence themselves. The Larger Catechism specifies opposition "unto all that is spiritually good,"<Cite source="westminster-larger-catechism" locator="Q. 25" /> and the Confession allows that works of unregenerate people may be things God commands and "of good use both to themselves and others." Yet it judges such works sinful, because they do not proceed from faith or aim at God's glory.<Cite source="wcf" locator="16.7" /> The doctrine is therefore total in extent, reaching every faculty and every act in its relation to God. It does not claim that every act is as bad as it could be.

#### The same gifts as instruments of sin

People "cannot but know" that their gifts are proofs of God, Calvin writes, "and yet they inwardly suppress them," some even using "the seed of Deity" to deny God.<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="I.5.4" /> This follows Paul's account of those who "knew God" yet "suppress the truth."<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 1:18–21" /> Augustine sets two catalogues side by side. One lists the crimes that spring "from that root of error and misplaced love which is born with every son of Adam."<Cite source="augustine-city-of-god" locator="XXII.22" /> The other lists the arts, goods God did not withdraw from a condemned nature, "else it had been annihilated."<Cite source="augustine-city-of-god" locator="XXII.24" /> At root, sin is pride: the soul "abandons Him to whom it ought to cleave as its end, and becomes a kind of end to itself."<Cite source="augustine-city-of-god" locator="XIV.13" />

#### Knowing the better and doing the worse

For Calvin the law written on the heart is "the judgment of conscience," and he rejects Plato's view that wrongdoing comes only from ignorance.<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="II.2.22" /> Often the general rule is held while the particular case is misjudged. But sometimes the sinner "rushes into sin knowingly and willingly."<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="II.2.23" /> Augustine stole pears he did not want: "It was foul, and I loved it."<Cite source="augustine-confessions" locator="II.4.9" /> Later he found that "the mind commands itself, and is resisted," because "it wills not entirely."<Cite source="augustine-confessions" locator="VIII.9.21" /> Edwards adds that "reason may resist present acts of the Will, and its resistance be insufficient."<Cite source="edwards-works-hickman-1" locator="Freedom of the Will I.4" />

Romans 7:15–25 needs care here. Calvin reads it of the regenerate and says its conflict "does not exist in man before he is renewed."<Cite source="calvin-commentary-romans" locator="Romans 7:15" /> In Reformed use it describes the believer's war with remaining corruption.<Cite source="wcf" locator="13.2" /> Many modern interpreters read it differently.

#### Restraint and civil virtue

Calvin admires unbelievers' law, philosophy, medicine and mathematics: "in despising the gifts, we insult the Giver."<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="II.2.15" /> The Spirit dwells as sanctifier only in believers, yet "fills, moves, and invigorates all things."<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="II.2.16" /> God checks corruption through shame, fear of the laws and self-interest, "yet without rendering it inwardly pure."<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="II.3.3" /> Bavinck, expounding Calvin, calls this the common grace by which God "immediately after the Fall interposed" to curb sin,<Cite source="bavinck-calvin-common-grace" locator="p. 118" /> with the Spirit working in unbelievers "as a spirit of life, of wisdom and of power."<Cite source="bavinck-calvin-common-grace" locator="p. 119" />

#### Adam, freedom and remedy

The Confession explains universality by Adam's place as "the root of all mankind," whose guilt was imputed and whose corrupted nature was conveyed to his descendants.<Cite source="wcf" locator="6.3" /> Witsius reads "all sinned" in Romans 5:12 as Adam's covenantal act counted to his posterity, the counterpart of Christ's obedience counted to believers.<Cite source="witsius-economy-covenants" locator="I.8.31" /> This is the confessional position. The historicity of Adam is debated among contemporary Reformed scholars.

Inability does not remove agency. The will is "neither forced, nor, by any absolute necessity of nature, determined,"<Cite source="wcf" locator="9.1" /> and fallen man sins "not forced or unwilling, but voluntarily."<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="II.3.5" /> Renewal is God's work, by which people "come most freely, being made willing by his grace."<Cite source="wcf" locator="10.1" /> Dignity follows the same logic. Calvin bids readers attend "to the image of God, which exists in all, and to which we owe all honour and love."<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="III.7.6" />

#### Edwards against Taylor

Edwards wrote *Original Sin* as a reply to Taylor. He argues first from fact. Every descendant of Adam comes into the world with no real prospect but sinning. Either God treats the race as one with its first father, or he subjects people he regards as wholly innocent to the same certain ruin, which "will not mend the matter."<Cite source="edwards-works-hickman-1" locator="Original Sin IV.3" /> Views that keep inherited corruption while dropping inherited guilt, on this argument, still face the question of justice.

He also reads moral history as evidence of a tendency. A disposition is known "by observing what is constant or general in event," especially when the effect persists against opposition. The tendency of a nature is what it would produce "were it not that the free mercy and kindness of God interposes."<Cite source="edwards-works-hickman-1" locator="Original Sin I.1.1" /> His own explanation of how Adam and his descendants are one, that all created identity depends on God's constitution, is distinctive and debated. The confessional doctrine does not depend on it.

#### Where Reformed theologians differ

In 1924 the Christian Reformed synod declared that God, "without renewing the heart," restrains sin and enables the unregenerate to do civil good, and it judged that expressions of H. Danhof and H. Hoeksema did not harmonize with these points.<Cite source="crc-acts-synod-1924" locator="Art. 132, pp. 145–147" /> Seventeenth-century Reformed theologians also debated whether Adam's guilt is imputed immediately or only through inherited corruption. These debates affect how universality and restraint are grounded. None of them removes the structure of image, fall, restraint and renewal.
