# Steelman packet: Rabbinic Judaism (`judaism`), question `great-and-terrible`

*Why is man so great and so terrible?*

Researcher lane: steelman only. This packet contains no Christian critique and does not use Christian
literature as evidence of Jewish teaching. All primary passages listed as "read" were opened on
2026-10-05, mostly through the Sefaria API (`https://www.sefaria.org/api/v3/texts/<ref>`), which serves
the named digitized editions. Soloveitchik's essay was read from the scanned original issue of
*Tradition* on traditiononline.org.

---

## 1. Scope

**Represented strands, and why.**

1. **Classical rabbinic literature** (Mishnah, Babylonian and Jerusalem Talmud, Midrash Rabbah). This
   is the foundation of the lane, as `docs/SOURCES.md` requires. It supplies the core vocabulary for
   this question: *tzelem Elohim* (the divine image), the two *yetzarim* (inclinations), *beḥirah*
   (choice), Torah as "antidote", and *teshuvah* (repentance).
2. **Medieval rationalist philosophy.** Saadia gives the first systematic statement of human
   superiority and free choice. Maimonides gives the decisive halakhic formulation of free will and
   repentance in *Hilkhot Teshuvah*, and the intellect-centred anthropology of the *Guide* and the
   *Eight Chapters*.
3. **Medieval exegetical-mystical thought (Nachmanides).** His reading of Gen 2:9 makes the origin of
   human *desire and will* the hinge of the Eden story. His reading of Deut 30:6 makes the
   messianic "circumcision of the heart" its undoing. This is a distinct and important alternative to
   the Maimonidean reading.
4. **Modern Orthodox thought (Soloveitchik).** *The Lonely Man of Faith* (1965) reads the two
   creation accounts as a dialectic in human nature: majestic, creative "Adam I" and covenantal
   "Adam II". It makes dignity a function of responsible mastery.
5. **Liberal streams, briefly.** Reform (CCAR 1999) and Conservative Judaism keep the image-of-God and
   repentance framework. They differ mainly on whether halakhic observance is the binding means of
   formation. That is material to §8 (remedy), but less so to the diagnosis itself.

**Deliberately under-weighted.** Lurianic Kabbalah and Ḥasidism (the *nefesh ha-bahamit* / *nefesh
elokit* of *Tanya*, sparks and shells) and the Musar movement. All three are relevant and could
deepen a later revision, but I did not verify their texts for this packet. Halevi is used lightly: his
anthropology of "degrees" and his particularist account of Israel's distinct rank are real but
contested (§13).

**Not identical with Old Testament religion.** The Tanakh texts are cited because the rabbinic
tradition reads them through its own interpretation. Every load-bearing interpretive claim below is
attached to a rabbinic or later Jewish source, not to the biblical verse alone.

## 2. Anthropology: what a human being fundamentally is

- **A creature made in the divine image.** In Gen 1:26–27 the image is tied to blessing and to rule
  over the earth. Gen 9:6 makes it the ground for the prohibition of murder. Mishnah Avot 3:14 makes
  the image an object of divine love: "Beloved is man for he was created in the image" (Kulp trans.).
  The Mishnah adds a further love in that this was *made known* to him.
- **A composite of "upper" and "lower".** Bereshit Rabbah 8:11 gives four traits from the higher
  realm (standing, speech, understanding, sight, "as do the ministering angels") and four from the
  lower (eating and drinking, procreating, excreting, dying "as does an animal"). God says He creates
  man from both realms: "If he sins he will die; if he does not sin he will live."
- **A being with two inclinations.** The double *yod* of *va-yitzer* (Gen 2:7) is read as the good and
  the evil *yetzer* (b. Berakhot 61a; Bereshit Rabbah 14:4). R. Shimon ben Pazi's gloss captures the
  human predicament: "woe unto me from my Creator [*yotzri*] and woe unto me from my inclination
  [*yitzri*]" (Berakhot 61a).
- **A free and responsible agent.** "Everything is foreseen yet freedom of choice is granted" (Avot
  3:15). Maimonides makes choice the defining human singularity: "Free will is granted to all men"
  (*Hilkhot Teshuvah* 5:1, Touger). He calls it "a pillar" of the Torah and the commandments (5:3).
- **Philosophical refinement.** For Maimonides the image (*tzelem*) is the human "specific form", the
  intellect, and not bodily shape (*Guide* I.1; *Yesodei ha-Torah* 4:8). For Saadia, human superiority
  lies in the wisdom God gave, which makes man "the axis (*qotev*) of the world" and therefore the one
  rightly commanded, warned, rewarded and punished (*Emunot ve-De'ot* IV.1, Ibn Tibbon Hebrew).
- **Unique individuals.** God stamped every human with the seal of Adam, "and not one of them is
  similar to another". So each person must say "the world was created for me" (m. Sanhedrin 4:5).

## 3. Metaphysical context

- **One good Creator, one created order.** There is no rival evil principle. Even the evil inclination
  is God's creation (b. Kiddushin 30b: "I created an evil inclination … and I created Torah as its
  antidote"). The Mishnah's teaching that Adam was created alone is directed partly against those who
  say "there are many authorities in Heaven" (m. Sanhedrin 4:5).
- **Providence without compulsion.** Maimonides holds that God *willed* that man have free choice,
  just as He willed fire to rise and water to fall (*Hilkhot Teshuvah* 5:4). Human freedom is
  therefore an expression of divine will, not an exception to it. Saadia argues that "the Creator has
  no governance in human acts and does not compel them to obedience or to rebellion". His case draws
  on sense experience, reason, Scripture and the rabbinic dictum "all is in the hands of Heaven except
  the fear of Heaven" (*Emunot ve-De'ot* IV.4, Hebrew; my paraphrase).
- **Covenant and Torah.** Torah is the "desirable instrument with which the world had been created",
  given to Israel (Avot 3:14). The commandments are an ordered way of life. In Saadia's account they
  include laws that reason itself requires: gratitude to the benefactor, and forbidding creatures to
  wrong one another (*Emunot ve-De'ot* III.1).
- **Judgment, repentance and the World to Come.** The world is judged with goodness "according to the
  preponderance of works" (Avot 3:15). Maimonides tells each person to regard himself and the world as
  evenly balanced, so that a single act tips both (*Hilkhot Teshuvah* 3:4). History moves toward a
  messianic age in which the evil inclination is "slaughtered" (b. Sukkah 52a), or in which desire for
  evil is no longer natural (Nachmanides on Deut 30:6).
- **Matter and form (philosophical strand).** For Maimonides "all corruption, destruction, or defect
  comes from matter". Man's "shortcomings and sins are all due to the substance of the body", while
  "all his merits are exclusively due to his form" (*Guide* III.8, Friedländer).

## 4. Why humans possess remarkable capacities

1. **Because God made them in His image and gave them dominion** (Gen 1:26–28; Ps 8:5–7). Rabbinic
   tradition understands the image as a real endowment that God loves (Avot 3:14).
2. **Because God gave wisdom.** Saadia lists what wisdom makes possible: remembering the past,
   foreseeing much of the future, domesticating animals, farming, drawing water from the depths and
   building self-drawing water wheels, fine houses, clothing and cuisine, commanding armies and
   ordering kingship "until human beings were set in order", and knowing the structure of the heavens.
   He insists this superiority "is not an error … nor arrogance, but clear truth" (*Emunot ve-De'ot*
   IV.1, Hebrew; my rendering).
3. **Because the intellect is the image.** Maimonides holds that intellectual perception is compared,
   "though only apparently", to divine perception, which needs no bodily organ (*Guide* I.1). This
   intellect is "from God, from heaven" and survives the body (*Yesodei ha-Torah* 4:8–9).
4. **Because the evil inclination is also generative.** This is the most distinctive rabbinic move.
   In Bereshit Rabbah 9:7, "very good" (Gen 1:31) includes the evil inclination. "Were it not for the
   evil inclination, a man would never build a house, would never marry a wife, would never beget
   children, and would never engage in commerce." Ambition, desire and rivalry (Eccl 4:4) drive
   civilization. In b. Yoma 69b the Men of the Great Assembly imprison the inclination, and no fresh
   egg can be found in the land. They conclude that killing it would destroy the world.
5. **Because humans are made for creative mastery.** Soloveitchik's Adam I is commanded to "fill the
   earth and subdue it". He seeks to understand "how the cosmos functions" in order to harness it.
   That pursuit, and the medicine, technology and civilization it yields, is a divine mandate and the
   way humanity realizes dignity (*Lonely Man of Faith*, Tradition 7:2, pp. 12–14). Adam II's
   capacity for wonder, covenant and relation to God is equally original (p. 17).
6. **Kindness and righteousness are real.** In Bereshit Rabbah 8:5, Kindness and Righteousness argue
   that man should be created *because* he performs acts of kindness and righteousness.

## 5. Why humans behave destructively or wrongly

**The rabbinic diagnosis is internal, cumulative and chosen. It is not fatalistic.**

- **The evil inclination is present from birth and renews itself daily.** It dominates "from the
  moment of emergence from the womb" (b. Sanhedrin 91b). It "renews itself … every day" and "seeks to
  kill him" (b. Kiddushin 30b; Sukkah 52b). Gen 8:21 ("the devisings of the human mind are evil from
  youth", JPS) is the proof-text, and Kiddushin 30b notes that "even its Creator calls it evil".
- **Habit turns it from guest to master.** At first the inclination is "like a strand of a spider's
  web" and at last "like the thick ropes of a wagon" (Sukkah 52a). Rava: it begins as "a traveler",
  becomes "a guest", and finally "the homeowner" (Sukkah 52b).
- **Sin is self-harm as well as harm to others.** Maimonides distinguishes evils that come from being
  embodied, evils people inflict on one another (including domination: "some of them use their
  strength against others"), and the "largest class", evils each person causes himself by his own
  action. Maimonides links the last to Eccl 7:29: God made man upright, "but they have thought out many
  inventions" (*Guide* III.12).
- **Disordered desire.** "Envy, lust and [the desire for] honor put a man out of the world" (Avot
  4:21). Saadia's account of disorder is that a person lets one love rule all the others. God
  implanted many loves, and a well-ordered life weighs and blends them as a builder combines stone,
  wood and nails (*Emunot ve-De'ot* X.1, Hebrew; my paraphrase). Maimonides: "eating, drinking, sexual
  intercourse, excessive lust, passion, and all vices" originate in the body's matter (*Guide* III.8).
- **Knowingly doing what one believes wrong.** The tradition names this directly. A prayer attributed to Rabbi
  Alexandri (some say Rav Hamnuna): "it is revealed and known before You that our will is to perform Your
  will, and what prevents us? … the yeast in the dough, the evil inclination that is within every
  person; and the subjugation to the kingdoms" (b. Berakhot 17a). The settled will can be good while
  a leavening impulse works against it. External oppression is named alongside the internal impulse.
  - Maimonides' *Eight Chapters* 6 analyses the *kovesh yitzro* (self-restrained person). He does
  good while "desiring and craving all the while" for wrong. Maimonides contrasts him with the
  *ḥasid*, whose desires are themselves ordered.
  - Hillel: "Do not trust in yourself until the day of your death" (Avot 2:4).
- **Greatness intensifies the struggle.** "Anyone who is greater than another, his evil inclination
  is greater than his" (Sukkah 52a).
- **Persistent wickedness can close the door.** Maimonides holds that great or many willful sins can
  bring the punishment that *teshuvah* is withheld. He reads Pharaoh's hardened heart this way: the
  hardening is a consequence of sin he "began … on his own initiative" (*Hilkhot Teshuvah* 6:3).
- **What the tradition does *not* say.** Guilt is personal: "Only the person who sins shall die. A
  child shall not share the burden of a parent's guilt" (Ezek 18:20, JPS). The Talmud concludes that
  "there is death without sin". It cites four who died "due to Adam's sin with the serpent … although
  they themselves were free of sin" (b. Shabbat 55b). So Adam's legacy is *mortality*, while guilt
  stays individual. The closest rabbinic analogue to an inherited taint is the aggadah of the serpent's
  *zuhama* (moral contamination). It "remained in all human beings" until it ceased for Israel at
  Sinai (b. Shabbat 146a). This view is disputed in the same passage and is not developed into a
  doctrine of inherited guilt (§13).

## 6. Do greatness and wickedness share a common explanation?

**Largely yes, in the classical rabbinic strand. Partly no, in Maimonides. Yes, in a different key,
in Nachmanides and Soloveitchik.**

- **Rabbinic (aggadic).** One created drive, the *yetzer*, underlies both the house, the marriage and
  the market (Bereshit Rabbah 9:7) and the destruction of both Temples (Sukkah 52a, Yoma 69b). The
  human task is not to extirpate it (Yoma 69b: that would end the world) but to rule it. "Who is
  mighty? He who subdues his [evil] inclination" (Avot 4:1). Torah is the antidote that lets one rule
  it (Kiddushin 30b, on Gen 4:7 "you can be its master"). The *yetzer ha-ra* is thus **morally
  ambivalent energy**, not evil substance. Greatness and terribleness are the same vitality, well or
  badly governed.
- **Maimonides (philosophical).** His explanation is a composite one. Merits flow from form (the
  intellect), vices from matter (*Guide* III.8). One designed unity, man as form-in-matter, explains
  both, but each has a different principle. In the *Eight Chapters* (6) he partly reconciles the
  rabbinic praise of struggle with the philosophers' praise of the untroubled virtuous. A noble soul
  has no desire for evils such as bloodshed, theft and ingratitude. The rabbinic dictum "I do want to,
  but my Father in Heaven has forbidden it" applies only to ritual prohibitions (*ḥukkim*).
- **Nachmanides (exegetical-mystical).** The capacity that makes humans godlike also makes them
  dangerous. Before eating from the tree, man "did whatever was proper for him to do naturally, just as
  the heavens". The fruit "gave rise to will and desire, that those who ate it should choose a thing or
  its opposite, for good or for evil". This power of choice "is a godlike attribute; but as far as man
  is concerned, it is bad because through it, he has a will and desire" (Ramban on Gen 2:9, Chavel).
  One endowment, will, is the root of both.
- **Soloveitchik (modern).** The majestic, creative drive of Adam I and the covenantal receptivity of
  Adam II are both God-willed. The Halakhah treats them as complementary, "not divisible into secular
  and hallowed sectors". The single world can "either plunge into ugliness and hatefulness" or "be
  roused to meaningful, redeeming activity" (*Lonely Man of Faith*, pp. 10, 51). The contrast is a
  creative tension in human nature, not a split between a good part and a sinful part.

## 7. Rationality; moral knowledge; conscience; evil and sin; agency; desire

| Topic | Rabbinic Judaism's account (by strand) |
|---|---|
| **Rationality** | A divine gift and the core of the image for the rationalists (*Guide* I.1; *Yesodei ha-Torah* 4:8). For Saadia, wisdom is the ground of human superiority (*Emunot* IV.1). In the aggadah, understanding (*da'at*) is an "upper" trait shared with angels (Bereshit Rabbah 8:11). |
| **Moral knowledge** | Saadia: reason itself requires many commandments (gratitude to God, no wronging of others), and revelation adds and specifies (*Emunot* III.1). Maimonides (*Guide* I.2): before the sin Adam knew true and false. Good and bad (*tov va-ra'*) are "apparent truths", what is proper and improper, which became man's preoccupation after he followed imagination and appetite. Moral knowledge is real, but it is a different register from demonstrative knowledge. This reading is contested (§13). |
| **Conscience** | There is no single technical term, but the *yetzer ha-tov* (good inclination), divine assistance, and Torah study play the role (Sukkah 52b: "if not for the Holy One … Who assists him … he would not overcome it"). The shofar as alarm: "Wake up you sleepy ones … Inspect your deeds" (*Hilkhot Teshuvah* 3:4). |
| **Evil / sin** | Sin (*ḥet*, *avon*, *pesha'*) is a free act against God's will and against others. It is cumulative and habit-forming (Sukkah 52a–b). Most human evils are self-inflicted (*Guide* III.12). Guilt is personal (Ezek 18:20); mortality is the Adamic inheritance (Shabbat 55b). |
| **Agency / free will** | It is foundational and strongly affirmed. "Each person is fit to be righteous like Moses … or wicked, like Jeroboam" (*Hilkhot Teshuvah* 5:2). Temperament gives predispositions, not necessity (*Eight Chapters* 8). God's foreknowledge is affirmed alongside freedom (Avot 3:15). Maimonides grounds the reconciliation in the identity of God and His knowledge, which is beyond human comprehension (*Hilkhot Teshuvah* 5:5, read). |
| **Desire / appetite** | Desire is created and necessary (Bereshit Rabbah 9:7; Yoma 69b). It goes wrong by excess, by monopoly (Saadia X.1), and by habit. It is ordered, not abolished. Halevi's pious man is a "prince" who gives each faculty "its share" and keeps it in its place (*Kuzari* III.5). For Nachmanides, desire as such entered with the tree and will be "circumcised" in the messianic age (on Deut 30:6). |

## 8. Can the human condition be remedied?

**Yes. The remedy is already available, and it is relational and covenantal.**

1. **Teshuvah (return/repentance).** Maimonides gives the definition: abandon the sin, regret the
   past, resolve before "He who knows the hidden", and confess verbally (*Hilkhot Teshuvah* 2:2).
   Complete *teshuvah* is to face the same situation with the same capacity and refrain (2:1; cf.
   b. Yoma 86b). It restores intimacy, not merely legal standing. The penitent "was separate from
   God … Now, he is clinging to the Shechinah" (7:7). He "is beloved and desirable before the Creator
   as if he never sinned" (7:4). Reish Lakish: repentance from love turns intentional sins into merits
   (Yoma 86b). Whether penitents stand higher than the wholly righteous is itself a talmudic dispute
   (R. Abbahu vs. R. Yoḥanan, Berakhot 34b). Maimonides rules with R. Abbahu (*Hilkhot Teshuvah* 7:4).
2. **Torah as antidote and formation.** "I created an evil inclination … and I created Torah as its
   antidote" (Kiddushin 30b). "Drag it to the study hall. … If it is like a stone, it will be
   dissolved" (Sukkah 52b). The commandments train desire: Halevi's ordered soul (*Kuzari* III.5),
   Maimonides' cultivation of the mean (*Eight Chapters*), and Avot 4:1's "mighty" one.
3. **Divine help.** Human effort is not left alone. "If not for the Holy One … Who assists him … he
   would not overcome it" (Sukkah 52b). Nachmanides on Deut 30:6 cites the rabbinic principle "If
   someone comes to purify himself, they assist him".
4. **The commandment is within reach.** "It is not in the heavens … the thing is very close to you,
   in your mouth and in your heart" (Deut 30:11–14). Maimonides adds that the choice to repent "is
   presently in our hand" (*Hilkhot Teshuvah* 5:2).
5. **Messianic consummation.** In the future God "will bring the evil inclination and slaughter it".
   The righteous weep that they overcame "so high a mountain", the wicked that they could not overcome
   "a mere strand of hair" (Sukkah 52a). For Nachmanides, "in the days of the Messiah, the choice of
   their [genuine] good will be natural". Man will "return … to what he was before the sin of Adam"
   (on Deut 30:6, with Jer 31 and Ezek 36).
6. **Limit case.** For one who sins persistently and willfully, *teshuvah* may be withheld as
   punishment (*Hilkhot Teshuvah* 6:3). The remedy is real but not mechanical.

**Liberal variation.** The Reform CCAR *Statement of Principles* (1999) speaks of "transgression and
repentance" and of mitzvot, but frames the latter as "those that address us as individuals and as a
community". Formation is thus a matter of personal discernment rather than binding halakhah.
Conservative Judaism holds halakhah binding but historically developing. The diagnosis and *teshuvah*
are common ground. The means of formation differ.

## 9. Dignity: inherent or derived?

**Both, in a specific sense.**

- **Inherent, conferred by creation.** It belongs to every human as such. "Beloved is man for he was
  created in the image" (Avot 3:14). Murder is forbidden "for in the image of God was humankind made"
  (Gen 9:6). Ben Azzai calls "This is the book of the descendants of Adam" (Gen 5:1) the central
  principle of the Torah. R. Tanḥuma warns that one who demeans another should "know who it is that you
  are disgracing" (Bereshit Rabbah 24:7; y. Nedarim 9:4). Every person is a world (m. Sanhedrin 4:5).
  CCAR 1999: "every human being is created in the image of God, and that therefore every human life is
  sacred."
- **Derived, not self-originating.** It is grounded in God's act and love, not in an autonomous human
  property.
- **Realized or achieved, in an additional sense.** Soloveitchik writes that "humanity = dignity =
  responsibility" and "there is no dignity without responsibility". The helpless, instinct-bound life
  is "undignified". Man "acquires dignity through glory, through his majestic posture vis à vis his
  environment" (*Lonely Man of Faith*, pp. 13–14). For Maimonides the image is an intellect to be
  actualized (*Guide* I.1–2). Halevi ranks humans by degrees of the divine influence (*Kuzari* I.31–43,
  I.95). These views add a dignity of achievement to the dignity of creation without cancelling the
  latter.
- **Covenantal layering.** Avot 3:14 moves from "beloved is man" to "beloved are Israel". The
  universal and the particular are distinct but coexisting grounds of worth. See §13 for the textual
  variant in m. Sanhedrin 4:5.

## 10. Internal school differences that materially change the answer

1. **Is the evil inclination original to created nature or a result of sin?**
   - *Rabbinic aggadah:* original. It is part of "very good" on the sixth day (Bereshit Rabbah 9:7)
     and created in the formation of man (Berakhot 61a).
   - *Nachmanides:* will and desire *arose* from eating of the tree. Before that, man acted
     "naturally" like the heavens (on Gen 2:9).
   - This changes whether human ambivalence is primordial design or a consequence of Eden.
2. **What did Eden change?**
   - *Maimonides* (*Guide* I.2): the loss of part of intellectual perfection, and a turn from
     true/false to the "apparent" register of proper/improper.
   - *Maimonides* (*Hilkhot Teshuvah* 5:1): Gen 3:22 states man's uniqueness in choosing good and
     evil.
   - *Nachmanides:* the emergence of desire-laden will.
   - *Aggadah:* mortality (Bereshit Rabbah 8:11; Shabbat 55b) and, in one view, *zuhama* (Shabbat
     146a).
3. **Where the image resides.** Maimonides places it in the intellect (and explicitly not in bodily
   shape). Rabbinic texts tie it to the whole person, body included in practice (Gen 9:6, murder; Ben
   Azzai and R. Tanḥuma). Soloveitchik places it in creative-majestic responsibility *and* in covenantal
   relation.
4. **The value of struggle.**
   - *Rabbis:* the one who desires and refrains is greater (Sukkah 52a; "according to the labor is the
     reward", Avot 5:23, cited by Maimonides but not independently read).
   - *Philosophers:* the one without wrong desire is greater.
   - *Maimonides:* the two apply to different classes of commandments (*Eight Chapters* 6).
5. **Universal vs. particular.** Halevi assigns Israel a distinct degree (*Kuzari* I.27, I.95), while
   Saadia and Maimonides make intellect and choice the human universal. Printed m. Sanhedrin 4:5 reads
   "a single soul from Israel", but the Mishnah text in the Jerusalem Talmud reads universally (§13).
6. **Modern streams.** Orthodoxy treats halakhah as the binding means of formation. Reform (CCAR 1999)
   speaks of mitzvot "that address us". The anthropology is largely shared; the remedy's form differs.

## 11. What this worldview genuinely explains well

1. **Why greatness and terribleness so often travel together.** Through the generative *yetzer* the
   rabbinic tradition explains why the drives that build houses, families, markets and cities are the
   same drives behind envy, lust and domination. It also explains why removing them would not leave a
   saint but a world without eggs (Bereshit Rabbah 9:7; Yoma 69b). It has no need to locate the good
   and the terrible in separate faculties or populations.
2. **Why greater people often have greater temptations** (Sukkah 52a). It treats this as something to
   expect, not as an embarrassment.
3. **Akrasia without denial.** It describes knowing wrong-doing in recognizable phenomenological
   terms: the good will impeded by the "yeast in the dough" (Berakhot 17a), the slide from spider-thread
   to wagon-rope (Sukkah 52a), the guest who becomes master (Sukkah 52b). It neither reduces sin to
   ignorance nor makes it inevitable.
4. **Dignity that survives failure.** Because dignity rests on creation in the image and on God's
   love (Avot 3:14), no degree of wickedness erases the worth of the victim *or* the possibility of the
   offender's return.
5. **A strong, livable account of moral change.** *Teshuvah* is precise (cessation, regret,
   resolution, confession, proof under the same temptation). It is relational (restored closeness to
   the Shechinah) and hopeful (the penitent "as if he never sinned"; sins become merits when one returns
   from love). It is woven into a calendar and a daily practice of study and law, which treat
   formation as a lifelong discipline rather than a single event.
6. **Responsibility without fatalism.** The insistence that each person could be "like Moses or like
   Jeroboam" grounds moral seriousness, education, law and hope (*Hilkhot Teshuvah* 5:2). So does the
   insistence that temperament inclines but does not compel (*Eight Chapters* 8).
7. **Civilization as vocation.** Soloveitchik's Adam I shows creativity, science and mastery as
   *commanded* expressions of the image, not as rebellion, while showing how the same majesty can turn
   to "ugliness and hatefulness" when cut off from covenant.

## 12. Strongest representative thinkers for this question

- **Maimonides.** The definitive halakhic statement of free will and *teshuvah* (*Hilkhot Teshuvah*
  2, 5–7), the intellect as image (*Guide* I.1–2), and a taxonomy of human evils, mostly self-inflicted
  (*Guide* III.12).
- **Nachmanides.** The most penetrating medieval reading of Eden as the birth of desire-laden will,
  and its messianic reversal (on Gen 2:9, Deut 30:6). He offers a genuine alternative to Maimonides.
- **Saadia Gaon.** The first systematic case that human superiority (wisdom, civilization) and free
  choice belong together and ground commandment. He also has an account of disordered desire as one
  love monopolising the rest (*Emunot* IV.1, IV.4, X.1).
- **Joseph B. Soloveitchik.** The modern Orthodox dialectic of majestic and covenantal humanity. It
  directly addresses creativity, technology and dignity as responsibility (*Lonely Man of Faith*).
- **Judah Halevi** (optional, secondary). The ordered soul as a just prince over the faculties
  (*Kuzari* III.5) and the hierarchy of degrees. Use only lightly, given the contested particularism.

## 13. Uncertainties and contested interpretations

1. **m. Sanhedrin 4:5, "from Israel."** The printed text (Torat Emet Hebrew; Koren and Kulp English
   on Sefaria) reads "a single soul *from Israel*". The Mishnah as presented in the Jerusalem Talmud
   (Sanhedrin 4:9, Guggenheimer ed.) reads universally: "anybody who destroys a single life". Many
   scholars regard the universal reading as earlier. I verified only the two readings, not the
   manuscript evidence (e.g. MS Kaufmann). The draft therefore cites the Jerusalem Talmud locator for
   the universal wording and should not claim which reading is original.
2. **Shabbat 146a *zuhama*.** This is the nearest rabbinic analogue to an inherited moral taint, but
   it is aggadic and disputed in the same passage (R. Abba bar Kahana). It concerns contamination, not
   guilt. Do not overstate it either way.
3. **Maimonides, *Guide* I.2.** Whether Maimonides holds that morality is *merely* conventional, or
   only that moral judgments are not demonstrative, is a long-standing scholarly debate. The draft
   uses the cautious wording "a different register of knowledge".
4. **Maimonides' two readings of Gen 3:22** (*Hilkhot Teshuvah* 5:1 vs. *Guide* I.2) need not
   conflict. The code and the *Guide* address different audiences. But they should not be blended
   into one claim.
5. **Nachmanides' Messianic age.** Chavel's annotation, citing R. Yeruḥam, holds that choice
   remains in principle. Nachmanides' own words ("neither merit nor guilt") are stronger. The draft
   quotes Nachmanides' own text only.
6. **Saadia locators.** Saadia was verified in the Hebrew of Judah ibn Tibbon (Leipzig 1864, on
   Sefaria), using its treatise/chapter divisions. Rosenblatt's English (Yale, 1948) was not
   accessible. Its chapter numbering for Treatise IV (exordium vs. ch. 1) may differ, so a reviewer
   should check before switching editions. My English renderings of Saadia are paraphrases. Note that
   SEP's Saadya entry attributes the Hebrew translation to Samuel ibn Tibbon; the standard attribution
   is Judah ibn Tibbon.
7. **Avot numbering.** Kulp/Sefaria number "Beloved is man" 3:14 and "all is foreseen" 3:15. Some
   prayer-book editions number these 3:18 and 3:19.
8. **Halevi's particularism** (*Kuzari* I.27, I.95) is contested within Jewish thought. Use it only to
   illustrate school difference, not as the lane's default anthropology.
9. **Kabbalistic and Ḥasidic anthropology** (two souls, *Tanya*) is not represented. A reviewer may
   judge that a short note is needed.
10. **Soloveitchik on repentance** (*Al ha-Teshuvah* / *On Repentance*) and *Halakhic Man*
    (self-creation through repentance) were **not** read. They are not cited.

## 16. Thinker profile notes

### Saadia Gaon (`saadia`)
- **Dates and setting.** 882–942. Born in the Fayyum, Egypt; died in Baghdad. Gaon of the Sura academy
  from 928.
- **School.** Geonic rabbinic leadership; medieval Jewish rationalism, with affinities to Mu'tazilite
  kalam (SEP).
- **Why relevant.** He gives the first systematic Jewish argument that human superiority lies in the
  wisdom God gave and that this superiority is why humans are commanded, judged and free.
- **Key ideas.** Reason and revelation as joint sources of knowledge. Rational vs. revelational
  commandments. Man as the "axis" of the created world. Free choice without divine compulsion. The
  well-ordered life as a balance of many loves (Treatise X).
- **Representative works.** *Kitab al-Amanat wa-l-I'tiqadat* / *Sefer ha-Emunot ve-ha-De'ot*; Arabic
  Bible translation (*Tafsir*); *Siddur*.
- **References.** https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/saadya/ ; https://www.sefaria.org/topics/saadia-gaon

### Judah Halevi (`halevi`)
- **Dates and setting.** c. 1075–1141. Born in Toledo or Tudela, al-Andalus. Left for Egypt in 1140
  and sailed for the Land of Israel in 1141, where he probably died soon after.
- **School.** Medieval Jewish philosophy, critical of Aristotelian rationalism; Hebrew poet.
- **Why relevant.** His hierarchy of degrees (natural, animal, rational, divine) and his picture of
  the pious person as a just ruler over the faculties.
- **Key ideas.** The *amr ilahi* (divine influence). Experience and tradition over speculation. Degrees
  of being. The election of Israel and the Land. Ordered rather than suppressed desire.
- **Representative works.** *Kitab al-Ḥujja wa-l-Dalil fi Nuṣr al-Din al-Dhalil* (*The Kuzari*);
  poems of Zion.
- **Reference.** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judah_Halevi

### Moses Maimonides (`maimonides`)
- **Dates and setting.** 1138–1204 (SEP; Sefaria gives 1137; some give 1135). Born in Córdoba. His
  family fled the Almohads; via Morocco he settled in Fustat, Egypt, as physician and communal leader.
- **School.** Medieval Jewish Aristotelian rationalism; halakhic codification.
- **Why relevant.** He gives the authoritative statement of free will and repentance, the intellect
  as the divine image, the matter/form account of vice and virtue, and a taxonomy of human evils.
- **Key ideas.** Free choice as a pillar of Torah. *Teshuvah* as cessation, regret, resolve and
  confession. *Tzelem* as intellect. Most evils self-inflicted. The ethical mean and the training of
  dispositions.
- **Representative works.** *Mishneh Torah* (esp. *Sefer ha-Madda'*: *Yesodei ha-Torah*, *De'ot*,
  *Teshuvah*); *Guide of the Perplexed*; *Commentary on the Mishnah*, including *Shemonah Perakim*.
- **References.** https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/maimonides/ ; https://www.sefaria.org/topics/rambam

### Nachmanides (`nachmanides`)
- **Dates and setting.** 1194–c. 1270. Born in Girona, Catalonia; died in Acre (Akko). Defended
  Judaism at the Barcelona Disputation (1263).
- **School.** Talmudist and Torah commentator integrating Kabbalah; often critical of Maimonidean
  rationalism.
- **Why relevant.** His reading of the tree of knowledge as the origin of will and desire, and of
  the messianic "circumcision of the heart" as its healing. This is the strongest medieval
  alternative to Maimonides on Eden.
- **Key ideas.** Original human nature acting "naturally" like the heavens. Choice as godlike yet
  burdensome for man. Divine assistance to one who seeks purity. Messianic restoration of natural
  goodness. Kabbalistic layers in the plain sense of Torah.
- **Representative works.** *Commentary on the Torah*; *Torat ha-Adam*; *Sefer ha-Vikkuaḥ*; Talmudic
  novellae.
- **Reference.** https://www.sefaria.org/topics/ramban

### Joseph B. Soloveitchik (`soloveitchik`)
- **Dates and setting.** 27 Feb 1903 (Pruzhany) – 9 Apr 1993 (Boston). Berlin PhD on Hermann Cohen
  (1932). Founded the Maimonides School in Boston. Rosh yeshiva at RIETS/Yeshiva University
  1941–1986.
- **School.** Modern Orthodox Judaism; Brisker Talmudic method joined with neo-Kantian and
  existentialist philosophy.
- **Why relevant.** His dialectic of majestic and covenantal humanity speaks directly to creativity,
  technology, civilization and dignity. He grounds dignity in responsibility.
- **Key ideas.** Adam I / Adam II. Dignity = responsibility. Majesty and covenant as complementary
  in Halakhah. Halakhic man as creator. Repentance as self-creation (not verified here).
- **Representative works.** *Halakhic Man* (Heb. 1944; Eng. 1983); *The Lonely Man of Faith*
  (*Tradition* 1965; Doubleday 1992); *Kol Dodi Dofek* (1956); *On Repentance* (ed. Peli, 1979).
- **References.** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_B._Soloveitchik ;
  https://traditiononline.org/the-lonely-man-of-faith/

## The view

In classical rabbinic Judaism, the human being is a single creature made from two realms. Every person
is made in God's image and loved for it.<Cite source="mishnah-kulp" locator="Avot 3:14" /> Each one
is unique, so that one life is "an entire world".<Cite source="yerushalmi-guggenheimer" locator="Sanhedrin 4:9" />
Humans stand, speak and understand like angels, yet eat, procreate and die like animals.<Cite source="midrash-rabbah-sefaria" locator="Bereshit Rabbah 8:11" />
God formed them with two inclinations, a good *yetzer* and an evil one.<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Berakhot 61a" />
The evil inclination is not foreign to creation. The midrash counts it within the "very good" of the
sixth day, because without it no one would build a house, marry, have children or trade.<Cite source="midrash-rabbah-sefaria" locator="Bereshit Rabbah 9:7" />
Greatness and wickedness therefore spring largely from the same created energy, governed well or
badly. Humans are free: "everything is foreseen yet freedom of choice is granted".<Cite source="mishnah-kulp" locator="Avot 3:15" />
Each person could become like Moses or like Jeroboam.<Cite source="maimonides-mishneh-torah-touger" locator="Teshuvah 5:2" />
Wrongdoing grows by habit, from a spider's thread into wagon ropes.<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Sukkah 52a" />
Guilt remains personal. God gave Torah as the "antidote" to the evil inclination<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Kiddushin 30b" />
and opened the way of *teshuvah*, return, by which the penitent is again "beloved and desirable"
before God.<Cite source="maimonides-mishneh-torah-touger" locator="Teshuvah 7:4" />

## What this explains well

This account explains why the drives that build homes, families, markets and cities are the same
drives behind envy, lust and domination. It also explains why removing them would not leave a saint
but a barren world. In one Talmudic story, imprisoning the inclination leaves not a single fresh egg in
the land.<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Yoma 69b" />
It expects that "anyone who is greater than another, his evil inclination is greater".<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Sukkah 52a" />
It describes knowing wrongdoing precisely. "Our will is to perform Your will", but the "yeast in the
dough" resists.<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Berakhot 17a" />
It holds together dignity that no crime erases, responsibility without fatalism, and a concrete,
hopeful practice of moral change.

## Deep dive

#### The yetzer: one energy, two outcomes

The rabbinic tradition's most characteristic contribution to this question is its account of the
*yetzer ha-ra*, the "evil inclination". Scripture says the devisings of the human heart are "evil from
youth".<Cite source="tanakh-jps-gender-sensitive" locator="Genesis 8:21" />
The Talmud adds that the inclination dominates from birth<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Sanhedrin 91b" />
and renews itself daily.<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Kiddushin 30b" />
Yet the midrash refuses to treat it as simply bad. It is part of creation's "very good", the source of
building, marriage and commerce.<Cite source="midrash-rabbah-sefaria" locator="Bereshit Rabbah 9:7" />
When the angels debate whether humans should be created at all, Kindness and Righteousness argue for,
while Truth and Peace argue against, since man is "full of lies" and "full of discord". God creates
him regardless.<Cite source="midrash-rabbah-sefaria" locator="Bereshit Rabbah 8:5" />
The ideal is mastery, not elimination: "Who is mighty? He who subdues his inclination."<Cite source="mishnah-kulp" locator="Avot 4:1" />
The inclination begins as a passing traveler, becomes a guest and finally the master of the house.<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Sukkah 52b" />
The Talmud names both the inner "yeast in the dough" and outward subjugation to empires as the forces
that keep a will set on God's will from acting on it.<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Berakhot 17a" />

#### Eden, mortality and the limits of inheritance

Rabbinic readings of Genesis 3 vary. One midrash has God create humans from both upper and lower realms
so that "if he sins he will die".<Cite source="midrash-rabbah-sefaria" locator="Bereshit Rabbah 8:11" />
The Talmud concludes that some died only "due to Adam's sin with the serpent", though themselves
without sin: there is "death without sin".<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Shabbat 55b" />
What passes from Adam in this view is mortality, while guilt stays with the person who sins.<Cite source="tanakh-jps-gender-sensitive" locator="Ezekiel 18:20" />
One aggadah speaks of a moral contamination from the serpent that ceased for Israel at Sinai. The same
passage records a dissenting view, and the idea is not developed into a doctrine of inherited guilt.<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Shabbat 146a" />

Medieval readers diverge sharply. Maimonides, in his code, reads "knowing good and evil" as the human
power to choose freely.<Cite source="maimonides-mishneh-torah-touger" locator="Teshuvah 5:1" />
In the *Guide*, he argues that Adam first knew true and false by intellect, and after following
appetite and imagination became absorbed in the lesser register of the proper and improper.<Cite source="maimonides-guide-friedlander" locator="I.2" />
Nachmanides holds instead that humans originally acted "naturally", like the heavens, and that the
fruit gave rise to "will and desire". Choice is "a godlike attribute" but, for humans, a burden.<Cite source="ramban-torah-chavel" locator="on Genesis 2:9" />
On this reading the same endowment explains both human grandeur and human danger.

#### The philosophers: wisdom, matter and choice

Saadia Gaon argued that God's gift of wisdom is what lifts humans above all creatures. That wisdom lets
them remember the past, farm, build, govern kingdoms and chart the stars. It makes the human being the
"axis" of the world, and therefore the one who is commanded, warned and judged.<Cite source="saadia-emunot-ibn-tibbon" locator="IV.1" />
He denied that God compels either obedience or rebellion.<Cite source="saadia-emunot-ibn-tibbon" locator="IV.4" />
He located much disorder in letting one love tyrannize the others, when God implanted many loves to be
weighed together.<Cite source="saadia-emunot-ibn-tibbon" locator="X.1" />

Maimonides identified the divine image with the intellect, not bodily shape.<Cite source="maimonides-mishneh-torah-touger" locator="Yesodei ha-Torah 4:8" />
He traced human merits to form and vices to matter.<Cite source="maimonides-guide-friedlander" locator="III.8" />
He also observed that the largest class of evils is the one people inflict on themselves.<Cite source="maimonides-guide-friedlander" locator="III.12" />
He held that temperament may make a virtue easier or harder, but never necessary.<Cite source="maimonides-eight-chapters" locator="ch. 8" />
Free choice, he wrote, is "a pillar" of the Torah and the commandments.<Cite source="maimonides-mishneh-torah-touger" locator="Teshuvah 5:3" />
God willed it, just as He willed the natures of the elements.<Cite source="maimonides-mishneh-torah-touger" locator="Teshuvah 5:4" />
Maimonides also reconciled the rabbinic praise of the one who desires wrong and refrains with the
philosophers' praise of the one who does not desire it. The rabbinic dictum, he argued, concerns ritual
prohibitions, while a noble soul should not even crave bloodshed or theft.<Cite source="maimonides-eight-chapters" locator="ch. 6" />

#### Majesty and covenant

Joseph B. Soloveitchik reads the two creation narratives as "a real contradiction in the nature of man".<Cite source="soloveitchik-lonely-man-of-faith" locator="p. 10" />
Majestic Adam I is commanded to subdue nature. For him, "there is no dignity without responsibility".<Cite source="soloveitchik-lonely-man-of-faith" locator="pp. 13–14" />
Covenantal Adam II seeks the God who addresses him.<Cite source="soloveitchik-lonely-man-of-faith" locator="p. 17" />
For Soloveitchik, Halakhah treats these as complementary in a single world. That world can plunge into
"ugliness and hatefulness" or be roused to "redeeming activity".<Cite source="soloveitchik-lonely-man-of-faith" locator="p. 51" />

#### Remedy: Torah, teshuvah and hope

The rabbinic remedy is already given. "I created an evil inclination … and I created Torah as its
antidote".<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Kiddushin 30b" />
God assists the one who struggles.<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Sukkah 52b" />
*Teshuvah* means abandoning the sin, regretting it, resolving before God and confessing. It is proven
complete when one faces the same temptation and refrains.<Cite source="maimonides-mishneh-torah-touger" locator="Teshuvah 2:1–2" />
In one Talmudic view, repentance from love turns intentional sins into merits.<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Yoma 86b" />
The Sages dispute whether penitents stand above the wholly righteous.<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Berakhot 34b" />
Maimonides also warns that persistent, willful wickedness can bring the withholding of repentance
itself.<Cite source="maimonides-mishneh-torah-touger" locator="Teshuvah 6:3" />
In the end, God will slaughter the evil inclination. The righteous will weep that they overcame a
mountain, and the wicked that they failed before a hair.<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Sukkah 52a" />
Nachmanides envisions a messianic "circumcision of the heart", when doing good will again be natural,
as before Adam's sin.<Cite source="ramban-torah-chavel" locator="on Deuteronomy 30:6" />

#### Streams and emphases

Orthodox thought treats halakhic observance as the binding form of this formation. Reform's 1999
principles affirm that "every human being is created in the image of God", and speak of the mitzvot
"that address us" and of "transgression and repentance".<Cite source="ccar-principles-1999" locator="Statement of Principles" />
The anthropology is broadly shared across streams; what differs is the authority of the means.
