# Steelman packet: Hindu traditions — `great-and-terrible`

Researcher lane: `hinduism`. Steelman only; no Christian critique. Research date: 2026-10-05.
All primary passages below were read in public-domain English translations on archive.org
(full-text OCR checked against the page headers of the scanned edition) unless marked otherwise.

## 1. Scope

The answer should represent **four Vedāntic and non-Vedāntic positions**, foregrounding the two
Vedānta schools that give the fullest anthropology, with the Gītā as the shared text:

1. **The Bhagavad Gītā as common ground.** Every Vedānta school comments on it, and its
   chapter 3 (Arjuna's question at 3.36: why does a person do wrong "though reluctant, as if
   constrained by force?"), chapter 14 (the three guṇas) and chapter 16 (divine and demonic
   endowments) address this question more directly than any other classical text. It is the best
   anchor for "knowingly doing wrong".
2. **Advaita Vedānta (Śaṅkara)**: the human problem as *adhyāsa* / *avidyā* — a beginningless
   mis-identification of the conscious Self with body-mind. Must be represented because it is
   the most influential intellectual account, but must not stand for the whole.
3. **Viśiṣṭādvaita (Rāmānuja)**: a real, eternal, personal soul whose essential knowledge and
   bliss are *contracted* by beginningless karma; God as inner controller who permits but does not
   coerce; bhakti and surrender (prapatti) as remedy. This is the strongest theistic,
   guilt-preserving, person-preserving answer and should get equal weight with Advaita.
4. **Dvaita (Madhva)**: real plurality and permanent dependence of souls on Viṣṇu; souls as
   dependent agents; the contested doctrine of innately different soul-natures. Included because it
   materially changes the answer on dignity and on whether wickedness has a single explanation.
5. **Nyāya (Gautama's sūtras; Udayana)** and **Mīmāṃsā (Kumārila)** as briefer contrasts: Nyāya's
   causal chain (misapprehension → faults → activity → birth → pain) and realist self whose
   qualities include desire and aversion; Mīmāṃsā's insistence that dharma is known through Vedic
   injunction and that the doer and enjoyer of an act must be one enduring self.

Not represented in depth: Sāṃkhya-Yoga (relevant to the guṇas but its own lane), Kashmir Śaivism
(Abhinavagupta) — see §10 and §13 — and modern reformers. Purāṇic/bhakti literature beyond the
Gītā is mentioned only where Rāmānuja uses it.

## 2. Anthropology: what a human being fundamentally is

- **Shared frame.** A human being is an embodied self (ātman / jīva) — conscious, not identical
  with body, senses or mind — passing through many births under karma. The body-mind complex is
  made of *prakṛti* and its three guṇas (Gītā 13.21; 14.5). The Gītā calls the jīva God's "higher
  nature" (7.5) and "a portion of Myself" (15.7).
- **Advaita.** The innermost Self is pure, unchanging consciousness, numerically identical with
  Brahman; the "individual" is that consciousness as apparently limited by adjuncts (upādhi).
  Agency, enjoyment, desire and "I am happy / miserable" belong to the mind-body and are
  superimposed on the Self (Śaṅkara on Gītā 13.21, 14.6; BSBh adhyāsa-bhāṣya).
- **Viśiṣṭādvaita.** The soul is an eternal knower whose essential nature is knowledge and bliss;
  its attributive knowledge is "in itself unlimited" but "capable of contraction and expansion",
  and in embodied existence is contracted by karma (Śrī Bhāṣya I.1.1, SBE 48 p. 63; I.1.14 p. 232).
  Souls are *modes* of Brahman, constituting its body (I.2.12, p. 271). Souls are "not
  intrinsically divided into several kinds" (Vedārthasaṅgraha §99, as quoted in IEP).
- **Dvaita.** The soul is eternal, atomic, a real agent, but "absolutely dependent" (paratantra)
  on Viṣṇu, its capability of action derived from the Lord (Madhva BSBh II.3.33–41). Souls differ
  innately from one another (IEP, Stoker §3).
- **Nyāya.** The self is an eternal non-material substance; "desire, aversion, volition, pleasure,
  pain and intelligence are the marks of the soul" (NS 1.1.10). Cognition and moral faults are
  real qualities of the self (IEP Nyāya §2a–b).
- **Mīmāṃsā (Kumārila).** The self is eternal and distinct from the body; it is both doer and
  enjoyer across states of life and births, which is what makes Vedic injunction meaningful
  (Ślokavārttika, Ātmavāda vv. 3–4, 29–33).

## 3. Metaphysical context

- **Advaita**: nondual Brahman; a two-tier ontology — the conventional empirical order
  (vyāvahārika), in which karma, agency, ethics and the Lord (Īśvara) are fully operative, and the
  ultimate order (pāramārthika), in which only Brahman is (SEP Śaṅkara §2.3). Saṃsāra is
  beginningless; inequalities among beings are due to their merit and demerit, not divine
  partiality (BSBh II.1.34–36).
- **Viśiṣṭādvaita**: one personal Brahman (Nārāyaṇa with Śrī), "antagonistic to all evil",
  comprising infinite auspicious qualities, with souls and matter as its real body (Śrī Bhāṣya
  I.2.12, p. 271; IEP Rāmānuja §2). The inner Ruler moves souls only by consenting to their own
  volitional effort (II.3.40–41).
- **Dvaita**: three eternal realities (God, souls, matter) and five-fold difference
  (pañcabheda); God alone independent (svatantra), all else dependent (IEP Stoker). Karma
  is beginningless, yet karma itself depends for its existence on the Lord (Madhva BSBh II.1.35–37,
  pp. 104–105).
- **Nyāya**: realist pluralism of substances; God (Īśvara) as intelligent guide of insentient
  atoms and karma (Uddyotakara, NV 4.1.21, quoted in IEP §3); Udayana's design-type arguments
  (Nyāyakusumāñjali ch. 5, per IEP).
- **Mīmāṃsā**: an eternal, authorless Veda; dharma as a "goal defined by injunction"
  (MS 1.1.2); classical Mīmāṃsā did not originally make liberation the summum bonum (SEP
  Kumārila, §1) — an important difference from Vedānta.

## 4. Why humans possess remarkable capacities

- **Upaniṣadic source text (strong).** Aitareya Āraṇyaka II.3.2: in man "the self develops
  gradually, for he is most endowed with knowledge. He says what he has known, he sees what he has
  known. He knows what is to happen to-morrow, he knows heaven and hell. By means of the mortal he
  desires the immortal" — whereas other animals "go so far and no further" (SBE 1, pp. 222–223).
  II.3.3.1: "Whatever he reaches, he wishes to go beyond." Aitareya Upaniṣad (= AA II.4.2.2–3):
  the cosmic powers reject cow and horse as a dwelling; offered man they say "Well done",
  "Therefore man is well done" (SBE 1, p. 239).
- **Advaita.** Remarkable capacities are the light of the Self — pure consciousness — shining
  through a mind; sattva, being "stainless" and "lucid", is the guṇa through which knowledge
  and happiness manifest (Śaṅkara on Gītā 14.6, 14.10). Human birth is "the means of attaining
  the (four) aspirations (purushartha), and ... very hard to attain" (Śaṅkara on 9.33,
  Sastri p. 233).
- **Viśiṣṭādvaita.** Capacities are traces of the soul's essential, unlimited knowledge and bliss,
  still operative though contracted; and the soul as God's body/mode shares with Brahman the
  nature of being a knower (IEP §2, Śrī Bhāṣya pp. 63, 232).
- **Guṇa account of civilization.** Rajas produces "action such as husbandry" (Śaṅkara on 14.10)
  — productive, civilizational activity — while sattva produces knowledge and happiness. Creativity
  and enterprise are thus partly *rajasic*.
- **Moral capacity.** The Gītā's "divine endowment" lists fearlessness, purity, almsgiving,
  self-control, harmlessness, truth, compassion to creatures, forgiveness (16.1–3). Gītā 6.32
  grounds compassion in self-comparison: the highest yogin sees "by comparison with himself"
  that what is painful to him is painful to all (Śaṅkara: "he causes pain to no being").

## 5. Why humans behave destructively or wrongly (incl. knowingly)

- **Gītā 3.36–43 (core).** Arjuna: "by what dragged on ... does a man, though reluctant, commit
  sin, as if constrained by force?" (3.36). Answer: "It is Desire, it is Wrath, born of the energy
  of Rajas, all-devouring, all-sinful; that ... is the foe" (3.37). Desire covers wisdom "as fire
  is surrounded by smoke, as a mirror by rust" (3.38); its seat is "the senses, mind, and reason";
  "veiling wisdom through these, it deludes" (3.40). Remedy: restrain the senses and, knowing the
  Self higher than reason, "slay ... the enemy in the form of desire, hard to conquer" (3.41–43).
- **Śaṅkara on 3.39 — the precise phenomenon of knowing wrongdoing.** "The wise man knows even
  before suffering the consequence, that he has been led by desire to evil ways, and therefore he
  feels ever miserable. Whence desire is a constant enemy of the wise, not of the ignorant", who
  takes desire for a friend until suffering teaches him. And on 3.37: "it is only when urged by
  desire that a man commits sin"; obstructed desire becomes wrath.
- **Rāmānuja on 3.37** (Govindacharya pp. 122–123): lust is "sharpened all the more by past
  habits", and when hampered "turns into hate (or wrath)", "resulting in the perpetration of violent
  acts against (such) creatures." Rāmānuja reads 3.36 as the obstacle facing one already desirous of
  the path of knowledge — i.e. a person with good intentions.
- **The causal ladder, Gītā 2.62–63:** dwelling on objects → attachment → desire → wrath → delusion
  → failure of memory → loss of buddhi (Sastri renders "loss of conscience") → ruin.
- **Guṇas (Gītā 14).** Rajas is "the source of thirst and attachment" binding by attachment to
  action; tamas, "born of unwisdom", binds "by heedlessness, indolence and sloth", and "covers the
  judgment caused by Sattva" (14.7–9 with Śaṅkara). Guṇas alternate in predominance (14.10).
- **Demonic endowment (Gītā 16.4, 16.7–21).** Ostentation, arrogance, anger, insolence; lack of
  purity and truth; a worldview of a universe "without a Lord ... brought about by lust"; the
  "triple ... gate to hell, destructive of the self: lust, wrath, and greed" (16.21). Śaṅkara insists
  this is described "as an attribute of some living men", so that it may be recognized and avoided
  (on 16.6).
- **Advaita root.** Behind desire lies adhyāsa: the "natural", beginningless superimposition
  "which has its cause in wrong knowledge", producing "That am I", "That is mine" (BSBh intro,
  SBE 34 pp. 3–4) — "the cause of all evil" (p. 9). Men, "although distinguished by superior
  intelligence", act like animals in this respect (pp. 7–8). SEP §3.4: avidyā produces fear and
  anxiety, so people seek wholeness in wealth, status, pleasure, which "are perpetually bound to
  fail".
- **Viśiṣṭādvaita root.** Souls' understanding is "obscured by Nescience operating in the form of
  beginningless karman" (Śrī Bhāṣya I.1.4, p. 198). Wrongdoing is the soul's own volitional effort,
  which the Lord permits but does not originate; those "resolved on lines of action altogether
  displeasing to him" are given delight in such actions as punishment (II.3.41, pp. 557–558) — sin
  compounds itself.
- **Nyāya root.** NS 1.1.2: the "world" is the uninterrupted chain misapprehension → faults →
  activity → birth → pain. Faults are three groups: affection (lust, avarice, covetousness),
  aversion (anger, envy, malignity, hatred, implacability), and stupidity/delusion (moha:
  misapprehension, suspicion, arrogance, carelessness) (NS 4.1.3); delusion is "the worst" because
  without it the other two do not arise (4.1.6).
- **Dvaita.** The Lord impels the soul "only according to (the tendency of) his previous actions
  and his effort (or aptitude)" (BSBh II.3.42); souls' innate moral propensities are worked out by
  karma (IEP Stoker §3). Some souls are said to be eternally of misery (BSBh III.1.21 quoting Padma
  Purāṇa, p. 178) — contested and sensitive; see §13.

## 6. Do greatness and wickedness share a common explanation?

**Yes, in most schools — and this is the strongest feature of the answer.**
- In the Gītā both arise from one embodied self conditioned by the same three guṇas: sattva
  yields knowledge and happiness, rajas yields productive action *and* thirst/greed/wrath, tamas
  yields delusion and sloth (14.6–10). The rajas that builds farms and cities is the rajas that
  becomes "all-devouring" desire (3.37).
- In Advaita the same consciousness that makes the human uniquely knowing is the ground on which
  superimposition occurs; error is possible only for a knower. Greatness = the Self's light;
  terribleness = its misidentification.
- In Viśiṣṭādvaita the greatness is the soul's essential nature (unlimited knowledge, bliss,
  belonging to God); wickedness is its contraction by self-chosen and accumulated karma. One
  subject, two states.
- In Nyāya, desire and aversion are qualities of the same self that knows; misapprehension
  corrupts a real cognitive power.
- **Exception / qualification: Dvaita.** Madhva's gradation of souls distributes the extremes of
  virtue and vice partly among *different* innate soul-natures (IEP Stoker §3), so the common
  explanation is "dependence on God plus innate nature worked out through karma" rather than one
  self-in-tension.

## 7. Rationality; moral knowledge; conscience; evil; agency; desire

- **Rationality.** Universally prized (the "ascertainment of the ultimate good requires correct
  apprehension of reality", IEP Nyāya §3); buddhi (reason, "characterized by determination") is
  superior to mind and senses, but the Self is superior to reason (Śaṅkara on 3.42). Reason itself
  can be colonized by desire (3.40) — rationalization is anticipated.
- **Moral knowledge.** Two sources: (a) scripture/dharma — Mīmāṃsā: dharma is known through Vedic
  injunction, because perception cannot reach a not-yet-existent good and inference lacks an
  observed mark for it (Ślokavārttika, Codanā-sūtra vv. 16–18); Rāmānuja likewise holds the Vedas
  are the primary source of moral knowledge (IEP Rāmānuja §4); (b) a natural moral perception —
  Gītā 6.32 self-comparison; Kaṭha Up. I.2.1–2: "the good and the pleasant approach man: the wise
  goes round about them and distinguishes them" (SBE 15 p. 8).
- **Conscience.** No single technical equivalent; closest are buddhi (discerning intellect, whose
  loss is "ruin", 2.63) and the inner Ruler (antaryāmin) dwelling in the heart (Gītā 18.61; Śrī
  Bhāṣya II.3.40). Do not import a Christian conscience concept.
- **Evil.** Classified by school: Advaita — ignorance (avidyā/adhyāsa) primarily, with desire as
  its proximate engine; Viśiṣṭādvaita — karmic contraction of the soul plus turning from God; the
  Gītā — guṇa-dynamics (rajas/tamas) and desire-anger; Nyāya — misapprehension producing faults;
  Dvaita — innate disposition plus karma under God's governance; Mīmāṃsā — transgression of
  injunction/prohibition (adharma).
- **Agency.** Gītā 6.5–6: "let a man raise himself by himself ... he alone is the friend of himself,
  he alone is the enemy of himself" (Śaṅkara: the external enemy "is made an enemy, only by
  himself"). Ramanuja II.3.41: action "primarily depends on the volitional effort of the soul";
  the fruit "properly belongs to him only". Madhva II.3.33, 40: soul a real but dependent agent,
  like a carpenter under a master. Advaita: agency is real conventionally (karma and guilt
  operative), but ultimately superimposed (Gītā 5.15: "wisdom is enveloped by unwisdom; thereby
  mortals are deluded" — thinking "I act").
- **Desire.** Not evil per se in the Gītā (it is God's own form "not opposed to dharma", 7.11 —
  not checked here, do not cite), but the insatiable desire that veils wisdom (3.39). BU IV.4.5:
  "a person consists of desires. And as is his desire, so is his will; and as is his will, so is his
  deed; and whatever deed he does, that he will reap" (SBE 15 p. 176).
- **Moral guilt and continuity preserved?** Viśiṣṭādvaita, Dvaita, Nyāya, Mīmāṃsā: yes,
  robustly — the same enduring self acts and reaps (Ślokavārttika Ātmavāda v. 29: "it is always the
  doer that enjoys the result of the action"). Advaita: yes at the empirical level (karma,
  transmigration and moral discipline are real for the jīva), but ultimately individuality and
  agency are dissolved in liberation (SEP §4.3) — a genuine and recognized point of difference.

## 8. Can the human condition be remedied?

Yes — all schools are soteriological optimists, though the means differ.
- **Karma-yoga** (Gītā 3; Śaṅkara accepts it as a purifier of mind, SEP §4.1): acting without
  attachment to fruits; restraint of senses (3.41).
- **Jñāna** (Advaita): only knowledge of the Self as Brahman "negates the error of superimposition"
  (SEP §3.4); prerequisites include discrimination, dispassion, self-control and desire for
  liberation (BSBh 1.1.1, per SEP §4.1). Śaṅkara on 18.66: abandon dharma and adharma; "I shall
  liberate thee ... by manifesting Myself as thy own Self."
- **Bhakti and prapatti** (Rāmānuja): "Those alone who resort to Me as their Refuge overcross this
  māyā" (7.14, with Rāmānuja); even one "of flagitiously wicked ways" who worships exclusively "is
  to be esteemed as virtuous" because his resolve is now right, and "speedily becometh ... a holy
  soul" (9.30–31). On 18.66 Rāmānuja gives two readings: renounce fruit, ownership and authorship of
  all paths; or, since accumulated sins are too many to expiate by rites, take God alone as refuge
  — "Me, the Most Merciful ... demanding no price whatever for admission" — and "I will untrammel
  thee from all the fetters of the sins" (Govindacharya pp. 563–565). Vedārthasaṅgraha §99: "no way
  of emancipation, other than surrender to the supreme Lord" (via IEP §5). Later Śrī Vaiṣṇavism
  divides on whether grace is conditional on effort (Vaḍagalai) or freely given (Teṅgalai) (IEP §5).
- **Grace in Dvaita**: "Attaining Viṣṇu's grace is the soul's only hope of achieving liberation"
  (IEP Stoker §3); capacities "exist by the grace of the Lord" (Madhva BSBh II.3.41, quoting
  Paiṅgi-śruti).
- **Nyāya**: true knowledge removes misapprehension, so faults, activity, birth and pain cease in
  reverse order (NS 1.1.2).
- **Mīmāṃsā**: performance of obligatory duties and abstention from prohibited ones; later
  Mīmāṃsakas adopt a goal of release (Jha's introduction, not Kumārila's verse — do not cite as
  Kumārila).

## 9. Dignity: inherent or derived?

- **Advaita: inherent and maximal, but impersonal.** The true Self in every being is Brahman; "in
  a Brāhmaṇa ... in a cow, in an elephant, as also in a dog and in a dog-eater, the wise see the
  same" (Gītā 5.18 with Śaṅkara, who nevertheless ranks the beings by guṇa). What is dignified is
  the Self, not the empirical personality.
- **Viśiṣṭādvaita: inherent and personal, yet relational.** Every soul has knowledge as its
  essential nature and souls are "fundamentally alike and equal", and that nature is "wholly
  subservient and instrumental to God" (VS §99 via IEP). Dignity is intrinsic to the soul but
  consists in belonging to God (śeṣa/śeṣin).
- **Dvaita: derived and graded.** Souls are dependent realities with innate, unequal capacities
  (IEP Stoker §§0, 3).
- **Precious human birth.** Śaṅkara on 9.33 (human birth "very hard to attain"); Aitareya
  texts (man "well done"; "most endowed with knowledge").
- **Honest tension.** Śaṅkara (BSBh I.3.25, SBE 34 pp. 196–197) says śāstra entitles "men only"
  — and his footnoted reasons, per the translator's gloss of the commentators, restrict ritual
  entitlement to the three upper varṇas; Gītā 9.32 promises the supreme goal to "women, vaiśyas as
  well as śūdras" who take refuge — while still calling them "of a sinful birth". Caste
  hierarchy is part of the classical record and should be acknowledged, not hidden.

## 10. Internal school differences that materially change the answer

|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Root of wrongdoing | avidyā / adhyāsa | karma-contracted knowledge; self-chosen effort | innate disposition + karma, under God | misapprehension → faults | adharma (violating injunction) |

**Kashmir Śaiva nondualism (Abhinavagupta)** would add a distinctive answer — Śiva's own
self-concealment as a free act, and impurities (mala) that contract consciousness — but no
primary or reliable reference source was opened for this packet; mention only as an omitted
alternative.

## 11. What these traditions genuinely explain well

1. **Akrasia with a phenomenology.** The Gītā poses the exact question — wrongdoing "though
   reluctant, as if constrained" — and answers with an account (desire-wrath, seated in senses,
   mind *and reason*, veiling knowledge) that explains why knowing better does not suffice and why
   reason can become desire's advocate. Śaṅkara's remark that desire is the enemy specifically of
   the wise, who see the trap and are "ever miserable", captures the inner division of the
   conscientious wrongdoer.
2. **One root for achievement and destruction.** The guṇa analysis makes enterprise, ambition and
   civilization-building (rajas) the same energy that becomes greed, domination and rage — an
   economical explanation of why the most capable cultures are often the most dangerous.
3. **Escalation.** Gītā 2.62–63 and Rāmānuja's II.3.41 (God gives delight in chosen evil as its
   punishment) explain habituation: wrongdoing reshapes desire, and repeated choices become
   character (vāsanā) and next life.
4. **Dignity that survives depravity.** Because the self's nature is consciousness/knowledge (and,
   for theists, God's own portion or body), no wickedness erases the worth of the person; even the
   "flagitiously wicked" can turn (9.30). The tradition thus holds high dignity and severe
   diagnosis together without contradiction.
5. **Self-transcending aspiration.** Aitareya Āraṇyaka's man who "by means of the mortal desires
   the immortal" and "wishes to go beyond" whatever he reaches explains restlessness and the
   insatiability that drives both greatness and destructive excess.
6. **Moral responsibility with long causal history.** Karma explains inherited dispositions and
   unequal starting points while keeping the agent answerable (Rāmānuja, Kumārila).
7. **Psychology of ignorance-driven grasping (Advaita).** Fear and lack produced by
   misidentification explain why people seek wholeness in status and possession — an account of
   domination as anxious self-inflation.

## 12. Strongest representative thinkers for this question

- **Śaṅkara** (primary) — the adhyāsa-bhāṣya and Gītā commentary give the most rigorous
  ignorance-based account and the sharpest gloss on knowing wrongdoing (on 3.39).
- **Rāmānuja** (primary) — the strongest guilt-preserving, person-preserving theistic account:
  contracted knowledge, divine permission without coercion, prapatti for unpayable sin.
- **Madhva** (primary) — necessary to show that Vedānta can make souls permanently distinct,
  dependent and innately different; changes the answer on dignity and evil.
- **Kumārila Bhaṭṭa** (primary, limited use) — for moral knowledge via injunction and the
  enduring doer-enjoyer that grounds responsibility.
- **Udayana** (primary, light use only) — represents Nyāya's realist self and theism; his own
  texts were not read for this packet, so cite Nyāya positions from the Nyāya-sūtra and IEP, and
  name Udayana only as the school's culminating defender of the self and God (IEP).
- **Abhinavagupta** (specialist) — **not recommended** for this answer (unverified; marginal to
  the core question).

## 13. Uncertainties / contested interpretations

1. **Madhva's soul-types.** The three classes (fit for liberation; eternally transmigrating;
   eternally in darkness) are attested in Madhva's BSBh III.1.21 only via a Padma Purāṇa quotation
   in Subba Rau's translation, and in secondary literature; Madhva often cites texts not otherwise
   extant, which scholars debate. Present as "Madhva and his tradition teach…", note it is
   contested and sensitive, and avoid caricature ("predestination") beyond what IEP says.
2. **Advaita and moral guilt.** Whether Advaita "preserves" guilt depends on level: fully at the
   empirical level; dissolved at the ultimate. Do not say Advaita denies responsibility.
3. **Śaṅkara's authorship** of the Gītā-bhāṣya is generally accepted; of some minor works
   (e.g. Vivekacūḍāmaṇi) disputed — so the packet avoids Vivekacūḍāmaṇi for "precious human birth".
4. **Rāmānuja and prapatti.** Lester and others argue prapatti doctrine belongs to the Gadyas,
   possibly inauthentic, not the main works; others read VS §99 and Gītā 18.66 as supporting it
   (IEP §5). The Govindacharya translator's footnote on 18.66 also adds comparative Christian
   remarks (a Matthew citation) — these are the translator's, not Rāmānuja's; do not use them.
5. **Rāmānuja's dates**: traditional 1017–1137; recent scholarship 1077–1157 (IEP).
6. **Nyāya liberation** as a state without cognition or pleasure is classically attributed and
   criticized by Vedāntins; not verified here — omit or mark.
7. **Translation of key terms.** "Loss of conscience" for *buddhi-nāśa* (2.63) is Sastri's;
   "reason/understanding" is more literal. "Sin" for *pāpa* is conventional.

## 16. Thinker profile notes

**Śaṅkara** (`shankara`). Dates debated: early scholarship 788–820 CE; Nakamura 700–750; Potter
late 7th–early 8th c.; SEP says "flourished during the eighth century CE". Setting: hagiographies
(much later) place his birth at Kālaḍi, Kerala; renouncer; teacher Govinda, in Gauḍapāda's
lineage. School: Advaita Vedānta — systematizer, not founder. Why relevant: the adhyāsa-bhāṣya is
the classical statement that human misery and wrongdoing rest on a beginningless
misidentification; his Gītā commentary analyses desire veiling wisdom. Key ideas: adhyāsa
(superimposition); avidyā; two levels of reality; witness-consciousness (sākṣin); knowledge as sole
direct means of liberation; jīvanmukti. Works: Brahmasūtra-bhāṣya; Bhagavad-gītā-bhāṣya;
Upaniṣad-bhāṣyas; Upadeśasāhasrī. Reference: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/shankara/

**Rāmānuja** (`ramanuja`). Traditionally 1017–1137; recent scholarship 1077–1157 (Carman, via
IEP). Setting: born Śrīperumbudūr near Chennai, Tamil-speaking Brahmin family; Śrī Vaiṣṇava
teacher. School: Viśiṣṭādvaita Vedānta. Why relevant: gives a real, eternal person whose innate
knowledge and bliss are contracted by karma, with genuine responsibility under divine permission
and a remedy in bhakti/prapatti. Key ideas: body–soul (śarīra–śarīrin) relation of world to God;
co-ordinate predication; refutation of Advaita avidyā; contraction/expansion of knowledge; divine
permission (anumati); bhakti and surrender. Works: Śrī Bhāṣya; Vedārthasaṅgraha; Gītā-bhāṣya;
Gadyatraya (authenticity contested). Reference: https://iep.utm.edu/ramanuja/

**Madhva** (`madhva`). 1238–1317 (IEP). Setting: 13th-century South India (Tuḷu region,
Karnataka — not on IEP page; confirm before using); considered himself an avatāra of Vāyu. School:
Dvaita / Tattvavāda. Why relevant: makes souls permanently distinct, dependent agents with innate
natures; changes answers on dignity and evil. Key ideas: svatantra/paratantra; pañcabheda; realist
epistemology; gradation (tāratamya) of souls; dependent agency; liberation by Viṣṇu's grace through
bhakti. Works: Brahmasūtra-bhāṣya; Anuvyākhyāna; Gītā-bhāṣya; Viṣṇutattvanirṇaya (titles beyond
the BSBh not verified here). Reference: https://iep.utm.edu/madhva/

**Kumārila Bhaṭṭa** (`kumarila`). Flourished c. 660 CE (SEP), contemporary of Dharmakīrti.
School: Pūrva Mīmāṃsā (Bhāṭṭa sub-school). Why relevant (limited): moral knowledge — dharma known
only through Vedic injunction; the enduring self as doer-and-enjoyer underwrites responsibility
across lives. Key ideas: intrinsic validity of cognition; authorless Veda; dharma as goal defined
by injunction; eternal self against Buddhist no-self. Works: Ślokavārttika; Tantravārttika;
Ṭupṭīkā. Reference: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kumaarila/

**Udayana** (`udayana`). c. 975 CE (IEP Nyāya). School: Nyāya (late classical, pre-Navya). Why
relevant (light): culminating defender of the enduring self and of God against Buddhists; Nyāya's
account of faults rooted in misapprehension. Key ideas: arguments for Īśvara (design-type, from
products); the self as eternal substance; theory of universals. Works: Nyāyakusumāñjali;
Ātmatattvaviveka; Nyāyavārttikatātparyapariśuddhi. Reference: https://iep.utm.edu/nyaya/
(Udayana's own texts not read for this packet.)

## The view

Hindu traditions read the human being as an embodied self (ātman) whose own nature is conscious and, in theistic Vedānta, belongs to God: the Bhagavad Gītā calls the individual self God's "higher nature" and "a portion of Myself."<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="7.5; 15.7" /> An Upaniṣad marks the human as "most endowed with knowledge," the creature who "knows what is to happen to-morrow" and "by means of the mortal ... desires the immortal."<Cite source="muller-upanishads-1" locator="Aitareya Āraṇyaka II.3.2.4" />

Wrongdoing arises when that self, bound in a body-mind made of three strands (guṇas), is overrun by desire. Arjuna asks why a person sins "though reluctant, as if constrained by force"; Krishna answers that desire and wrath, born of rajas, veil wisdom through the senses, mind and reason itself.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="3.36–40" /> The schools differ on the root. Advaita Vedānta, especially Śaṅkara, traces it to a beginningless superimposition of body and mind onto the Self, "the cause of all evil."<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra" locator="I.1.1, intro., pp. 3–9" /> For Rāmānuja the soul's innate knowledge is "contracted" by karma, and it remains answerable for its own choices.<Cite source="thibaut-ramanuja-sribhashya" locator="I.1.1, p. 63; II.3.41, p. 557" /> Greatness and wickedness are therefore two states of one self: its native light, and that light obscured. The cure is knowledge, disciplined action, and devotion and surrender to God.

## What this explains well

The Gītā names the precise puzzle of doing what one knows is wrong and gives it a psychology: desire takes up residence even in reason, so knowing better is not enough. Śaṅkara adds that desire is the enemy of the wise, who see in advance where it leads and are "ever miserable."<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="3.39, comm." /> The guṇa analysis explains why civilization and domination share a root: rajas drives productive work like "husbandry" and also insatiable craving.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="14.7–10, comm." /> Habit and escalation are built in: dwelling on objects leads by stages to wrath, delusion and ruin.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="2.62–63" /> And because the self's worth lies in its nature rather than its record, high dignity and severe diagnosis stand together: even one "of flagitiously wicked ways" who turns to God "speedily becometh ... a holy soul."<Cite source="govindacharya-gita-ramanuja" locator="9.30–31" />

## Deep dive

#### The shared text: Gītā 3, 14 and 16

The Bhagavad Gītā is the common ground of the Vedānta schools, and it addresses this question directly. Arjuna's question at 3.36 concerns a person who does not want to do wrong yet is "dragged on." Krishna's answer is layered. Desire, transformed into wrath when obstructed, is "all-devouring, all-sinful" (3.37). It covers wisdom "as fire is surrounded by smoke, as a mirror by rust" (3.38). Its seats are "the senses, mind, and reason" (3.40), so reason is not a neutral judge standing outside desire. The remedy is to restrain the senses and, knowing the Self that is "superior to reason," to subdue "the self by the self" (3.41–43).<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="3.36–43" /> Chapter 14 places this inside a cosmology of three guṇas: sattva brings lucidity and happiness, rajas brings "thirst and attachment" and action, tamas brings heedlessness that "covers the judgment."<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="14.6–9, comm." /> Chapter 16 sets the "divine endowment" (harmlessness, truth, compassion to creatures, forgiveness) beside the "demonic" (ostentation, arrogance, anger), whose world is one "without a Lord ... brought about by lust," and names "lust, wrath, and greed" the "triple ... gate to hell, destructive of the self."<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="16.1–8, 16.21" /> Śaṅkara stresses that the demonic nature is described as a trait "of some living men," so that it can be recognized and avoided.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="16.6, comm." />

The Gītā also insists on agency: "he alone is the friend of himself, he alone is the enemy of himself."<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="6.5–6" /> Its moral vision includes a natural basis for compassion: the highest yogin sees "by comparison with himself" that what hurts him hurts all.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="6.32" />

#### Advaita: superimposition and the anxious self

Śaṅkara opens his Brahma Sūtra commentary by defining superimposition (adhyāsa): it is "a natural procedure," beginningless and "which has its cause in wrong knowledge," by which we merge Self and not-Self and say "That am I," "That is mine."<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra" locator="I.1.1, intro., pp. 3–4" /> Men, "although distinguished by superior intelligence," act like animals in this respect, and this wrong notion is "the cause of all evil."<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra" locator="I.1.1, intro., pp. 7–9" /> As a recent interpreter puts it, the error breeds fear and lack, so people seek wholeness in wealth, status and pleasure, which "are perpetually bound to fail."<Cite source="sep-shankara" locator="§3.4" /> On the question of knowingly doing wrong, Śaṅkara's gloss on Gītā 3.39 is notable: "the wise man knows even before suffering the consequence, that he has been led by desire to evil ways, and therefore he feels ever miserable."<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="3.39, comm." />

Advaita works on two levels. At the conventional level, karma, agency, moral discipline and the Lord are fully real. Śaṅkara explains the unequal lots of beings by their merit and demerit across a beginningless saṃsāra, so that the Lord is not partial or cruel.<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra" locator="II.1.34–36, pp. 357–360" /> Ultimately, though, only nondual Brahman is real.<Cite source="sep-shankara" locator="§2.3" /> Ethical virtues and selfless action purify the mind, but liberation comes by knowledge alone, and it dissolves the sense of individual agency.<Cite source="sep-shankara" locator="§§4.1, 4.3" />

#### Viśiṣṭādvaita: contracted knowledge, permitted freedom, surrender

Rāmānuja rejects Advaita's account of ignorance. For him the soul is an eternal knower whose knowledge is "in itself unlimited" but "capable of contraction," and in embodied life it is contracted "owing to the influence of work (karman)."<Cite source="thibaut-ramanuja-sribhashya" locator="I.1.1, p. 63" /> Souls are real modes of Brahman and form its body, "antagonistic to all evil," and their true nature is "obscured by ... the beginningless chain of works."<Cite source="thibaut-ramanuja-sribhashya" locator="I.2.12, p. 271" /> On responsibility he is precise. God, the inner Ruler, acts only by granting "permission (anumati)" to the soul's own "volitional effort," so the fruit "properly belongs to him only." Those who are resolved on what displeases God are given "a delight in such actions" as their punishment.<Cite source="thibaut-ramanuja-sribhashya" locator="II.3.40–41, pp. 556–558" /> This explains why wickedness escalates. Commenting on Gītā 3.37, he says lust is "sharpened ... by past habits" and turns, when blocked, into wrath and violence against others.<Cite source="govindacharya-gita-ramanuja" locator="3.37" />

The remedy is relational. Those who take refuge in God cross the bewildering power of māyā.<Cite source="govindacharya-gita-ramanuja" locator="7.14" /> On Gītā 18.66 Rāmānuja allows that a person's accumulated sins may be too many to expiate, and has God offer himself as "the Most Merciful ... demanding no price whatever for admission."<Cite source="govindacharya-gita-ramanuja" locator="18.66, pp. 564–565" /> His Vedārthasaṅgraha teaches that souls are "fundamentally alike and ... equal" in their nature as knowers, and are "wholly subservient" to God. Scholars debate how far the later Śrī Vaiṣṇava doctrine of surrender (prapatti) belongs to Rāmānuja himself.<Cite source="iep-ramanuja" locator="§5" />

#### Dvaita: dependent agents and graded souls

Madhva holds that God alone is independent and that souls are real, eternal and dependent.<Cite source="iep-madhva" locator="introduction" /> The soul is a genuine agent, like a carpenter working under a master, but its "capability of action is derived ... from the perfect Lord," who impels it "according to ... his previous actions and his effort (or aptitude)."<Cite source="subbarau-madhva-brahmasutra" locator="II.3.40–42, pp. 145–146" /> More controversially, Madhva taught a hierarchy of souls with innate virtues and faults, in which karma is "the mechanism by which a given soul is propelled" toward its destiny.<Cite source="iep-madhva" locator="§3" /> Here greatness and wickedness are partly spread across different natures rather than held in tension within one self. Liberation depends on Viṣṇu's grace, reached through devotion.<Cite source="iep-madhva" locator="§3" />

#### Nyāya and Mīmāṃsā

Nyāya gives an explicitly causal account. The "world" is the chain from misapprehension to faults, activity, birth and pain, and release comes by undoing it in reverse.<Cite source="vidyabhusana-nyaya-sutras" locator="NS 1.1.2" /> Desire and aversion are real qualities of an enduring self.<Cite source="vidyabhusana-nyaya-sutras" locator="NS 1.1.10" /> The faults are affection, aversion and delusion. Delusion is "the worst," because without it the other two do not arise.<Cite source="vidyabhusana-nyaya-sutras" locator="NS 4.1.3, 4.1.6" /> Mīmāṃsā, through Kumārila, explains why moral knowledge needs revelation. Dharma has "Injunction for its sole authority," because a good that does not yet exist cannot be reached by perception or inference.<Cite source="jha-slokavarttika" locator="Codanā-sūtra 16–18" /> It also holds that one enduring self must be both doer and enjoyer: "it is always the doer that enjoys the result of the action."<Cite source="jha-slokavarttika" locator="Ātmavāda 29" />

#### The worth of a human birth

Many texts treat human birth as uniquely valuable. Śaṅkara calls it the means to life's aims and "very hard to attain."<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="9.33, comm." /> The classical record also ranks persons by caste. Śaṅkara confines entitlement to the śāstra to "men only," a restriction his commentators apply to the three upper varṇas.<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra" locator="I.3.25, pp. 196–197" /> The Gītā promises the supreme goal to women, vaiśyas and śūdras who take refuge in God, while still speaking of "sinful birth."<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="9.32" />
