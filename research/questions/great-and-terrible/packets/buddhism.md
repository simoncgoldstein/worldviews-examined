## 1. Scope

**Strands this answer represents, and why:**

1. **Early Buddhism / Theravāda (Pali Nikāyas + Buddhaghosa's *Visuddhimagga*).** This is the
   foundation of the diagnosis that every school inherits: the three unwholesome and three wholesome
   roots, intention as karma, craving and ignorance in dependent origination, latent tendencies
   (*anusaya*), moral shame and moral dread (*hiri*, *ottappa*), the rarity of human birth, the
   brahmavihāras. Buddhaghosa is the systematic voice of the living Theravāda tradition.
2. **Abhidharma and Yogācāra (Vasubandhu).** These give the most detailed account of *why
   disorder is habitual and precedes conscious choice*: the *anuśaya* as "root of existence"
   (AKK 5.1), and in Yogācāra the storehouse consciousness with its seeds, plus a constantly
   self-centred afflicted mind (*kliṣṭa-manas*) (Triṃśikā 2–6).
3. **Madhyamaka (Nāgārjuna).** Needed for "agency without a substantial self". Emptiness is what
   makes moral change possible (MMK 24.32–33), and agent and action are mutually dependent
   (MMK 8.12).
4. **Buddhist epistemology (Dharmakīrti).** This is the sharpest argument that the root error is
   one specific cognitive mistake, the view of a real self. Self-view generates
   self-attachment, which generates craving that "conceals faults". Because that view is
   *epistemically defeasible* and the mind is luminous by nature, the faults are removable
   (PV II.208, II.211–220).
5. **Indian Mahāyāna ethics (Śāntideva).** This is the most psychologically vivid source on
   knowingly doing wrong, self-destruction, and the "same root" of joy and misery: self-cherishing
   versus other-cherishing (BCA 8.129). It also gives the bodhisattva account of human greatness.
6. **Buddha-nature (Ratnagotravibhāga) and East Asian Zen (Dōgen).** These supply the strongest
   Buddhist account of *dignity* and of *moral aspiration itself*. Without buddha-nature there
   would be no revulsion at suffering and no longing for nirvāṇa (RGV I.40–41). Dōgen's
   practice-realization reworks this so that it is not a hidden self (*Busshō*, *Bendōwa*).
7. **Tibetan Gelug (Tsongkhapa).** This is a lighter presence. It integrates the "freedoms and
   advantages" of human life, renunciation, bodhicitta and the view of dependent
   origination/emptiness into one path (*Three Principal Aspects* 2–12). It also holds that the
   ethical person is real *conventionally* (SEP §5).

**Deliberately not represented as central:** Pure Land (Shinran's account of human incapacity
would be a striking variant: see §13); tantric Vajrayāna practice; modern secular or "Buddhist
modernist" readings. Each would change emphasis, but none is needed for the core explanation.
I flag Pure Land in §10/§13 because it materially changes the "remedy" question.

## 2. Anthropology: what a human being fundamentally is (by school)

- **Early Buddhism / Theravāda.** A human being is a living stream of five aggregates (form,
  feeling, perception, volitional formations, consciousness). None of them is a self, because
  none is permanent or controllable (SN 22.59). That is not a denial that persons exist.
  Buddhaghosa: "the wise say 'doer' when there is doing", as conventional usage, while "there
  is no doer of a deed" over and above the doing (Vism XIX.19–20). A human is a *kammic agent
  defined by intention* ("It is intention that I call deeds", AN 6.63:33.3). The mind is
  "radiant" but "corrupted by visiting corruptions" (AN 1.49–52). The human realm is ranked
  among the good destinations and is singled out as surpassing even the gods in "bravery,
  mindfulness, and the spiritual life is lived here" (AN 9.21:3.3).
- **Sarvāstivāda/Sautrāntika Abhidharma (Vasubandhu, AKBh).** A causal continuum of momentary
  dharmas. "The variety of the world is born of action (karma); action is intention and what
  is produced by it" (AKK 4.1). "The root of existence is the latent tendencies" (AKK 5.1).
- **Yogācāra (Vasubandhu, Triṃśikā).** Experience is a transformation of consciousness at three
  levels:
  - the storehouse consciousness (*ālaya-vijñāna*), "containing all the seeds", which flows
    "like a torrent" (Tvk 2–4);
  - a subtle afflicted mentation that constantly takes the storehouse as a self, with
    self-view, self-delusion, self-conceit and self-love (Tvk 5–6);
  - the six ordinary consciousnesses (Tvk 8–9).

  Karmic impressions (*vāsanā*) together with "dual grasping" generate future experience
  (Tvk 19). A human being is fundamentally *a habit-structured cognitive stream with an
  in-built, pre-reflective self-misconstrual*.
- **Madhyamaka (Nāgārjuna).** Persons are empty of intrinsic nature (*svabhāva*) but
  conventionally real, as dependent designations (MMK 24.18). "Self" is taught and "not-self" is
  taught; neither is an ultimate thesis (MMK 18.6). Agent and action establish one another
  (MMK 8.12).
- **Epistemologists (Dharmakīrti).** Mind is "luminous by nature; the stains are adventitious"
  (PV II.208cd). The person is a mental continuum whose faults are rooted in a mistaken
  cognition (*satkāya-darśana*) that a correct cognition can oppose (PV II.212–215).
- **Tathāgatagarbha / Buddha-nature (Ratnagotravibhāga).** "All embodied beings are always
  buddha-garbha" (have the buddha-element within) (RGV I.28). Defilements are adventitious to a
  luminous, unchanging nature of mind (RGV I.63).
- **Dōgen (Sōtō Zen).** He reads the *Nirvāṇa Sūtra*'s "all sentient beings have buddha-nature"
  (一切衆生悉有仏性) as "entire being is buddha-nature" (悉有は仏性なり). He explicitly denies
  that buddha-nature is a self of the Senika type (先尼外道の我) (*Busshō*). Buddha-nature is
  "abundantly endowed in each person" but "not manifest without practice" (*Bendōwa*).
- **Tsongkhapa (Gelug).** A human life of "freedoms and advantages" is rare and is the basis for
  renunciation, bodhicitta and the view (*Three Principal Aspects* 2, 4). The person "is
  ultimately not present as an ethical subject, but is so, on a conventional ... level, through
  the natural working of dependent origination" (SEP *Tsongkhapa* §5).

## 3. Metaphysical context

- No creator. Vasubandhu argues against one; Buddhaghosa glosses dependent origination as
  causation by "ignorance, etc. ... not with creation by an Overlord" (Vism XVII.50). The human
  condition is explained by *conditionality* (dependent origination, SN 12.1), not by a
  personal origin or design.
- Beginningless saṃsāra. "No first beginning of ignorance is made known", yet ignorance has
  its specific conditions. It is *not* a causeless "root cause like the Primordial Essence"
  (Vism XVII.36–37, quoting AN 10.61). This matters: Buddhism does not posit an original
  catastrophe. It posits a beginningless but *conditioned and therefore dissolvable* pattern.
- Karma and rebirth across realms. Human greatness and wickedness are placed within a
  six-realm cosmology in which the human realm is a pivot point (SN 56.47–48; AN 9.21).
- Two truths (Madhyamaka/Gelug): persons, agency, merit and fault are conventionally real.
  Emptiness is not nihilism but the condition for causality and change (MMK 24.18–19;
  *Three Principal Aspects* 10–13).
- Yogācāra "appearance-only" (*vijñapti-mātra*, Tvk 17) radicalises this: the world as
  experienced is shaped by karmic seeds. Its implication here is that much of human disorder
  is *perceptual and cognitive construction*, not merely bad behaviour.
- Tathāgatagarbha / Dōgen: a strand in which the unconditioned nature of mind
  (RGV I.62–63) or "entire being" (Dōgen) is the ground against which defilement is
  adventitious.

## 4. Why humans possess remarkable capacities

1. **Mind is creative and more varied than its products.** "That painting was devised by the
   mind, but the mind is even more diverse than that" (SN 22.100:2.3). The same discourse
   immediately adds that this mind "has been corrupted by greed, hate, and delusion" and that
   "sentient beings are corrupted because the mind is corrupted ... purified because the mind is
   purified" (2.5–2.7). The canon thus locates artistic creativity and corruption in one
   faculty.
2. **Intention is real causal power.** Karma *is* intention (AN 6.63:33.3; AKK 4.1;
   MMK 17.2–3). "Intention is the leader of things" (Dhp 1–2). So humans are *makers*. Their
   choices shape their future existences and the world's "variety" (AKK 4.1).
3. **Wholesome roots are native possibilities, not imports.** The skilful roots are non-greed,
   non-hate (love) and non-delusion (understanding) (AN 3.69:9.3; MN 9.7). Buddhaghosa defines
   them positively: non-greed is non-adherence "like a water drop on a lotus leaf"; non-hate is
   "like a gentle friend"; non-delusion is "penetrating [things] according to their individual
   essences ... like a lamp" (Vism XIV.143).
4. **Conscience is part of human moral equipment.** *Hiri* and *ottappa* are "two bright things
   [that] protect the world". Without them human society would collapse into indiscriminate
   promiscuity "like goats and sheep" (AN 2.9). Buddhaghosa: conscience has "the characteristic
   of disgust at evil", shame "of dread of it", and their proximate causes are self-respect and
   respect for others (Vism XIV.142).
5. **Boundless love and compassion are cultivable.** The four brahmavihāras (Vism IX). Śāntideva:
   bodhicitta, "wanting for others what they do not wish even for themselves", is "an
   extraordinary and precious state of mind" (BCA 1.25, Pearcey). Dharmakīrti argues that
   compassion, being a mental quality on a stable mental basis, can be increased limitlessly by
   habituation (*abhyāsa*) (PV II.120ff; II.131).
6. **The human birth is uniquely placed.** It is rare (blind turtle, SN 56.47–48; Dhp 182;
   BCA 4.20; *Three Principal Aspects* 4). Humans surpass gods in "bravery, mindfulness, and the
   spiritual life is lived here" (AN 9.21:3.3). "This free and well-favoured human form" is the
   means "to accomplish what is meaningful for a person" (*puruṣārtha-sādhanī*, BCA 1.4).
7. **Buddha-nature / luminous mind.** In Mahāyāna strands, the deepest reason for human
   greatness is that awakening is the mind's own nature (RGV I.28; PV II.208; AN 1.49–52 in a
   more modest early form). In *aspiration* itself, RGV I.40–41 sees evidence of this nature:
   "If the buddha-element did not exist, there would be no revulsion at suffering, nor desire,
   seeking, or aspiration for nirvāṇa."

## 5. Why humans behave destructively or wrongly

**Core mechanism (shared across schools):** ignorance → craving/aversion → grasping → intentional
action → suffering, in dependent origination (SN 12.1, 12.2:14.2). Unwholesome action
specifically arises from three roots:

- **Greed (lobha), hate (dosa), delusion (moha).** "When a greedy person, overcome by greed,
  causes another to suffer under a false pretext—by execution or imprisonment or confiscation or
  condemnation or banishment—thinking 'I'm powerful, I want power', that too is unskillful"
  (AN 3.69:2.3). This is a direct canonical account of *domination*. Kālāma Sutta: greed, hate
  and delusion arise "for harm", and one overcome by them kills, steals, lies "and encourages
  others to do the same" (AN 3.65).
- **Craving (taṇhā)** for sense pleasure, for existence, and for non-existence is the origin of
  suffering (SN 56.11:4.4–4.5). The third, craving for non-existence, is relevant to
  *self-destruction*. Sensual craving drives war and family violence: "for the sake of sensual
  pleasures kings fight with kings ... mother fights with her child ... they attack each other
  with fists, stones, rods, and swords" (MN 13.11).
- **Origin of civilisation's vices (DN 27, the Aggañña Sutta).** In this origin narrative,
  craving arises at the first taste of the earth's nectar (DN 27.11). Theft, blame, lying and
  violence ("the taking up of rods") follow hoarding (DN 27.19). Kingship/government is then
  instituted *by agreement* to punish wrongdoers (DN 27.20–21). Read this way, civilisation is
  both a product of moral decline and a remedy for it. Genre and seriousness are contested:
  see §13.
- **Mental proliferation (papañca).** Perception feeds conceptual proliferation that "besets" a
  person (MN 18.16). Habitual thought shapes inclination: "Whatever a mendicant frequently
  thinks about and considers becomes their heart's inclination" (MN 19.6).
- **Latent tendencies (anusaya).** These are dispositions that "lie latent" even in an infant
  with no concept of a self or of sensuality: "Yet the underlying tendency to substantialist
  view still lies within them" (MN 64.3). Buddhaghosa lists seven (sensual greed, resentment,
  conceit, views, doubt, greed for becoming, ignorance), "called inherent tendencies since they
  inhere as cause for the arising of greed for sense desires, etc., again and again"
  (Vism XXII.60). Vasubandhu: they are "the root of existence" (AKK 5.1), "subtle" and
  "binding" (AKK 5.39). Yogācāra relocates this into seeds in the storehouse consciousness
  and the afflicted mentation's constant self-love (Tvk 2, 6, 19).
- **The self-view as root (Dharmakīrti).** "One who sees a self has an enduring attachment to
  'I'; from attachment he thirsts for pleasures; thirst conceals faults [in its objects];
  seeing only virtues ... he appropriates their means as 'mine'". Then: "When there is a self,
  there is the notion of an other; from the division of self and other come possessiveness
  and hatred; bound up with these two, all faults arise" (PV II.217–220, Miyasaka numbering;
  my paraphrase from the Sanskrit). "All stains are rooted in it, and it is the view of a real
  personal self" (PV II.213ab). Śāntideva: "All the injury, fear and pain in this world arise
  from grasping at a self" (BCA 8.134).
- **Self-destruction.** Śāntideva: "Although seeking to avoid pain, they run headlong into
  suffering. They long for happiness, but foolishly destroy it, as if it were their enemy"
  (BCA 1.28, Pearcey). Under the sway of the defilements, people harm themselves with
  carelessness, starvation, hanging, leaping from cliffs and poison. "If ... people will even
  kill their treasured selves, how can they be expected not to cause harm to the bodies of
  other living beings?" (BCA 6.35–37, paraphrased from Batchelor/Sanskrit). Buddhaghosa: a cow
  crazed by torment drinks hot water "although it gives no satisfaction and does her harm". The
  person in the grip of ignorance does kamma the same way (Vism XVII.39).

**Knowingly acting against one's judgement.** This is treated directly, and in a way that
distinguishes levels of knowing:

1. *Conceptual understanding does not uproot latent tendencies.* Mahānāma: "For a long time,
   sir, I have understood your teaching ... 'Greed, hate, and delusion are corruptions of the
   mind.' Despite understanding this, sometimes my mind is occupied by thoughts of greed, hate,
   and delusion." The Buddha replies that a quality remains unabandoned. "Even though a noble
   disciple has clearly seen this with right wisdom, as long as they do not achieve the rapture
   and bliss that are apart from sensual pleasures ... they can return to sensual pleasures"
   (MN 14.2–4). The diagnosis: knowledge that is not yet *experiential*, and not backed by a
   superior, non-sensual happiness, is motivationally weak.
2. *Defilements "overcome" (pariyādinna) the mind.* This is the standard formula "overcome by
   greed" (AN 3.65; AN 3.69).
3. *Śāntideva's phenomenology of akrasia.* "If, having understood this, I still foolishly
   continue to be slothful..." (BCA 4.24). "As though I were hypnotised by a spell ... Even I do
   not know what is causing me confusion; what is there dwelling inside me?" (BCA 4.27). "Enemies
   such as hatred and craving have neither arms nor legs, are neither courageous nor wise; how
   have I been used like a slave by them?" (BCA 4.28). Batchelor's renderings are cited only
   for the reading; the cited edition is Crosby and Skilton.
4. *Dharmakīrti: craving conceals faults* (*tṛṣṇā doṣāṃs tiraskurute*, PV II.218 line in the
   GRETIL e-text). The agent does not simply override clear knowledge. Desire actively
   occludes the object's faults at the moment of choice.
5. *Dōgen: saying versus practising.* In *Shoaku makusa*, Bai Juyi objects that "do no evil,
   practise good" is something a three-year-old could say. Niaoke replies: "Though a
   three-year-old can say it, an eighty-year-old cannot practise it" (三歳孩兒縦道得、八十老翁行不得).
   Dōgen adds that "not doing evil" is not first produced by the ordinary person's contrived
   effort (凡夫のはじめて造作して). Rather, as the teaching is heard and practised, "evils come
   to be not done", and the power of practice is actualised (修行力たちまち現成す).

## 6. Do greatness and wickedness share a common explanation?

**Yes. This is the tradition's distinctive answer.** Several converging formulations:

- **One mind, two conditions.** The same mind that devised paintings is corrupted or purified
  (SN 22.100:2.3–2.7). AN 1.49–52: the same "radiant" mind is either "corrupted by" or "freed
  from" visiting corruptions.
- **One faculty, two directions.** Intention (*cetanā*) is the common factor in both skilful and
  unskilful action (AN 6.63; Dhp 1–2, which differ only by "corrupt" vs "pure" intent). The
  roots come in matched pairs: greed / non-greed, hate / love, delusion / understanding
  (AN 3.69; MN 9).
- **The "near enemy" doctrine.** Buddhaghosa: loving-kindness "has greed as its near enemy,
  since both share in seeing virtues". Compassion's near enemy is worldly grief, gladness's is
  worldly joy, equanimity's is the "equanimity of unknowing" (Vism IX.98–101). The
  sub-commentary: greed is "like an enemy masquerading as a friend" (Vism IX.98 n.14, citing
  Vism-mhṭ 309). On this view, human love and human possessiveness are close neighbours that
  share a structure (attention to the excellent) and differ in grasping.
- **One question: whose happiness?** "Whatever joy there is in this world all comes from
  desiring others to be happy, and whatever suffering there is in this world all comes from
  desiring myself to be happy" (BCA 8.129, Batchelor; cited edition Crosby and Skilton). Both
  greatness and misery spring from the universal wish for happiness (BCA 8.95–96). They differ
  by whether that wish is self-enclosed or extended.
- **Desire (chanda) vs craving (taṇhā).** The path *uses* desire. Right effort means one
  "generates enthusiasm" (*chandaṃ janeti*) for abandoning the unwholesome and developing the
  wholesome (SN 45.8:8.2–8.5). Ānanda's reply to Uṇṇābha: the desire for awakening fades when
  awakening is reached, as the desire to go to a park fades on arriving (SN 51.15:4–5). So
  human aspiration is not the enemy. Its *craving*-form is.
- **Buddha-nature.** In this strand even moral aspiration and revulsion at suffering are
  expressions of the luminous nature (RGV I.40–41). Wickedness is adventitious obscuration of
  the same nature (RGV I.63; BCA 6.40).

**Nuance:** not every school puts the "good" side on the same footing. In early/Theravāda
sources, the wholesome roots and the defilements are both conditioned mental factors. The
radiance of mind (AN 1.49) is interpreted modestly by the commentaries, as *bhavaṅga* or
naturally clear consciousness, not a substantive "true self". In tathāgatagarbha strands the
good side is ontologically *prior* and the bad side adventitious. See §10.

## 7. Specific topics

**Rationality.** Buddhism prizes discernment (*paññā*) and testable reasoning. The Kālāma
discourse tells people to judge teachings by whether they lead to harm or welfare when
undertaken, as wise people would assess them (AN 3.65:4.3). In Dharmakīrti, reason
(*pramāṇa*) is a soteriological instrument: correct cognition opposes the self-view
(PV II.213–215). SEP *Dharmakīrti* §4 confirms that PV ch. 2 uses logical argument to establish
the four truths, compassion, Buddha-nature and no-self. Rationality is not itself the
problem. *Distorted* cognition is, and reason is part of the cure.

**Moral knowledge.**

- *Conventional moral knowledge:* the ten wholesome and unwholesome courses of action
  (MN 9.4–9.6; Vism XXII.62).
- *Experiential/liberating knowledge:* seeing the four truths. Ignorance is "not knowing about
  suffering, its origin, its cessation and the practice" (SN 12.2:14.2). Buddhaghosa:
  ignorance "has the characteristic of unknowing. Its function is to confuse. It is manifested
  as concealing" (Vism XVII.51).

**Conscience.** *Hiri* (moral shame/conscience: self-respecting recoil from evil) and *ottappa*
(moral dread/prudence: other-regarding fear of wrongdoing) are the "guardians of the world"
(AN 2.9; Vism XIV.142). Yogācāra lists them among the wholesome mental factors (*hrī*,
*apatrapā*, Tvk 10–11), and their absence (*āhrīkya*, *anapatrapā*) among the secondary
defilements (Tvk 13). Conscience is therefore real, native and fragile, not merely
socialisation.

**Evil / defilement / ignorance.** Evil is unskilful action rooted in greed, hate and delusion.
It is not a substance or a cosmic principle. Ignorance is "not causeless" (Vism XVII.36).
Madhyamaka: "desire, hatred and delusion are said to arise from conceptualisation (*saṃkalpa*),
dependent on the errors about the attractive and unattractive" (MMK 23.1). Dōgen goes further:
evils have no fixed nature (諸悪にさだまれる調度なき) and "good and evil are time"
(善悪は時なり) (*Shoaku makusa*). Mahāyāna texts add a teaching about the *agent*: wrongdoers
are themselves driven by conditions, so anger at them is misplaced. One should rather be
"angry with their hatred" (BCA 6.22–33, 6.41). This is not exculpation. Restraint remains
"conventionally" appropriate (BCA 6.32).

**Agency without a substantial self.**

- *Theravāda:* there is no doer over and above doing, but "doer" is legitimate conventional
  usage, and kamma and result continue causally (Vism XIX.19–20). Responsibility attaches to
  the *continuity* of a stream.
- *Madhyamaka:* "the agent depends on the action and the action on the agent" (MMK 8.12).
  Against the objection that emptiness abolishes ethics, Nāgārjuna argues the reverse. If
  things had intrinsic nature, "no one would ever do what is right or wrong" and no one could
  progress to awakening (MMK 24.32–33). Emptiness is what *permits* moral change.
- *Gelug:* the ethical subject is conventionally established through dependent origination
  (SEP *Tsongkhapa* §5).
- *Śāntideva* turns no-self into an ethical argument. "There is no (real) owner of suffering",
  so there is no principled ground for preferring my suffering's relief to yours
  (BCA 8.101–103).

**Desire / craving.** *Taṇhā* (craving: sensual, for existence, for non-existence; SN 56.11) must
be abandoned. *Chanda* (wholesome desire/enthusiasm) is cultivated (SN 45.8:8.2; SN 51.15).
Dharmakīrti's chain locates craving downstream of self-attachment (PV II.217–218). The
Abhidharma calls *chanda* an ethically variable mental factor; Yogācāra lists it among the
"determinate" factors (Tvk 10).

## 8. Can the human condition be remedied?

**Yes, completely, according to every strand.** This is the tradition's central claim.

- **Four Noble Truths.** Craving is to be abandoned, cessation realised, the path developed
  (SN 56.11:6–8). For one with the wholesome roots perfected, the unwholesome are "cut off at
  the root, made like a palm stump" (AN 3.69:14.1, 15.9).
- **Path structure.** Ethics, meditation and wisdom (Noble Eightfold Path, SN 45.8). Right
  effort is the deliberate redirection of desire (8.2–8.5). Meditative joy supplies the
  "superior pleasure" that makes renunciation stable (MN 14.4; SN 45.8:10). Buddhaghosa's
  *Visuddhimagga* is an entire map of this purification.
- **Removing latent tendencies.** Only path-knowledge eradicates them. "The Arahant has none"
  (Ñāṇamoli's translator's note in Vism ch. XVII; cf. XXII.60ff). Nāgārjuna: "ignorance
  ceases through cultivation of this very knowledge" (MMK 26.11). Vasubandhu: the afflicted
  mentation ceases at arhatship (Tvk 5, 7).
- **Dharmakīrti's argument for possibility.** Mind is luminous by nature and the stains
  adventitious (PV II.208). Faults depend on a false cognition, and a true cognition has
  "the strength of its support" while errors do not. So, once habituated, the antidote cannot
  be overturned (PV II.210–211, 215–216; read in Sanskrit). Liberation is argued for, not
  merely promised. These verse locators are within a passage I read, but finer
  verse-to-claim alignment should be confirmed against Jackson 1993 or Franco 1997.
- **Bodhisattva path (Mahāyāna).** The bodhisattva path transforms the very self-concern that
  produces misery into other-concern (BCA 8.90–131). Bodhicitta "takes this ordinary, impure
  human form and makes of it a buddha's priceless body" (BCA 1.10, Pearcey). It "annihilates
  terrible misdeeds" (BCA 1.13–14).
- **Tsongkhapa's integration.** Renunciation alone does not suffice; it needs bodhicitta.
  Bodhicitta alone does not cut saṃsāra's root; it needs the wisdom that sees dependent
  origination and emptiness together (*Three Principal Aspects* 6, 9, 11–12).
- **Dōgen.** Practice and realisation are one (修証これ一等). Even the beginner's practice is
  "the whole of original realisation" (初心の弁道すなはち本証の全体なり), so practice is not
  a means to a distant end (*Bendōwa*).
- **Nirvāṇa.** The extinguishing of greed, hate and delusion "in this very life"
  (AN 3.69:14.3). Schools differ on how to characterise it (Theravāda: unconditioned element;
  Madhyamaka: not other than saṃsāra's emptiness, MMK 25.19–20, not separately verified here;
  Zen: realised in practice). The site should not flatten this.

## 9. Dignity: inherent or derived?

The answer depends on the school, so do not flatten it:

- **Derived from opportunity (early/Theravāda, Gelug lamrim).** The human birth is valuable
  *because* it is rare and uniquely suited to awakening: SN 56.47–48 (blind turtle), SN 56.48:2.4
  ("now you have been reborn as a human being ... a Realised One has arisen"), AN 9.21, BCA 1.4
  / 4.20–23, *Three Principal Aspects* 4. Here dignity is *occasion-based*, a precious,
  squanderable opportunity. The moral corollary is urgency. It is not an essence-based
  inviolability. Universal compassion rests not on human dignity as such but on the shared
  wish of all beings, animals included, to be happy and not to suffer (BCA 8.90–96; Snp 1.8
  "May all beings be happy"; Vism IX).
- **Inherent (Tathāgatagarbha, East Asian, much Tibetan).** All beings "are always
  buddha-garbha" (RGV I.28). Faults are adventitious, beings are "by nature good" (*prakṛti-
  peśalāḥ*, BCA 6.40, in a conditional argument), and the mind's nature is luminous
  (PV II.208; RGV I.63). Dignity is grounded in a nature that cannot be lost.
- **Dōgen's reworking.** Buddha-nature is "entire being", not a possession or inner self
  (*Busshō*). Dignity is realised in practice rather than simply possessed (*Bendōwa*).
- **Note:** in both strands, dignity is not *specifically human*. It extends to all sentient
  beings, so the human/animal difference is one of *opportunity*, not of kind of worth.

## 10. Internal school differences that materially change the answer

1. **Is the good side primary?** Theravāda/Abhidharma treat wholesome and unwholesome roots
   alike as conditioned factors. The "radiant mind" passage (AN 1.49–52) is commentarially
   interpreted modestly (as *bhavaṅga*, the life-continuum mind) and not as a hidden pure
   essence (SEP *Mind in Indian Buddhist Philosophy* §5.2 on life-continuum mind; I did not
   read the Pali commentary itself). Tathāgatagarbha and Zen strands make luminosity or
   buddha-nature ontologically basic. This changes whether "greatness" is the *true* human
   nature (Mahāyāna) or one cultivable set of possibilities (Theravāda).
2. **Where disorder lives.** For Abhidharma, it lies in the latent tendencies of a dharma
   stream. For Yogācāra, it lies in a storehouse of seeds plus an *innate*, *continuous*
   self-misconstruing mentation (Tvk 5–6). Yogācāra therefore gives the strongest account of
   disorder that precedes and survives conscious choice.
3. **What ignorance is.** Early texts define it as not knowing the four truths (SN 12.2).
   Dharmakīrti and later Indo-Tibetan traditions identify it with the *view of a real self*
   (PV II.213). For Tsongkhapa it is grasping at intrinsic existence, removable only by the view
   of emptiness (*Three Principal Aspects* 9–12; SEP §3).
4. **Gradual vs. sudden / practice-as-realisation.** Gradualist path maps (Buddhaghosa,
   Tsongkhapa) differ from Dōgen's insistence that practice *is* realisation (*Bendōwa*). This
   changes how "knowing the good but not doing it" is diagnosed. For the gradualists it is
   insufficient cultivation; for Dōgen, the knowing/doing split itself reflects a dualism that
   practice dissolves (*Shoaku makusa*).
5. **Self-power vs other-power (Pure Land, not represented).** Shinran-style Pure Land would
   answer "can humans remedy themselves?" largely *no*: reliance on Amida's vow. That would
   change §8. Flag it if the site wants a fuller East Asian picture.
6. **Icchantika question.** Some Mahāyāna texts speak of beings "never to attain nirvāṇa"
   (*icchantika*). RGV's commentary reinterprets this as a statement meant to turn people from
   hostility to Mahāyāna, implying eventual liberation "after a time" (RGV vyākhyā on I.41,
   Johnston p. 37). The universality of the remedy is thus contested historically.

## 11. What these traditions genuinely explain well

1. **A single, economical explanation of the paradox.** One mind, one faculty of intention, and
   paired roots explain both the cathedral and the war. No second principle is needed
   (SN 22.100; AN 3.69; AN 6.63).
2. **Knowingly doing wrong.** The tradition offers a fine-grained account. Conceptual knowledge
   can coexist with untouched latent tendencies (MN 14; MN 64). Craving conceals the faults of
   its object (PV II.218). The akratic agent experiences defilements as an alien "enemy" that
   is nonetheless internal (BCA 4.27–28). Saying and doing come apart (*Shoaku makusa*). It also
   prescribes something specific: a superior, non-sensual happiness plus experiential insight
   (MN 14.4).
3. **Domination and self-destruction as one disorder.** Power-lust (AN 3.69), war over
   pleasures (MN 13) and suicide/self-harm (BCA 6.35–37) are all the same craving
   misapprehending where happiness lies (BCA 1.28; Vism XVII.39).
4. **Universality prior to choice.** Latent tendencies in infants (MN 64.3) explain why disorder
   appears everywhere without anyone choosing it. Yogācāra explains why self-centredness is
   pre-reflective (Tvk 6).
5. **Love and its counterfeits.** The near-enemy doctrine explains how possessiveness can
   masquerade as love (Vism IX.98–101). This is a psychological observation of real
   diagnostic power.
6. **Conscience without a lawgiver.** *Hiri/ottappa* explain moral emotion as native and
   socially protective (AN 2.9; Vism XIV.142).
7. **A real, practicable hope.** The analysis is tied to a training that adherents report and
   observers can partly see. The pattern is held to be conditioned and therefore dissolvable
   (Vism XVII.36; MMK 24.32–33). Compassion toward wrongdoers sits alongside firm restraint
   (BCA 6.33, 6.41).

## 12. Strongest representative thinkers for this question

- **Buddhaghosa.** He systematises roots, conscience, latent tendencies, ignorance, near
  enemies and conventional agency in one map (Vism IX, XIV, XVII, XIX, XXII).
- **Śāntideva.** He gives the most vivid first-person treatment of akrasia, self-destruction and
  the self-vs-other source of joy and misery (BCA 1, 4, 6, 8).
- **Vasubandhu.** Latent tendencies as "root of existence" (AKK 5.1), karma as intention
  (AKK 4.1), and Yogācāra's innate self-love (Tvk 6). He is the best account of *habitual*
  disorder.
- **Dharmakīrti.** He gives the tightest argument that one cognitive error (self-view) grounds
  all faults, and that luminous mind makes their removal possible (PV II.208–220).
- **Nāgārjuna.** Agency without a substantial self; emptiness as the condition of moral change
  (MMK 8, 17, 18, 24, 26).
- **Dōgen.** On knowing vs. doing, on practice-realisation, and on non-substantialist
  buddha-nature (*Shoaku makusa*, *Bendōwa*, *Busshō*).
- **Tsongkhapa** (secondary here). He integrates human opportunity, renunciation, compassion
  and wisdom (*Three Principal Aspects*) and holds the ethical person to be conventionally real
  (SEP §5).

## 13. Uncertainties and contested interpretations

1. **"Radiant mind" (AN 1.49–52).** Is it an innate purity (later Mahāyāna) or a modest claim
   about mind's capacity? Theravāda commentary reads it modestly. Scholarship is divided. Do
   not present it as early Buddhist "Buddha-nature".
2. **Tathāgatagarbha and "self".** Some Mahāyāna texts use self-language (e.g. the
   *ātma-pāramitā* in RGV's commentary, vyākhyā at Johnston p. 30). Others (Dōgen, Gelug
   Madhyamaka) insist buddha-nature is not a self. Tibetan traditions disagree on whether
   buddha-nature is emptiness alone (Gelug) or luminous awareness empty of other (Jonang
   *gzhan stong*). Do not flatten this.
3. **DN 27 (Aggañña).** Many scholars (Gombrich; Collins 1993) read it as partly satirical of
   Brahmanical origin myths. Use it as a canonical *narrative of civilisation with craving and
   agreement*, not as literal Buddhist history. I did not verify the Collins article directly.
4. **Dharmakīrti PV II verse numbering.** It differs between editions: Miyasaka in GRETIL vs
   Pandeya, where "prabhāsvaram idaṃ cittam" is reported as 210cd. Half-verse alignment in the
   GRETIL e-text around II.216–220 is not fully transparent to me. Locators II.208, II.213 and
   II.217–220 follow the e-text's own labels. A specialist check against Miyasaka or a
   published translation (Jackson 1993; Eltschinger) is recommended. Eltschinger's JIABS
   articles were behind an anti-bot wall I did not bypass.
5. **RGV verse numbering.** I used Johnston's edition via GRETIL. Takasaki's 1966 translation and
   Tibetan-based translations may number some verses differently. I.28 vs I.27 needs checking
   before citation in a reviewed answer.
6. **Do the defilements "overpower" understanding, or is the understanding defective?** Pali
   (MN 14) and Dharmakīrti (craving conceals faults) lean towards the latter: the knowing was
   not yet full knowing. Śāntideva's language ("used like a slave", BCA 4.28) can sound like
   the former. Both are within the tradition, and the answer should present both.
7. **Pure Land omission.** See §10.5.
8. **"Inherent dignity".** Applying modern dignity-language to Buddhist texts is an
   interpretive move. The texts speak of precious opportunity, buddha-nature and
   universal compassion, not of "dignity" (*dignitas*). The answer should mark this.
9. **MMK 25.19–20** (no difference between saṃsāra and nirvāṇa) was not separately re-read for
   this packet. It is mentioned in §8 but is not proposed for citation.

## 16. Thinker profile notes

**Buddhaghosa** (`buddhaghosa`)
- *Dates/setting:* fl. first half of 5th c. CE. An Indian monk who came to Sri Lanka in the
  reign of Mahānāma (who was reigning in 428) and worked at the Mahāvihāra, Anurādhapura.
  He rendered the Sinhalese commentaries into Pali.
- *School:* Theravāda (Mahāvihāra).
- *Why relevant:* he gives the most systematic Theravāda map of the mental factors behind good
  and evil, and of their purification.
- *Key ideas:* virtue–concentration–understanding as the path of purification; near and far
  enemies of the divine abidings; conscience and shame as guardians of the world;
  dependent origination without a creator; no doer apart from kamma and result; seven inherent
  tendencies.
- *Works:* *Visuddhimagga*; commentaries on the Nikāyas.
- *Reference used:* Ñāṇamoli's Introduction to *The Path of Purification* (BPS 2010 PDF, pp.
  xxxiii–xxxvi; dating discussion at the "Mahānāma ... 428" passage).

**Nāgārjuna** (`nagarjuna`)
- *Dates:* c. 150–250 CE, probably southern India (SEP *Nāgārjuna*: "between 150 and 250 CE").
- *School:* founder of Madhyamaka.
- *Why relevant:* he shows how moral agency and transformation are possible without a
  substantial self, and argues that emptiness makes ethics *possible*.
- *Key ideas:* emptiness as dependent origination (MMK 24.18); mutual dependence of agent and
  action (8.12); karma as intention (17.2–3); defilements from conceptual proliferation
  (18.5; 23.1); two truths.
- *Works:* *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā*; *Ratnāvalī*; *Vigrahavyāvartanī*.
- *Reference used:* https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/nagarjuna/

**Vasubandhu** (`vasubandhu`)
- *Dates:* 4th–5th c. CE (SEP: "4th century", dating disputed; one-vs-two-Vasubandhus
  question). Gandhāra/Kashmir/Ayodhyā.
- *School:* Sarvāstivāda–Sautrāntika Abhidharma, later Yogācāra.
- *Why relevant:* the anuśaya doctrine and Yogācāra's storehouse and afflicted mind give the
  deepest account of pre-reflective, habitual disorder.
- *Key ideas:* karma as intention (AKK 4.1); latent tendencies as root of existence (AKK 5.1);
  seeds and karmic impressions; the storehouse consciousness; afflicted mentation with innate
  self-love (Tvk 6); appearance-only and the three natures.
- *Works:* *Abhidharmakośa(bhāṣya)*; *Viṃśikā*; *Triṃśikā*; *Trisvabhāvanirdeśa*.
- *Reference used:* https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/vasubandhu/

**Dharmakīrti** (`dharmakirti`)
- *Dates:* c. 600–660 CE (Frauwallner), possibly half a century earlier (SEP §1). India
  (Nālandā tradition).
- *School:* Buddhist epistemology (Yogācāra–Sautrāntika).
- *Why relevant:* he argues that all faults stem from one defeasible cognition (self-view)
  and that liberation is rationally possible because the mind's nature is luminous.
- *Key ideas:* pramāṇa (valid cognition); self-view as root of attachment and hatred
  (PV II.213, 217–220); craving conceals faults; habituation (*abhyāsa*) of compassion;
  luminous mind and adventitious stains (II.208).
- *Works:* *Pramāṇavārttika*; *Pramāṇaviniścaya*; *Nyāyabindu*; *Hetubindu*.
- *Reference used:* https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/dharmakiirti/

**Śāntideva** (`shantideva`)
- *Dates:* late 7th to mid-8th c. CE. Monk at Nālandā (SEP *Śāntideva*).
- *School:* Indian Mahāyāna, Madhyamaka.
- *Why relevant:* he gives the most penetrating Buddhist first-person account of akrasia,
  self-destruction and the self-cherishing root of misery. His bodhisattva ideal is the
  Mahāyāna account of human greatness.
- *Key ideas:* bodhicitta; the precious human form; afflictions as internal enemies;
  patience grounded in conditionality; equalising and exchanging self and other; "all
  suffering from wanting oneself to be happy" (8.129).
- *Works:* *Bodhicaryāvatāra*; *Śikṣāsamuccaya*.
- *Reference used:* https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/shantideva/

**Tsongkhapa** (`tsongkhapa`)
- *Dates:* 1357–1419. Amdo and Central Tibet.
- *School:* founder of Gelug; Prāsaṅgika Madhyamaka.
- *Why relevant:* he integrates the value of human life, renunciation, compassion and the view
  of emptiness. The ethical person is conventionally real.
- *Key ideas:* stages of the path (lamrim); freedoms and advantages; three principal aspects
  (renunciation, bodhicitta, correct view); identifying the object of negation;
  compatibility of emptiness and dependent origination.
- *Works:* *Lam rim chen mo* (1402); *Legs bshad snying po* (1407); *Rigs pa'i rgya mtsho*
  (1408); *Dgongs pa rab gsal* (1418); *Lam gyi gtso bo rnam gsum*.
- *Reference used:* https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/tsongkhapa/

**Dōgen** (`dogen`)
- *Dates:* 1200–1253, Japan. Trained in Song China under Rujing; founded Eiheiji.
- *School:* Sōtō Zen.
- *Why relevant:* he gives a non-substantialist buddha-nature, the oneness of practice and
  realisation, and a distinctive reading of the gap between knowing and doing the good.
- *Key ideas:* practice-realisation (*shushō ittō*); "entire being is buddha-nature";
  "to study the self is to forget the self"; not-doing evil as the power of practice, not
  contrived will; just sitting (*shikantaza*); being-time.
- *Works:* *Shōbōgenzō* (Genjōkōan, Busshō, Shoaku makusa, Uji); *Bendōwa*; *Eihei kōroku*.
- *Reference used:* https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/japanese-zen/ (dates "1200–54",
  shikantaza, practice-realisation). Registry gives 1253, which is correct for the
  Western-calendar death.

## The view

Buddhist traditions do not explain the human paradox by two natures, one noble and one fallen,
but by one mind under two conditions. The same mind that devised paintings has "for a long time
... been corrupted by greed, hate, and delusion"; beings are corrupted when the mind is
corrupted and purified when it is purified.<Cite source="samyutta-nikaya-sujato" locator="SN 22.100" />
Human action is intention,<Cite source="anguttara-nikaya-sujato" locator="AN 6.63" /> and
intentions grow from roots. Greed, hatred and delusion produce killing, deceit and the
oppression of those who think "I'm powerful, I want power"; their opposites (contentment, love
and understanding) produce the reverse.<Cite source="anguttara-nikaya-sujato" locator="AN 3.69" />
Beneath the roots lies ignorance. Above all, ignorance is the misreading of an impermanent,
dependently arisen process as a fixed self to be fed and defended: "when there is a self,
there is an other", and from that division come possessiveness and hatred.<Cite source="dharmakirti-pramanavarttika" locator="II.219" />
Because this is a habit rather than an essence, the corruptions are "visiting",<Cite source="anguttara-nikaya-sujato" locator="AN 1.49–52" />
and Mahāyāna texts describe beings as good by nature with faults that are adventitious.<Cite source="shantideva-bodhicaryavatara" locator="6.40" />
Human birth is rare and uniquely suited to awakening.<Cite source="samyutta-nikaya-sujato" locator="SN 56.48" />
Knowing the good does not by itself cure us. Latent tendencies outlast understanding until
deeper insight and a better joy uproot them.<Cite source="majjhima-nikaya-sujato" locator="MN 14.2–4" />

## What this explains well

Buddhist analysis gives a single, economical account of the whole range. The faculty that loves
is the faculty that grasps, which is why Buddhaghosa calls greed the "near enemy" of
loving-kindness.<Cite source="buddhaghosa-path-of-purification" locator="IX.98" /> It describes
knowingly doing wrong with real precision. Someone can understand that greed corrupts and still
be overtaken by it.<Cite source="majjhima-nikaya-sujato" locator="MN 14.2–4" /> Śāntideva asks
how enemies "without arms or legs" have made him their slave.<Cite source="shantideva-bodhicaryavatara" locator="4.28" />
It connects domination and self-destruction as one error about where happiness lies: people
"long for happiness, but ... destroy it, as if it were their enemy".<Cite source="shantideva-bodhicaryavatara" locator="1.28" />
Latent tendencies present even in infants explain why the disorder is universal before anyone
chooses it.<Cite source="majjhima-nikaya-sujato" locator="MN 64.3" /> Moral shame and moral
dread, "two bright things [that] protect the world", give conscience a native place.<Cite source="anguttara-nikaya-sujato" locator="AN 2.9" />

## Deep dive

#### Roots, intention and latent tendencies

The early discourses analyse conduct by its roots. Killing, stealing, sexual misconduct, false
or harsh speech, covetousness, ill will and wrong view are "unskillful", and their root is
greed, hate and delusion. Their opposites have the roots contentment, love and
understanding.<Cite source="majjhima-nikaya-sujato" locator="MN 9.4–9.7" /> Buddhaghosa gives
the wholesome roots positive content. Non-greed is like "a water drop on a lotus leaf",
non-hate like "a gentle friend", and non-delusion illuminates "like a lamp".<Cite source="buddhaghosa-path-of-purification" locator="XIV.143" />
Craving, for sense pleasure, for existence and for non-existence, is the origin of suffering.<Cite source="samyutta-nikaya-sujato" locator="SN 56.11" />
For its sake "kings fight with kings" and parents with children.<Cite source="majjhima-nikaya-sujato" locator="MN 13.11" />
Ignorance stands at the head of dependent origination,<Cite source="samyutta-nikaya-sujato" locator="SN 12.1" />
but it is not a causeless first principle. It has "no first beginning", yet it has its own
conditions.<Cite source="buddhaghosa-path-of-purification" locator="XVII.36–37" /> Disorder is
also dispositional. Seven "inherent tendencies" (sensual greed, resentment, conceit, views,
doubt, greed for becoming, ignorance) "inhere as cause for the arising" of defilement
"again and again".<Cite source="buddhaghosa-path-of-purification" locator="XXII.60" />
Vasubandhu calls them "the root of existence".<Cite source="vasubandhu-abhidharmakosa" locator="5.1" />
Yogācāra deepens the point. Beneath ordinary awareness, a subtle mentation constantly
construes the mind-stream as a self and is accompanied by self-view, self-delusion,
self-conceit and self-love.<Cite source="vasubandhu-trimsika" locator="6" />

#### Knowing the good and doing otherwise

The canon faces akrasia directly. Mahānāma has long understood that greed, hate and delusion
corrupt the mind, yet they still occupy it. The Buddha's diagnosis is that something remains
unabandoned. Even with right understanding, one who has not found a happiness "apart from
sensual pleasures" can return to them.<Cite source="majjhima-nikaya-sujato" locator="MN 14.2–4" />
Dharmakīrti adds a cognitive mechanism. Self-attachment breeds craving, and craving "conceals
faults" in its objects, so the agent sees only their attractions.<Cite source="dharmakirti-pramanavarttika" locator="II.217–218" />
Buddhaghosa's image is a tormented cow drinking scalding water that only harms her.<Cite source="buddhaghosa-path-of-purification" locator="XVII.39" />
Śāntideva gives the first-person view. Knowing all this, he still sinks back, as if bewitched,
asking "what is there dwelling inside me?"<Cite source="shantideva-bodhicaryavatara" locator="4.24–27" />
Dōgen's *Shoaku makusa* retells the exchange in which a poet objects that "do no evil" is
something a three-year-old could say, and the master replies that an eighty-year-old cannot
practise it. Dōgen insists that not-doing evil is not first contrived by the ordinary person's
will. It appears as the power of practice.<Cite source="dogen-shobogenzo" locator="Shoaku makusa" />

#### One root of greatness and misery

Śāntideva puts the shared explanation in one verse: "Whatever joy there is in this world all
comes from desiring others to be happy, and whatever suffering there is ... from desiring
myself to be happy."<Cite source="shantideva-bodhicaryavatara" locator="8.129" /> Both rest on
the same universal wish for happiness. Since "there is no owner of suffering", there is no
principled reason to confine it to oneself.<Cite source="shantideva-bodhicaryavatara" locator="8.101" />
The tradition does not condemn desire as such. Right effort means generating desire
(*chanda*) for wholesome states,<Cite source="samyutta-nikaya-sujato" locator="SN 45.8" /> a
desire that fades once its goal is reached.<Cite source="samyutta-nikaya-sujato" locator="SN 51.15" />
Conscience belongs to the same equipment. Buddhaghosa defines it as disgust at evil, grounded
in self-respect, and shame as dread of evil, grounded in respect for others.<Cite source="buddhaghosa-path-of-purification" locator="XIV.142" />

#### Agency without a substantial self

No-self is not the claim that persons do not exist. The aggregates are not-self because they
cannot be controlled and lead to affliction.<Cite source="samyutta-nikaya-sujato" locator="SN 22.59" />
Buddhaghosa: there is "no doer over and above the doing", though "the wise say 'doer'" as
conventional usage.<Cite source="buddhaghosa-path-of-purification" locator="XIX.19–20" />
Nāgārjuna holds that agent and action depend on one another.<Cite source="nagarjuna-mmk" locator="8.12" />
He turns the usual objection around. If things had a fixed intrinsic nature, no one could do
right or wrong or progress toward awakening.<Cite source="nagarjuna-mmk" locator="24.32–33" />
In Tsongkhapa's Gelug presentation, the ethical subject is real conventionally, through
dependent origination.<Cite source="sep-tsongkhapa" locator="§5" />

#### Buddha-nature, aspiration and dignity

Here the schools diverge. In early and Theravāda sources, the value of human life lies in its
rare opportunity. The blind turtle surfacing through a yoke is the image of how rarely one is
born human.<Cite source="samyutta-nikaya-sujato" locator="SN 56.47–48" /> Humans surpass the
gods in "bravery, mindfulness, and the spiritual life".<Cite source="anguttara-nikaya-sujato" locator="AN 9.21" />
The *Ratnagotravibhāga* goes further: "all embodied beings are always" bearers of the
buddha-element.<Cite source="ratnagotravibhaga-johnston" locator="I.28" /> Moral aspiration
itself testifies to that nature. Without it there would be no revulsion at suffering and no
longing for nirvāṇa.<Cite source="ratnagotravibhaga-johnston" locator="I.40–41" /> Dharmakīrti
argues that the mind is luminous by nature and its stains adventitious, so they can be
removed.<Cite source="dharmakirti-pramanavarttika" locator="II.208" /> Dōgen reads
"all beings have buddha-nature" as "entire being is buddha-nature" and rejects any reading of
it as a hidden self.<Cite source="dogen-shobogenzo" locator="Busshō" /> It is fully present
yet "not manifest without practice".<Cite source="dogen-shobogenzo" locator="Bendōwa" />

#### The remedy

Every strand holds the condition curable. For one who has cultivated the wholesome roots, the
unwholesome are "cut off at the root, made like a palm stump" and extinguished "in this very
life".<Cite source="anguttara-nikaya-sujato" locator="AN 3.69" /> Nāgārjuna: ignorance ceases
through cultivating knowledge.<Cite source="nagarjuna-mmk" locator="26.11" /> Tsongkhapa
summarises the Tibetan path. Renunciation needs bodhicitta, and both need the wisdom that sees
dependent origination and emptiness together.<Cite source="tsongkhapa-three-principal-aspects" locator="vv. 6–12" />
Schools describe the goal differently: the Theravāda unconditioned, the Madhyamaka emptiness,
Dōgen's practice-realisation. Pure Land traditions, not represented here, rely on Amida's vow
rather than self-cultivation.
