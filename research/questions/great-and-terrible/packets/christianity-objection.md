# Critic packet: strongest objection to Reformed anthropology (`great-and-terrible`)

Prepared by an independent critic-researcher. Scope: the `## Strongest objection` section of the
Christian answer only. This packet does **not** contain or anticipate the Christian reply. All
verification was done on 2026-10-05. Quotations from the 1740/1741 Taylor volumes are given with
modern "s" for the long s; spelling and capitalization otherwise follow the scan.

**The target.** The Reformed answer to "why is man so great and so terrible?" has three load-bearing
parts: (1) greatness from the image of God, still partly present, together with general revelation
and common grace; (2) terribleness from Adam's fall, with "the guilt of this sin ... imputed" to all
his posterity and a corrupted nature conveyed "by ordinary generation" (WCF 6.3), a corruption by
which "we are utterly indisposed, disabled, and made opposite to all good, and wholly inclined to
all evil" (WCF 6.4); (3) moral inability: fallen man "hath wholly lost all ability of will to any
spiritual good accompanying salvation" (WCF 9.3). Works by unregenerate people, though useful, "are
therefore sinful, and cannot please God" (WCF 16.7). (All four read at https://www.opc.org/wcf.html.)
An objection is strong in proportion to how much of what is *distinctively* Reformed it puts
under pressure, rather than what Christians generally hold.

---

## 1. Candidate objections surveyed

### A. The empirical objection: depraved people are visibly good

**Claim.** If human nature is corrupted so that we are "wholly inclined to all evil", the
intellectual achievement, courage, civic virtue and self-sacrificing love of unbelievers are
inexplicable; the evidence shows a nature that is mixed, not ruined.

**Best proponents.**
- Pelagius, *Letter to Demetrias* 3: the "good of nature" shows itself even among people with no
  worship of the true God; many philosophers have been chaste, patient, modest, generous, temperate
  and kind (read in English only, as quoted in Wiggers, *Augustinism and Pelagianism*, trans.
  Emerson, ch. 13, at https://www.gospeltruth.net/Wiggers/wiggerschap13.htm; Latin of ch. 3 **not
  read**).
- John Taylor of Norwich makes a related point against an inherited sinful nature: different
  children have different tempers, families pass on different vices and also piety, and the
  "plain and obvious Reason" is "good Instructions, Discipline and Examples", "without having
  Recourse to the Propagation of a sinful Nature" (*Supplement*, 1741, p. 108; read).
- Council of Trent, Session 6, canon 7, anathematizes the claim that "all works done before
  Justification, in whatsoever way they be done, are truly sins": a direct rejection of the
  position in WCF 16.7 (read at papalencyclicals.net, translation probably Waterworth 1848, not
  confirmed).

**Assessment: weak to moderate against Reformed theology as it is actually stated.** The doctrine
expects this evidence. "Total" depravity is extensive, not intensive: corruption reaches every
faculty but is not maximal. Common grace and the remaining image are said to explain unbelievers'
virtue and knowledge (this site's own `docs/METHODOLOGY.md` says so). An objection that assumes
Reformed theology denies unbelievers do anything good is a straw man (see §3).

### B. Second-order form: common grace makes the doctrine idle or unfalsifiable

**Claim.** Goodness gets credited to common grace or the image, and evil to depravity; WCF 16.7
classes even admirable unbelievers' works as sin because of their motive. No pattern of human
behavior could then count against the doctrine, so it explains nothing that a plain "mixed
nature" account does not.

**Proponents.** I found **no** major published critic who states this precise objection. The
confessional texts (WCF 16.7 with WCF 6.4) give it a genuine basis, and Taylor's family-temper
argument above is an early version of the rival-explanation point. Without a named proponent, the
method's preference for "objections actually made by major critics" counts against using it
as the lead objection.

**Assessment: moderate.** It names a real tension, but not a contradiction. The doctrine does make
risky claims: that sin is universal, that it reaches every faculty, and that no one is without
sin. A Reformed theologian can reply that WCF 16.7 is a claim about motive and orientation toward
God, not about observable behavior. This is better treated as **underdetermination** than as
falsity, and it is strongest when it is part of C, not when it stands alone.

### C. The moral objection to imputed guilt and federal headship

**Claim.** Guilt cannot be transferred. To count me guilty for an act done before my birth,
without my knowledge or consent, by a representative I did not appoint, is unjust. If the terrible
in man is explained by guilt that is not his own, it is explained by injustice.

**Best proponents.**
- **John Taylor**, against the federal scheme of Isaac Watts's *Ruin and Recovery of Mankind*
  (identified in Taylor's text only as "R. R."): "A Representative, the Guilt of whose Conduct
  shall be imputed to us, and whose Sins shall corrupt and debauch our Nature, is one of the
  greatest Absurdities in all the System of corrupt Religion". That any man "without my Knowledge
  or Consent, should so represent me, that when he is guilty I am to be reputed guilty ... and all
  this before I am born", is "unreasonable, and altogether inconsistent with the Truth and Goodness
  of God" (*Supplement*, pp. 108–109; read). On Rom. 5:19, those "made Sinners by the
  Disobedience of another, without their own Knowledge or Consent, surely can be Sinners in no
  other Sense but as they are Sufferers" (*Scripture-Doctrine*, Part I, pp. 33–34; read). Taylor
  is the critic Jonathan Edwards chose to answer in *Original Sin* (1758). That is evidence the
  objection is serious by Reformed standards.
- **Immanuel Kant** grants a *universal, radical* propensity to evil. It is radical "because it
  corrupts the ground of all maxims" (*Religion*, Bk. I, §III). But because it must be imputable,
  it must be self-incurred: "Man himself must make or have made himself into whatever, in a moral
  sense, whether good or evil, he is or is to become ... for otherwise he could not be held
  responsible for it" (Bk. I, General Observation). Of all explanations of the spread of evil,
  "the most inept is that which describes it as descending to us as an inheritance from our first
  parents" (Bk. I, §IV). All read.
- **Current philosophy of religion.** Kevin Timpe's SEP article reports that "Original guilt ... is
  defended by very few philosophers." It lists as holders of "corruption-only" views Crisp 2020a,
  Plantinga 2000, Swinburne 1989 and others, and notes that Catholic and many Orthodox traditions
  deny original guilt (SEP, "Sin in Christian Thought", §4.3; read). The same article describes
  federalism as involving a "treating as if" or "legal fiction" (§4.4; read). Swinburne,
  *Responsibility and Atonement* (1989), is the standard analytic statement, but I did **not**
  read it. Oliver Crisp, a sympathetic Reformed analyst, has criticized federal imputation (per
  SEP §4.4, citing Crisp 2009; not read).

**Assessment: strong.** It hits what is distinctively Reformed: federal headship and imputed
guilt (WCF 6.3), not a general Christian claim. It has first-rank proponents, one secular-
philosophical (Kant) and one that Reformed theology itself treated as a serious opponent (Taylor).
A secular critic can fully endorse it, since it rests only on a widely shared principle of
responsibility. Even many Christian philosophers concede its core.

### D. "Ought implies can" against moral inability

**Claim.** If fallen people cannot do good (WCF 6.4, 9.3), they cannot be obliged to, and cannot
be blamed for failing. The very moral demand that marks human greatness is evidence of ability.

**Best proponents.** Pelagius, *Letter to Demetrias* 16: the just God did not will to command
anything impossible, nor would the good God condemn a man for what he could not avoid (Latin read
at earlychurchtexts.com, from Migne PL 33 col. 1109; my paraphrase, not a published translation).
Taylor: a man who "sinneth necessarily, or is unavoidably made a Sinner, through the malignant
Influence of some Principle, which it was never in his Power to command", would "then ... be no
Sinner at all" (*Scripture-Doctrine*, Part III, p. 222; read). Kant: "despite the fall, the
injunction that we ought to become better men resounds unabatedly in our souls; hence this must
be within our power" (Bk. I, General Observation; read). SEP §4.3 sets out Rea's "principle of
possible prevention" as the main source of opposition to original guilt.

**Assessment: strong, but Reformed theology has a long-developed answer.** Edwards's distinction
between natural and moral inability in *Freedom of the Will* was built for exactly this objection.
D on its own therefore invites a well-rehearsed exchange. It gains force when combined with C,
because together they form a dilemma (see §2).

### E. The evolutionary-history objection

**Claim.** Genomics gives no evidence of a human population ever reduced to a single pair. The
traits behind the "terrible" (aggression, dominance, deception, selfishness), and also those behind
the "great" (cooperation, care), appear continuous with pre-human primate ancestry. On this view
there was no prior state of original righteousness from which humans fell.

**Proponents.** Dennis Venema, *PSCF* 62:3 (2010), p. 174: the single-recent-pair hypothesis
"has no support from a genomics perspective, and, indeed, is counter to a large body of evidence"
(read). John R. Schneider (formerly of Calvin College), "The Fall of 'Augustinian Adam'", *Zygon*
47:4 (2012), 949–69, argues that the Augustinian Adam does not survive evolutionary science (paywalled
and blocked; **not read**). Daryl Domning and Monika Hellwig, *Original Selfishness* (Ashgate,
2006), argue that selfish behavior is an evolutionary inheritance older than humanity (lending-only;
**not read**). Peter Enns is a further Christian critic (noted, not relied on).

**Assessment: moderate to strong empirically, weaker as the lead objection.** (i) The single-pair
point is contested by genealogical-ancestry models that place a historical Adam within a larger
population. These are widely discussed, for example by S. Joshua Swamidass (2019; not read), and
Venema's population estimates have been challenged in the literature (not read). (ii) The
stronger form, that violence predates any fall, depends on treating pre-human animal aggression as
*moral* evil, which is a disputed category move. (iii) It targets historical Adam in all
Augustinian traditions, not specifically the Reformed explanation of greatness and misery. It
could be added as a supplementary pressure point in the Deep dive. It is not the strongest form.

### F. Intra-Christian critiques (Trent; Remonstrants)

Trent, Session 6, canon 5, anathematizes the claim that "since Adam's sin, the free will of man is
lost and extinguished". Chapter 1 says free will, though "attenuated ... and bent down, was by no
means extinguished". Canon 7 is quoted under A. (Read at
https://www.papalencyclicals.net/councils/trent/sixth-session.htm; translation attribution not
confirmed.) These are useful as evidence that C and D are serious and not merely secular
complaints. The Catholic tradition denies *original guilt* in the federal sense (per SEP §4.3).
**Caution:** the 1610 Remonstrance (Art. 3) is usually read as *affirming* inability without
grace. The Arminian disagreement lies mainly in resistible grace and conditional election, not in
a denial of depravity. I did not re-read the Remonstrance in this session, so do not use it as
evidence for D.

### Why C+D is chosen

C and D are not two objections bolted together. They are the two horns of one dilemma about the
*explanatory* role of the fall in the Reformed answer, and they target the specifically Reformed
mechanism (imputation and inability). They are endorsed by a secular philosopher who grants the
data in full, so the objection cannot be dismissed as a refusal to see human evil. They also rest
on a principle of responsibility that most contemporary philosophers, including many Christians,
accept. A and B are absorbed as support: Kant and Taylor each offer a rival explanation of the
same two-sided phenomenon (an inextirpable "seed of goodness" alongside a self-incurred propensity
to evil; or nature plus "Instructions, Discipline and Examples"). That makes the federal scheme
look not only morally costly but explanatorily dispensable. E is weaker and less specific. It is
better used in the Deep dive.

## 2. Chosen strongest objection (for `## Strongest objection`)

> Word count ≈ 240. Cite ids are the proposed ids in §4. Taylor's spelling is retained inside
> quotations.

The objection does not deny the darkness; it denies that the Reformed mechanism explains it. Kant
grants a propensity to evil that is universal and "radical" because "it corrupts the ground of all
maxims"<Cite source="kant-religion-greene-hudson" locator="Bk. I, §III" />. Yet guilt, he argues,
must be self-incurred: a person "must make or have made himself" whatever he is morally, "for
otherwise he could not be held responsible for it"<Cite source="kant-religion-greene-hudson" locator="Bk. I, General Observation" />.
Inheritance from first parents is therefore "the most inept" account of evil<Cite source="kant-religion-greene-hudson" locator="Bk. I, §IV" />.
John Taylor, whom Edwards thought worth a treatise, pressed the point against federal headship:
that another should so represent me "that when he is guilty I am to be reputed guilty", and "all
this before I am born", is "unreasonable"<Cite source="taylor-original-sin-supplement" locator="pp. 108–109" />.
This yields a dilemma. If the corruption is inherited and leaves us unable to do good, the cruelty it
explains is misfortune, not guilt. A man who sins from a principle "never in his Power to command"
would "be no Sinner at all"<Cite source="taylor-original-sin" locator="Part III, p. 222" />. If instead
we are truly guilty, the guilt must be our own, and Adam's imputed guilt does no explanatory work.
The greatness side presses the same way: "the injunction that we ought to become better men
resounds unabatedly in our souls; hence this must be within our power"<Cite source="kant-religion-greene-hudson" locator="Bk. I, General Observation" />.
Few philosophers now defend original guilt, and many Christian philosophers hold
corruption-only views<Cite source="sep-sin-christian" locator="§4.3" />. A mixed creature with a
self-incurred bent to evil explains the same phenomena without imputing to anyone a sin he did
not commit.

## 3. What a weak version would look like, and why it was avoided

- **"If people are totally depraved, why are atheists kind?"** This misstates the doctrine. It
  confuses extensive with intensive depravity and ignores common grace and the remaining image,
  which Reformed theology explicitly uses to explain unbelievers' virtue (WCF 16.7 itself grants
  such works are "of good use"). A Reformed reader would rightly call it a straw man.
- **"It's cruel to damn babies."** This is emotionally loaded and shifts the topic to infant
  destiny. The chosen version states the underlying principle (no guilt without one's own act or
  ability) in a form a philosopher would accept.
- **"Evolution disproves Genesis, so the fall is a myth."** This assumes a single reading of
  Genesis, ignores genealogical-Adam and other models, and does not engage the anthropology at
  all.
- **"The doctrine is a contradiction."** Under the brief's §2 rules, the argument shows an
  internal tension and a disputed ultimate premise about responsibility, not a formal
  contradiction. The formulation therefore says "dilemma" and "does no explanatory work", not
  "incoherent".
- **Quote-mining.** Kant is not used as if he denied human evil. His affirmation of radical evil is
  stated first, because it is what gives the objection its force.

## 6. Thinker registry recommendation

Kant, Taylor and Pelagius are not in `thinkers.yaml`.

- **Minimal option (recommended): cite them as sources only, with no thinker profiles.** They
  appear in one section of one answer as critics. The registry's thinker list is a research map
  of representatives and key interlocutors, and none of them is needed as a recurring figure yet.
- If profiles are wanted later:
  - **Kant** would be a philosophical interlocutor. He does not fit `naturalism` (he was not a
    naturalist, and placing him there would misrepresent that lane) and is not a representative of
    the Christian lane. Registering him as `role: interlocutor` under `christianity`, with a note
    that he is a critic of the Augustinian and Reformed doctrine rather than a representative, is
    the least distorting option. Revisit only if he recurs on morality questions.
  - **Taylor** and **Pelagius**, if ever registered, would be `interlocutor` under `christianity`,
    with notes marking them as historical critics of Augustinian/Reformed original-sin doctrine.
    That parallels how Aquinas, Athanasius and Anselm are marked as non-Reformed.
