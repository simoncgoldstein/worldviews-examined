# Steelman packet: Naturalistic atheism — `great-and-terrible`

Lane: `naturalism`. Prepared by an independent steelman researcher under `BRIEF.md`. This packet
contains no Christian critique and draws on no Christian critical literature. Research date:
2026-10-05.

Verification shorthand used below: **V** = passage read in the cited edition (or in an

## 1. Scope

**Represent:** *evolutionary-sentimentalist naturalism* — the broad, mainstream form of
philosophical naturalism that (a) treats human beings as wholly natural organisms produced by
biological and cultural evolution, (b) explains moral capacities through social instincts,
sympathy and culturally developed norms (the line running Hume → Darwin → contemporary
evolutionary ethics and moral psychology), and (c) remains **neutral or pluralist on metaethics**,
showing how the same anthropology is held by naturalistic moral realists (Railton) and by
anti-realists (Mackie's error theory; constructivist and pragmatist views such as Kitcher's).

**Why this form.** It is the account a well-read naturalist would recognize as "ours" for this
particular question. The question is anthropological and moral-psychological, not mainly
metaphysical; the strongest naturalist answer here is not an argument against theism but a
positive *explanatory* story, and that story is shared across most naturalist metaethics.
Scoping to physicalist reductionism (Rosenberg) or to error theory alone would represent a
narrower, more contested wing (`docs/SOURCES.md` warns against attributing nihilism to all
naturalists and against assuming physicalism when not needed).

**Not represented as the default:** eliminativist/"nice nihilist" reductive naturalism
(Rosenberg) — flagged in §10 as a materially different answer; popular polemicists (Dawkins,
Hitchens), per `docs/SOURCES.md`.

## 2. Anthropology: what a human being fundamentally is

- A human being is an evolved social primate: a living organism, continuous with other
  animals, whose distinctive capacities (language, abstract reasoning, cumulative culture,
  conscience) emerged by natural processes. Russell: "Man is a part of Nature, not something
  contrasted with Nature" (*What I Believe*, ch. 1). Darwin closes *Descent* by saying that man,
  "with all his noble qualities", "still bears in his bodily frame the indelible stamp of his
  lowly origin" (ch. XXI, p. 619).
- Humans are fundamentally **passionate** creatures whose reason is instrumental and
  regulative rather than a separate governing faculty. Hume: reason alone cannot produce or
  oppose volition; only a contrary passion can (T 2.3.3.4).
- Human nature is **mixed by constitution**, not by corruption: Hume's "some particle of the
  dove, kneaded into our frame, along with the elements of the wolf and serpent" (EPM 9.4);
  Hume's "selfishness and confin'd generosity" (T 3.2.2.18); Kitcher's "incomplete
  psychological altruists" (Précis, p. 18).
- Human nature is relatively **constant** across history (Hume, EHU 8.7), which is why history
  shows the same mix of nobility and cruelty in every age.
- Persons/selves: on Dennett's view the unified self is not an inner soul or a single brain
  module but a "center of narrative gravity" — a real, functionally important construct of a
  brain that has to answer to others and to itself for what it does (Dennett 2003, pp. 48–49).
  The "partially coherent agency" this yields (abstract, p. 39) is itself part of the
  explanation of inner conflict.

## 3. Metaphysical context

- **Ontological naturalism:** reality contains only natural entities; mental, biological and
  social phenomena are constituted by (or realized in) physical processes (SEP "Naturalism",
  §1). There is no God, soul substance or cosmic purpose that assigns humans a role.
- **Methodological naturalism:** our best route to knowledge of human nature is continuous
  with the sciences (SEP "Naturalism", §2) — evolutionary biology, psychology, anthropology,
  history.
- **No cosmic teleology.** Russell's 1903 statement: man is "the product of causes which had no
  prevision of the end they were achieving" ("A Free Man's Worship", ¶5). Meaning and value
  are located in human (and sentient) life, not in the cosmos.
- **Nonreductive vs reductive.** Most naturalists accept that higher-level kinds (beliefs,
  reasons, persons, moral properties) are real, even if realized physically (SEP
  "Naturalism", §1); reductive/eliminativist naturalists deny some of these (see §10).
- Oppy's formulation (naturalism = natural reality exhausts causal reality; minds are late,
  local products of natural history) is the most careful contemporary philosophical
  statement, but I could not open an Oppy text during this research (see §13–14), so it is
  not cited in the draft.

## 4. Why humans possess remarkable capacities

1. **Social instincts + intelligence → conscience.** Darwin's "fundamental proposition": any
   animal with well-marked social instincts "would inevitably acquire a moral sense or
   conscience, as soon as its intellectual powers had become as well, or nearly as well
   developed, as in man" (*Descent*, ch. IV, p. 98). Three components: social instincts
   (sympathy, pleasure in company, services), memory and reflection comparing past motives,
   and language making shared expectations possible (pp. 98–99).
2. **Love, sympathy and sacrifice.** Darwin calls the moral sense "the most noble of all the
   attributes of man," leading him "to risk his life for that of a fellow-creature" (ch. IV,
   p. 97). Kin selection, reciprocal altruism and (contested) group-level selection explain
   how other-regarding dispositions can evolve (SEP "Biological Altruism", Okasha). Crucially,
   *biological* altruism (fitness-defined) is distinct from *psychological* altruism (genuine
   concern), and evolution can yield the latter: genuine love is not a disguised selfishness
   (SEP "Biological Altruism"; SEP "Morality and Evolutionary Biology" §2.2).
3. **Sympathy is real and powerful** (Hume, T 3.3.1.10), and can be corrected by taking
   "steady and general points of view" (T 3.3.1.15) — this is the natural basis of impartial
   moral judgment.
4. **Rationality and agency.** Dennett: humans add to animal voluntary action a "virtual" layer
   that lets us ask and answer for reasons — "We can engage in the practice of asking and giving
   reasons" (2003, p. 48) — which makes us self-redesigning "transformers" (p. 48). Rationality
   is an evolved, culturally amplified competence, not a gift from outside nature.
5. **Civilization and culture as social technology.** Kitcher: the ethical project began as a
   social technology to remedy "altruism failures" in small bands and acquired new functions
   over time (Précis, pp. 4, 10). Cumulative culture, language and institutions build on the
   evolved base, so civilization is a natural achievement of a cultural species.
6. **Moral aspiration** is the reflective extension of sympathy: Darwin's "simplest reason"
   extends sympathy from tribe to nation, and there is "only an artificial barrier" to its
   extension to all nations and races (ch. IV, p. 122). Russell: "The good life is one inspired by
   love and guided by knowledge" (*What I Believe*, ch. 2).

## 5. Why humans behave destructively or wrongly

1. **Limited, partial sympathy.** Darwin: social feelings and services "are by no means extended
   to all the individuals of the same species, only to those of the same association" (ch. IV,
   p. 98). Hume: justice arises "from the selfishness and confin'd generosity of men, along with
   the scanty provision nature has made" (T 3.2.2.18). Mackie (following Warnock): among the
   factors that make things go badly, "above all limited sympathies"; "Men sometimes display
   active malevolence to one another" (*Ethics*, p. 108).
2. **Competing evolved motives.** Self-interest, status-seeking, sexual and appetitive drives,
   fear, and resentment are as natural as sympathy; Darwin: "At the moment of action, man will
   no doubt be apt to follow the stronger impulse; and though this may occasionally prompt him
   to the noblest deeds, it will more commonly lead him to gratify his own desires at the
   expense of other men" (ch. IV, p. 114).
3. **Mismatch and scarcity.** Dispositions tuned to small bands, under conditions of scarcity
   and instability of possession (Hume, T 3.2.2.7), misfire in large societies. (The
   evolutionary-mismatch point is widely held; not separately sourced here.)
4. **Knowingly doing wrong (akrasia).**
   - *Hume:* "Men often act knowingly against their interest" (T 2.3.3.10): violent passions can
     overcome calm ones; "strength of mind" is the prevalence of the calm passions (same para).
   - *Darwin's model of conscience* explicitly predicts doing what one knows to be wrong: a
     stronger, momentary impulse wins; then the "ever-enduring social instinct" reasserts itself
     and the agent feels "remorse, repentance, regret, or shame" and resolves to act differently:
     "this is conscience; for conscience looks backwards, and serves as a guide for the future"
     (ch. IV, pp. 113–114).
   - *Philosophy of action:* Davidson's analysis of incontinence (intentional action against
     one's all-things-considered judgment) and later work on hyperbolic discounting (Ainslie)
     and resolution-violation (Holton) (SEP "Weakness of Will", §§2, 3.3, 3.4.2).
   - *Dual-process moral psychology:* Greene's "automatic settings" vs "manual mode"; e.g.
     occupied working memory tips choice toward immediate temptation (Greene 2014, §I).
   - *Divided self:* Dennett's "only partially coherent agency" (2003, p. 39).
5. **Ideology and self-deception.** Russell: cruelty sustained by persuading oneself that a
   class of people "is of inferior clay" (*What I Believe*, ch. 4); worship of power ("A Free
   Man's Worship"). Mackie: objectifying our demands gives them spurious absolute authority
   (*Ethics*, pp. 42–43) — a mechanism that can sanctify both good and bad norms.

## 6. Do greatness and wickedness share a common explanation?

**Yes — this is the heart of the naturalist answer.** The same evolved equipment produces both:

- **Parochial cooperation.** Sympathy and loyalty evolved within "the same association"
  (Darwin, p. 98). Darwin's own tribal-competition sketch: a tribe rich in "courageous,
  sympathetic and faithful members" would "succeed better and conquer the other" (ch. V,
  p. 130) — fidelity, courage and self-sacrifice are selected *in the context of* inter-group
  conflict. In-group virtue and out-group hostility are two faces of one adaptation.
- **Limited generosity.** Hume's mixed nature — "confin'd generosity" — explains both why we
  are capable of justice and why we need it (T 3.2.2.5, 3.2.2.18).
- **Intelligence cuts both ways.** The same reason that extends sympathy and designs
  institutions also plans, deceives and rationalizes; the same capacity for norm-following
  that underwrites conscience can enforce cruel norms (Mackie, pp. 42–43; Kitcher Précis, p. 4,
  on early conscience being built partly from fear of punishment by unseen beings and peers).
- **Kitcher's formula:** "Our propensities for psychological altruism committed us to a form
  of social life, and the limitations of those propensities made that form of life
  problematic" (Précis, p. 10); humans are "incomplete psychological altruists" (p. 18).
- **Darwin's formula:** the same remorse-generating conscience exists *because* the enduring
  social instinct and stronger momentary impulses coexist (pp. 113–114).

So the "great and terrible" combination is not a puzzle requiring two sources (a high origin
and a fall); it is what one should expect from a clever, social, partially altruistic animal
whose motives were assembled by selection for different jobs.

## 7. Specific topics

- **Rationality.** An evolved, culturally scaffolded competence (Dennett, reasons-giving,
  p. 48). Instrumental in Hume's sense for action (T 2.3.3.4); but also reflective and
  self-correcting (general points of view, T 3.3.1.15). Naturalized epistemology (Quine)
  treats its reliability as an empirical matter — not needed for this question.
- **Moral knowledge.** Realists (Railton): moral facts are facts about what would be
  rationally approved "were the interests of all potentially affected individuals counted
  equally under circumstances of full and vivid information" (1986, p. 190); we learn them by
  individual and social feedback (pp. 191–194). Anti-realists (Mackie): "There are no
  objective values" (p. 15); morality is invented but not arbitrary — it has an object
  (counteracting limited sympathies, p. 108). Evolutionary debunking (Street, Joyce) vs
  realist replies appeal to autonomous moral reflection (SEP "Morality and Evolutionary
  Biology", §4.1).
- **Conscience.** Darwin's backward-looking, socially informed, remorse-generating faculty
  (pp. 98, 113–114); Kitcher's culturally "built" conscience (p. 4); Hume's sentiment of
  disapprobation found by turning "into your own breast" (T 3.1.1.26).
- **Evil/disorder.** No metaphysical evil; "evil" names severe harms and vicious dispositions —
  cruelty, betrayal, domination — explained by natural causes (limited sympathy, competition,
  ideology, mismatch). Realists add that such acts are objectively wrong because of the
  interests they ignore (Railton). Russell insists "the world of fact, after all, is not
  good" ("A Free Man's Worship").
- **Agency/free will.** Mainstream naturalism is compatibilist. Hume: liberty is "a power of
  acting or not acting, according to the determinations of the will" (EHU 8.23). Dennett:
  responsibility attaches to the extended, narrative self that can keep track of and answer
  for its intentions (2003, pp. 48–49). (Hard incompatibilist naturalists, e.g. Caruso,
  dissent: §10.)
- **Desire/appetite.** Desires are natural and not intrinsically disordered; disorder is
  conflict among desires, or desire unregulated by calm passions/long-term goals (Hume
  T 2.3.3.10; Greene §I on immediate vs long-term goals; Ainslie in SEP "Weakness of Will",
  §3.4.2).

## 8. Can the human condition be remedied?

- **No total cure, real improvement.** Naturalism offers no redemption of human nature, but
  holds that institutions, education, science and expanding sympathy can substantially
  improve conduct.
- **Hume:** justice is an artifice — "nature provides a remedy in the judgment and
  understanding, for what is irregular and incommodious in the affections" (T 3.2.2.9).
- **Darwin:** expansion of sympathy through instruction, example and public opinion; humanity
  to animals as "one of the latest moral acquisitions" (ch. IV, pp. 122–123).
- **Russell (anti-utopian gradualism):** "There is no short cut to the good life, whether
  individual or social. To build up the good life, we must build up intelligence, selfcontrol,
  and sympathy"; it is "a matter of gradual improvement, of early training, of educational
  experiment" (*What I Believe*, ch. 4). He explicitly rejects catastrophic-conversion hopes
  and acknowledges that "Human nature imposes some limitation of sympathy" (ch. 4).
- **Railton:** social feedback produces "an uneven secular trend" toward inclusion of
  previously discounted interests — explicitly without guarantee ("It would be silly to
  think … there will be a univocal trend") (1986, pp. 193–194).
- **Kitcher:** ethical progress as functional refinement of a social technology (e.g. the
  recognition of slavery as an altruism-failure); progress is possible but "may be very rare"
  (Précis, pp. 6, 12).
- **Mackie:** morality's content should be designed to counteract limited sympathies (p. 108).
- **Greene:** reflective "manual mode" can correct automatic responses where they misfire,
  especially in novel, inter-group problems (§I and passim).

## 9. Dignity: inherent or derived?

Naturalists do not ground dignity in a divine image; they offer several groundings, not all
compatible:

1. **Capacity-based (derived from what we are):** rational, reason-giving, self-governing
   agency (Dennett, p. 48); sentience and interests. Dignity is real but grounded in natural
   properties, and comes in degrees across creatures.
2. **Realist equal consideration:** on Railton's view, the moral point of view counts "the
   interests of all potentially affected individuals ... equally" (p. 190); equal standing is
   an objective feature of moral rightness, not merely a preference.
3. **Sentimentalist universality:** Hume's sentiment of humanity is shared and extends "to all
   mankind" (EPM 9.5–9.6); dignity is the object of a universal, correctable sentiment.
4. **Conferred/constructed:** Russell: "It is we who create value, and our desires which
   confer value" (*What I Believe*, ch. 1); in "A Free Man's Worship" he urges us "to exalt the
   dignity of Man" by refusing to worship non-human power. Mackie: values are invented but
   serve a real, shared object.
5. **Historical achievement:** Kitcher/Darwin — equal dignity is a hard-won product of moral
   progress (the expanding circle), not a pre-given metaphysical fact.

Honest summary: most naturalists treat dignity as **derived** (from capacities or interests)
or **conferred/achieved**, not "inherent" in a metaphysically primitive sense; realists
(Railton) claim it is nonetheless objective. Non-naturalist atheist realists (e.g. Erik
Wielenberg, *Robust Ethics*, 2014 — not verified here) argue for brute, necessary moral facts
including intrinsic worth; they are atheists but not naturalists about ethics.

## 11. What this worldview genuinely explains well

- **Unified explanation.** It derives the greatness and the terribleness from one source —
  evolved, limited sociality plus intelligence — and so predicts their *specific shape*:
  heroism and cruelty clustering along group lines; generosity strongest to kin and friends;
  self-sacrifice in war; the same loyalty producing both.
- **Continuity with animals.** It explains why rudiments of empathy, reconciliation,
  fairness-sensitivity and also coalitionary violence appear in other social animals.
- **Moral diversity with a common core.** Universal concerns (care, reciprocity, prohibitions
  on violence within the group) plus wide cultural variation are what an evolved-plus-cultural
  account expects.
- **Akrasia without mystery.** Hume's calm vs violent passions, Darwin's account of remorse,
  and dual-process/discounting research give a detailed, testable mechanism for knowingly
  doing wrong, including predictable conditions (fatigue, distraction, immediacy) under which
  it increases.
- **Conscience as social and backward-looking.** It explains why guilt tracks the judgments of
  one's community, why it can be miscalibrated (enforcing cruel norms), and why it can be
  corrected by reflection.
- **Moral progress as real but fragile.** It accounts for both the expansion of the moral circle
  (abolition, rights movements) and its frequent reversals without guaranteeing either.
- **Epistemic modesty.** It makes claims answerable to empirical evidence and revisable as the
  sciences progress.

## 13. Uncertainties / contested interpretations

- **Hume's religious position** is contested (atheist, agnostic, or attenuated deist); registry
  note already says so. Hume is used here for his account of human nature, which is
  naturalistic regardless.
- **Darwin's group-level sketch** (ch. V) is contested in biology; kin selection/reciprocity
  vs multi-level selection remains debated (SEP "Biological Altruism"). Darwin's language
  ("savages") and some of his racial and civilizational judgments are dated; do not quote those
  passages.
- **Russell's views shifted**: "A Free Man's Worship" (1903) reflects an earlier, more
  Platonist-tinged view of ideals; *What I Believe* (1925) is more subjectivist. Do not merge
  them silently.
- **Mackie's error theory** is a minority view among naturalists; it should not stand for
  naturalism.
- **Greene's Central Tension Principle** (deontology ↔ automatic, consequentialism ↔ manual) is
  contested; cite only the general dual-process framework.
- **Moral progress**: naturalists disagree whether it is objective progress (realists) or
  functional improvement relative to evolving aims (Kitcher), or whether the notion survives at
  all (debunkers).
- **Oppy** could not be read (PhilArchive blocked automated access). His naturalism is
  well known, but nothing from him should be cited until a text is opened.
- **Mackie edition**: verified against a scan of the Penguin 1990 reprint (whose pagination
  follows the 1977 Pelican). The scan is not an authorized open copy; do not link it.

## 16. Thinker profile notes

**David Hume** (1711–1776). Edinburgh; Scottish Enlightenment; empiricist, "science of man".
Relevant as the classic naturalistic anatomy of human nature as a mix of benevolence and
self-love, and of morality as grounded in sentiment. Key ideas: reason as servant of the
passions; calm vs violent passions; sympathy and the general point of view; justice as an
artificial virtue arising from confined generosity and scarcity; compatibilist liberty;
uniformity of human nature. Works: *A Treatise of Human Nature* (1739–40); *Enquiry
Concerning Human Understanding* (1748); *Enquiry Concerning the Principles of Morals* (1751);
*Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion* (1779). Reference: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/hume/ ;
https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/hume-moral/ ; texts at https://davidhume.org/.

**Charles Darwin** (1809–1882). English naturalist; theory of evolution by natural selection.
*Descent of Man* (1871; 2nd ed. 1874) applies it to human mental and moral faculties. Darwin
called himself agnostic; register him as a specialist used for evolutionary anthropology.
Key ideas: moral sense as product of social instincts plus intellect; conscience as
backward-looking remorse; sympathy limited to one's association and capable of extension;
selection of social virtues in competing groups; continuity of human and animal minds.
Works: *On the Origin of Species* (1859); *The Descent of Man* (1871/1874); *The Expression of
the Emotions in Man and Animals* (1872). Reference: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/origin-descent/ ;
https://darwin-online.org.uk/.

**J. L. Mackie** (1917–1981). Born Sydney; taught at Sydney, Otago, York; Fellow of
University College, Oxford, from 1967; FBA 1974 (Penguin author note in *Ethics*). Analytic
philosophy; moral error theory. Relevant for the view that morality is a human device with a
definite object — counteracting limited sympathies — and for the projection account of why
morality feels objective. Key ideas: "no objective values"; argument from queerness;
objectification; morality in the narrow sense as a device; game-theoretic structure of
cooperation. Works: *Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong* (1977); *Hume's Moral Theory* (1980);
*The Miracle of Theism* (1982). Reference: Penguin author note (verified in edition);
https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/moral-anti-realism/.

**Peter Railton** (b. 1950). American; Gregory S. Kavka Distinguished University Professor
(emeritus), University of Michigan; PhD Princeton 1980 under David Lewis. Naturalistic moral
realism. Relevant because he shows a naturalist can hold that cruelty and exclusion are
*objectively* wrong and that moral learning tracks real facts. Key ideas: reductive account of
non-moral good via idealized interests; moral rightness as social rationality; wants/interests
feedback; social learning and unrest pushing toward inclusion; moral progress without
guarantee. Works: "Moral Realism" (1986); *Facts, Values, and Norms* (2003); *Homo Prospectus*
(2016, with Seligman, Baumeister, Sripada). Reference: https://lsa.umich.edu/philosophy/people/emeritus-faculty/prailton.html
(listed by search; page returned 403 to the fetcher) and https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Railton.

**Bertrand Russell** (1872–1970). British logician and philosopher; analytic philosophy;
public humanist. Relevant for a humanist framing of man as part of nature, value as created
by human desire and love, and a sober gradualist remedy. Key ideas: man as product of
purposeless causes; refusal to worship power; "good life inspired by love and guided by
knowledge"; no short cut to the good life; intelligence, self-control, sympathy. Works:
"A Free Man's Worship" (1903); *What I Believe* (1925); *Why I Am Not a Christian* (1927);
*Human Society in Ethics and Politics* (1954). Reference: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/russell/.

**Daniel C. Dennett** (1942–2024). American; Tufts University; philosophy of mind,
evolutionary naturalism. Relevant for a naturalist account of agency and responsibility that
keeps persons, reasons and selves real. Key ideas: intentional stance; self as center of
narrative gravity; reasons-giving as a distinctively human layer of control; compatibilist
"free will worth wanting"; competence before comprehension; cultural evolution. Works:
*Elbow Room* (1984); *Consciousness Explained* (1991); *Darwin's Dangerous Idea* (1995);
*Freedom Evolves* (2003). Reference: https://doi.org/10.1196/annals.1279.003 (article read);
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Dennett for dates.

**Philip Kitcher** (b. 1947). British-born American philosopher; John Dewey Professor
Emeritus, Columbia University; philosophy of science and pragmatist naturalist ethics; secular
humanist. Relevant for the most complete philosophical naturalist account of the origin of
ethics and of moral progress. Key ideas: humans as incomplete psychological altruists;
altruism failures; ethics as social technology; progress as functional refinement rather than
discovery of truth; pragmatist (Deweyan) naturalism. Works: *Living with Darwin* (2007);
*The Ethical Project* (2011); *Life After Faith: The Case for Secular Humanism* (2014).
Reference: https://www.hup.harvard.edu/books/9780674284289 ;
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_Kitcher ; précis read at analyse-und-kritik.net.

**Joshua D. Greene** (optional; b. 1974). Harvard psychologist and philosopher; dual-process
moral psychology. Key ideas: automatic settings vs manual mode; moral tribes; tragedy of
commonsense morality. Works: *Moral Tribes* (2013); "Beyond Point-and-Shoot Morality" (2014).
Reference: author's preprint (joshua-greene.net).

## The view

Naturalism explains the human mixture as the expected result of being a clever, social,
partially altruistic animal. Human beings are part of nature, shaped by biological and
cultural evolution, not fallen from a higher state.<Cite source="russell-what-i-believe" locator="ch. 1" />
Darwin argued that any animal with strong social instincts would acquire a conscience once
its intelligence approached ours.<Cite source="darwin-descent" locator="ch. IV, p. 98" />
Love, loyalty, fairness and self-sacrifice are real expressions of those instincts.

Those same instincts are limited. Sympathy evolved for one's own group, not for everyone,<Cite source="darwin-descent" locator="ch. IV, p. 98" />
and it lives alongside self-interest, fear and status-seeking. Hume put it this way: there is
"some particle of the dove" in us, "along with the elements of the wolf and serpent."<Cite source="hume-epm" locator="9.4" />
Knowingly doing wrong needs no special explanation: a strong impulse in the moment
overrides more lasting concerns, and remorse follows.<Cite source="darwin-descent" locator="ch. IV, pp. 113–114" /><Cite source="hume-treatise" locator="2.3.3.10" />

On this view, morality is a set of natural and cultural devices that make up for our
limited sympathies.<Cite source="mackie-ethics" locator="p. 108" /> Naturalists disagree
about whether moral facts are objective. Their remedy is gradual and partial: institutions,
education, knowledge and a widening of sympathy.<Cite source="russell-what-i-believe" locator="ch. 4" />

## What this explains well

Naturalism explains greatness and terribleness from one source, so it predicts the particular
shape they take. Generosity is strongest toward kin and friends. Heroism and cruelty cluster
along group lines. The loyalty that inspires sacrifice in war also fuels hatred of the enemy.<Cite source="darwin-descent" locator="ch. V, p. 130" />
It explains why rudiments of empathy and of coalitionary violence appear in other social
animals. It gives detailed, testable accounts of weakness of will. It explains why conscience
tracks one's community and can be miscalibrated or corrected. And it treats moral progress,
such as the abolition of slavery, as real but fragile: an uneven trend with no guarantee.<Cite source="railton-moral-realism" locator="pp. 193–194" />
Each of these claims answers to evidence from biology, psychology and history.

## Deep dive

#### Which naturalism?

Naturalism has no creed. What is represented here is the mainstream view that human beings
are wholly natural organisms, and that our best knowledge of them is continuous with the
sciences.<Cite source="sep-naturalism" locator="§1; §2" /> Its account of human nature runs
from Hume through Darwin to contemporary evolutionary ethics and moral psychology. That
account is shared by naturalists who disagree sharply about the status of morality, so it is
kept separate from metaethics below. Researchers distinguish three questions: how moral
capacities evolved, what we ought to do, and what moral claims are. Moving from the first
answer to the other two is not straightforward.<Cite source="sep-morality-biology" locator="§1.1" />

#### Hume: the mixed constitution

Hume's "science of man" supplies the basic picture. Reason by itself cannot move us to act or
hold back a passion; only a contrary passion can.<Cite source="hume-treatise" locator="2.3.3.4" />
People rarely love anyone more than themselves, yet in most people the kind affections taken
together outweigh the selfish ones.<Cite source="hume-treatise" locator="3.2.2.5" /> Justice
exists only because human generosity is "confin'd" and goods are scarce.<Cite source="hume-treatise" locator="3.2.2.18" />
The remedy is artifice, the product of judgment and understanding.<Cite source="hume-treatise" locator="3.2.2.9" />
Hume also anticipates the problem of knowing better and doing worse: "Men often act knowingly
against their interest." What we call strength of mind is the calm passions prevailing over
the violent ones.<Cite source="hume-treatise" locator="2.3.3.10" /> Sympathy is "a very
powerful principle in human nature."<Cite source="hume-treatise" locator="3.3.1.10" /> It
becomes impartial moral judgment when we adopt "steady and general points of view."<Cite source="hume-treatise" locator="3.3.1.15" />

#### Darwin: conscience from social instinct

Darwin called the moral sense "the most noble of all the attributes of man." It can lead a
person to risk their life for another.<Cite source="darwin-descent" locator="ch. IV, p. 97" />
He traced it to social instincts combined with memory, reflection and language. He also noted
that those instincts extend "only to those of the same association."<Cite source="darwin-descent" locator="ch. IV, p. 98" />
His account of conscience directly explains acting against one's own judgment. In the moment,
we tend to follow the stronger impulse, which more often serves our own desires at others'
expense. Afterward the enduring social instinct reasserts itself, and remorse, shame and a
resolve to do better follow. "Conscience looks backwards, and serves as a guide for the future."<Cite source="darwin-descent" locator="ch. IV, pp. 113–114" />
Darwin also saw how virtue and conflict could be linked. Groups with more faithful,
courageous and sympathetic members would prevail over rival groups.<Cite source="darwin-descent" locator="ch. V, p. 130" />
Yet "the simplest reason" pushes sympathy from tribe to nation. After that, only "an
artificial barrier" stands in the way of extending it to all peoples.<Cite source="darwin-descent" locator="ch. IV, p. 122" />

#### One source, two faces

Contemporary naturalists sharpen Darwin's picture. Kin selection and reciprocity explain how
dispositions that help others could evolve. This *biological* altruism, defined by effects on
fitness, is distinct from genuine *psychological* care. Evolution can produce the second, so
love is not disguised selfishness.<Cite source="sep-altruism-biological" locator="§5" />
Philip Kitcher, a naturalist philosopher, gives the human condition a compact formula. Our
propensities for altruism committed us to social life, and their limits made that life
troubled.<Cite source="kitcher-ethical-project-precis" locator="p. 10" /> We are "incomplete
psychological altruists."<Cite source="kitcher-ethical-project-precis" locator="p. 18" /> On
his account, ethics began as a "social technology" for remedying failures of altruism. Even
early conscience was assembled partly from fear of peers and of unseen powers.<Cite source="kitcher-ethical-project-precis" locator="p. 4" />
The same norm-following that makes conscience possible can therefore enforce cruelty.
Moral psychologists add a dual-process picture. Fast, automatic responses usually serve us
well but can be overridden, or can override slower reflection, depending on circumstances.<Cite source="greene-point-and-shoot" locator="§I" />
Philosophers of action analyze the resulting failures as incontinence, broken resolutions
and steep discounting of the future.<Cite source="sep-weakness-will" locator="§2; §3.3; §3.4.2" />

#### Agency and the self

Most naturalists are compatibilists. For Hume, liberty is "a power of acting or not acting,
according to the determinations of the will."<Cite source="hume-enquiry" locator="8.23" />
Daniel Dennett argues that what sets humans apart is the practice of "asking and giving
reasons," including to ourselves.<Cite source="dennett-self-artifact" locator="p. 48" /> The
self is a "center of narrative gravity," and moral responsibility belongs to it. It is the
extended self that keeps track of past and future intentions.<Cite source="dennett-self-artifact" locator="p. 49" />
On this view, the inner conflict behind moral failure reflects an agency that is only
partially coherent.<Cite source="dennett-self-artifact" locator="p. 39" />

#### Realists and anti-realists

The same human nature is read in two ways. J. L. Mackie held that "there are no objective
values."<Cite source="mackie-ethics" locator="p. 15" /> On his account, morality feels
objective because we project our attitudes and socially instilled demands onto the world.<Cite source="mackie-ethics" locator="pp. 42–43" />
Even so, morality has a real job: to counteract "limited sympathies."<Cite source="mackie-ethics" locator="p. 108" />
Peter Railton defends naturalistic moral realism. Moral rightness is what would be rationally
approved if the interests of everyone potentially affected were counted equally, with full
information.<Cite source="railton-moral-realism" locator="p. 190" /> Social arrangements that
discount a group's interests generate unrest and feedback that push toward inclusion. The
result is "an uneven secular trend," not a guarantee.<Cite source="railton-moral-realism" locator="pp. 193–194" />
Evolutionary debunkers argue that evolution gives realists reason to doubt their moral
beliefs. Realists reply that much moral belief comes from reflection that evolution did not
fix.<Cite source="sep-morality-biology" locator="§4.1" />

#### Remedy and dignity

Bertrand Russell is typical in refusing utopian shortcuts. "There is no short cut to the good
life, whether individual or social." What is needed is to build up "intelligence,
selfcontrol, and sympathy" by gradual training and experiment. He also conceded that human
nature limits sympathy.<Cite source="russell-what-i-believe" locator="ch. 4" /> The good life
is "inspired by love and guided by knowledge."<Cite source="russell-what-i-believe" locator="ch. 2" />
Naturalists ground dignity in several ways, and the groundings do not all agree:

- in capacities such as reason-giving agency and sentience;
- in the realist demand that everyone's interests count equally;
- in Hume's sentiment of humanity, which extends to all mankind;<Cite source="hume-epm" locator="9.5–9.6" />
- in human valuing, as when Russell says "it is we who create value."<Cite source="russell-what-i-believe" locator="ch. 1" />

Russell's earlier essay grounds dignity in refusing to worship non-human power.<Cite source="russell-mysticism-logic" locator="ch. III, ¶9" />
Most naturalists treat dignity as derived from these sources or achieved over time, rather
than as metaphysically primitive. Realists hold that it is objective all the same.
