## 1. Scope

**Account represented.** Confessional Reformed theology as stated in the Westminster Confession
of Faith (WCF) and Larger Catechism (WLC), expounded chiefly by John Calvin (1559 *Institutes*),
with Augustine as the principal patristic source the Reformed tradition consciously inherits
(Calvin's central distinction in *Inst.* II.2.12 is explicitly "borrowed from the writings of
Augustine"). Herman Bavinck (as interpreter of Calvin on common grace), Jonathan Edwards (will,
conscience, natural vs. true virtue) and Herman Witsius (federal headship) supply specialist
precision. Van Til is used only for one point (the unbeliever's suppressed knowledge under
common grace), and only via a checked secondary source, because his own texts could not be read.

**Why this scope.** The Westminster Standards are the site's confessional norm (docs/SOURCES.md),
and on this question they are specific: creation in the image (WCF 4.2), fall and corruption of
all faculties (6.2–4), the covenant with Adam as public person (7.2; WLC 22), natural liberty with
moral inability (9.1, 9.3), the works of the unregenerate as materially useful yet sinful (16.7),
and renewal by the Spirit (10.1–2; 13). Calvin's *Institutes* I.15 and II.1–3 are the classic
Reformed treatment of exactly the phenomenon in the research question: a creature of
"admirable gifts" whose nature is nevertheless a "ruin".

## 2. Anthropology: what a human being fundamentally is

- A **creature**: wholly dependent, "our very being is nothing else than subsistence in God alone"
  (Calvin I.1.1, R-ed Beveridge). Body from the dust, soul an immortal spirit; the dust is "a curb
  … on his pride" while the spirit is ground for glorying in God's liberality (I.15.1, R-ed).
- **Made in the image of God**, male and female (Gen 1:26–27, R-ed ESV), "endued with knowledge,
  righteousness, and true holiness … having the law of God written in their hearts, and power to
  fulfill it: and yet under a possibility of transgressing" (WCF 4.2, R-ed OPC; cf. WLC 17).
- For Calvin the image "extends to everything in which the nature of man surpasses that of all
  other species of animals", its primary seat being "the mind and the heart, or in the soul and its
  powers", yet with "rays of glory" even in the body (I.15.3, R-ed). Its content is learned from its
  renewal in Christ: knowledge, righteousness, holiness (I.15.4; Col 3:10; Eph 4:24).
- A **covenantal** creature: made for communion with God, with whom fruition is possible only by
  God's "voluntary condescension … by way of covenant" (WCF 7.1). Adam stood as "a public person,
  not for himself only, but for his posterity" (WLC 22).
- A **religious** creature by constitution: a *sensus divinitatis* is "indelibly engraven on the
  human heart"; all are "born and live for the express purpose of learning to know God" (Calvin
  I.3.1, I.3.3, R-ed).
- Faculties: intellect (to discern good and evil) and will (choice), originally with the will
  "perfectly submissive to the authority of reason" (I.15.7–8, R-ed). Augustine and Edwards place
  the decisive centre in the will's loves/inclination ("the heart"), which the Reformed tradition
  follows (CoG XIV.6–7; Edwards, *True Virtue* ch. 1).

## 3. Metaphysical context

- **Creator–creature distinction**: God creates *ex nihilo* "for the manifestation of the glory of
  his eternal power, wisdom, and goodness" (WCF 4.1). Man is not a fragment of the divine nor a
  product of impersonal process; he images a personal God.
- **Providence**: God upholds and governs all creatures and actions (WCF 5.1), including sin, "not
  by a bare permission" but by bounding and ordering it, "yet so, as the sinfulness thereof
  proceedeth only from the creature" (WCF 5.4). This is the metaphysical basis of *restraint*.
- **Evil as privation, not substance**: Augustine — vices of the soul "are nothing but privations of
  natural good" (Enchiridion 11, R-ed NPNF/New Advent); "nothing can be evil except something
  which is good", so "an evil man" is "an evil good" (Ench. 13). The evil will has no efficient
  but a "deficient" cause (CoG XII.7). Calvin: corruption is "an adventitious event which befell
  man, [not] a substantial property assigned to him from the beginning" (II.1.11, R-ed). This
  excludes Manichaean dualism (two natures/two creators), which Augustine and Calvin both name and
  reject (Conf. VIII.10.22–24; Inst. II.1.11).
- **Revelation**: general revelation (creation, providence, the human constitution, conscience)
  leaves men "unexcusable" but is insufficient for saving knowledge (WCF 1.1; 21.1).

## 4. Why humans possess remarkable capacities

1. **Creation in the image.** Reason, freedom, moral sense, creativity and dominion are created
   endowments (Gen 1:26–28; Ps 8:4–6; WCF 4.2). Calvin calls man "a microcosm … a rare specimen of
   divine power, wisdom, and goodness" (I.5.3, R-ed).
2. **The natural gifts survive the Fall.** "Since reason … is a natural gift, it could not be
   entirely destroyed"; "the will, because inseparable from the nature of man, did not perish"
   (II.2.12, R-ed). Augustine: in condemning human nature God "did not withdraw all that He had
   given it, else it had been annihilated" (CoG XXII.24, R-ed); "the little spark of reason, which
   was the image of God in him, has not been quite quenched" (ibid.).
3. **Earthly vs. heavenly knowledge.** Fallen reason is competent, often brilliant, in "things
   terrestrial" — "policy and economy, all mechanical arts and liberal studies" — and blind in
   "heavenly things" (knowledge of God's fatherly favour and true righteousness) (II.2.13, 18, R-ed).
   Every person has "impressions of civil order and honesty"; quarrels about particular laws do not
   destroy "the primary idea of justice" (II.2.13).
4. **The Spirit as source of all truth (common grace).** "If we reflect that the Spirit of God is
   the only fountain of truth, we will be careful … not to reject or condemn truth wherever it
   appears" (II.2.15, R-ed). The Spirit dwells as sanctifier only in believers, but "fills, moves,
   and invigorates all things" according to the nature of each, and gives skill as to Bezalel
   (Exod 31:2–3) (II.2.16, R-ed). Variety of talents is God's gift, "special inspiration" in
   distinguished acts; "in this diversity we can trace some remains of the divine image" (II.2.17).
   Bavinck's 1909 exposition: the Spirit "as a spirit of life, of wisdom and of power … works also in
   those who do not believe" (Calvin and Common Grace, p. 119, R-ed).
5. **Love, pity, gratitude, conscience as natural principles.** Edwards: natural affection, pity,
   gratitude and conscience are real, God-ordered principles that "for the most part, tend to the
   good of mankind" and "restrain vice", though they are not true virtue (*True Virtue* ch. 7, R-ed).

## 5. Why humans behave destructively or wrongly

1. **The Fall.** Adam's sin was at root unbelief, pride and ingratitude — "a son of earth" deeming
   it "little to have been made in the likeness, unless he were also made the equal of God"
   (Calvin II.1.4, R-ed). Augustine: the evil act was preceded by an evil will, and "what is the
   origin of our evil will but pride? … when the soul abandons Him to whom it ought to cleave as its
   end, and becomes a kind of end to itself" (CoG XIV.13, R-ed).
2. **Original sin**: "a hereditary corruption and depravity of our nature, extending to all the
   parts of the soul" (II.1.8), conveyed to all Adam's posterity (WCF 6.3; WLC 26), from which "do
   proceed all actual transgressions" (WCF 6.4). It is not idle: "a lighted furnace sends forth sparks
   and flames" (II.1.8).
3. **Disordered love.** Two cities "formed by two loves: the earthly by the love of self, even to the
   contempt of God" (CoG XIV.28, R-ed). Vices are perverse imitations of God — pride imitates
   exaltation, cruelty of the powerful wishes "to be feared", curiosity "affects a desire for
   knowledge" (Conf. II.6.13, R-ed). This links domination, deceit and disordered desire to a single
   root: the self put in God's place.
4. **Evil loved for itself.** The pear theft: "I loved to perish. I loved my own error — not that
   for which I erred, but the error itself" (Conf. II.4.9, R-ed); partly a perverse imitation of
   omnipotence, "doing with impunity things which I was not allowed to do" (II.6.14), and partly the
   pull of companionship (II.8.16). Augustine also notes that ordinary crimes usually have a
   motive in some lesser good (II.5.11) — the pear theft is presented as the limit case.
5. **Knowingly acting against one's own judgment.**
   - Rom 1:18–21: those who "knew God" did not honour him and "by their unrighteousness suppress the
     truth" (R-ed ESV). Calvin I.5.4: people "cannot but know" their gifts are proofs of God, "and
     yet they inwardly suppress them" — even using "the seed of Deity deposited in human nature as a
     means of suppressing the name of God" (R-ed).
   - Rom 2:14–15: the work of the law written on hearts; conscience accusing/excusing (R-ed ESV).
     Calvin: natural law is "the judgment of conscience distinguishing sufficiently between just and
     unjust", whose end "is to render man inexcusable"; he rejects Plato's claim that sins are
     committed only through ignorance (II.2.22, R-ed).
   - Two modes (II.2.23, R-ed): (a) Themistius' account — the rule is held in the abstract but
     forgotten "when he comes to the particular" (the murderer deliberates "as if the thing was
     good"); (b) but sometimes "the sinner does not impose upon himself by a false semblance of good,
     but rushes into sin knowingly and willingly", citing Ovid's Medea ("I see the better course, and
     approve it: I follow the worse") and Aristotle's incontinence/intemperance distinction.
     II.2.13: lawbreakers "licentiously hate what their mind and understanding approve".
   - Augustine's divided will (Conf. VIII.9.21, R-ed): "the mind commands itself, and is resisted …
     it wills not entirely"; a chain: "a perverse will … lust … custom … necessity" (VIII.5.10).
   - Edwards (Freedom of the Will I.4, R-ed): moral inability "consists in the opposition or want of
     inclination"; "reason may resist present acts of the Will, and its resistance be insufficient";
     one "may be in miserable slavery and bondage to a strong habit."
   - Rom 7:15–25: see §12 — Calvin reads it of the regenerate; not used as primary evidence for
     unbelievers.
6. **Self-flattery and self-deception.** "There is nothing more acceptable to the human mind than
   flattery"; "innate self-love by which all are blinded" (Calvin II.1.2, R-ed). Edwards: conscience
   can be dulled — "by long acting from the selfish principle … men … lose entirely the sense of ill
   desert" (*True Virtue* ch. 7, R-ed).
7. **Judicial hardening.** God may give the wicked "over to their own lusts" and withdraw gifts
   (WCF 5.6); WLC 28 lists "blindness of mind … hardness of heart, horror of conscience, and vile
   affections" as inward punishments of sin. Self-destruction is thus partly sin's own penalty.

## 6. Do greatness and wickedness share a common explanation?

Yes — this is the structural centre of the Reformed answer. Four elements:

1. **One created nature, not two.** Corruption is "adventitious", not "a substantial property"
   (II.1.11); evil exists only in a good subject (Ench. 13). There is no evil faculty; there are good
   faculties wrongly ordered. Hence wickedness is parasitic on greatness.
2. **Corrupted, not annihilated.** Natural gifts corrupted, supernatural gifts withdrawn (II.2.12);
   the image is not "utterly effaced" but "a ruin, confused, mutilated, and tainted with impurity"
   (I.15.4, Beveridge). The same reason that builds is the reason that schemes; the same freedom that
   loves is the will that dominates.
3. **The same gifts become the instruments and pretexts of sin.** Calvin I.5.4: human excellence is
   made "a pretext for denying that there is a God". Augustine CoG XXII.24: the genius that
   invented weaving, building, agriculture, navigation, music and astronomy also invented "poisons,
   weapons, engines of destruction"; and XXII.22's catalogue of crimes springs "from that root of
   error and misplaced love which is born with every son of Adam".
4. **Restrained and gifted by providence.** The worst does not always appear because God "lays
   them under such restraint as may prevent them from breaking forth"; some are held back "only by
   shame, others by a fear of the laws" (II.3.3); and outstanding virtue in a Camillus is a "special
   gift of God", not a proof of an uncorrupted nature (II.3.4).

So the "great and terrible" combination is predicted by: *created dignity* + *radical but non-
substantial corruption* + *providential restraint and endowment* (+ *partial renewal* in the
regenerate, who therefore show the conflict most sharply — WCF 13.2).

## 7. Rationality; moral knowledge; conscience; evil/sin; agency; desire

- **Rationality.** Retained, "partly weakened and partly corrupted" (II.2.12); competent in earthly
  things, blind in heavenly (II.2.13–18). Love of truth is natural but "fails before it reaches the
  goal" (II.2.12). Van Til (via Frame): the unbeliever has knowledge of God by creation, suppressed
  by his "principle of autonomy", which is in turn suppressed by common grace, so that "the ideas
  with which he daily works do not proceed consistently" from either (Frame, WTJ 1995, quoting
  *Defense of the Faith* 3rd ed., p. 170 — the Van Til page was NOT read by this researcher).
- **Moral knowledge.** Second-table duties known far better than first-table (worship, trust in
  God); concupiscence (inner desire) "altogether escapes our animadversion" (II.2.24). So moral
  knowledge is real but selective, strongest where society needs it.
- **Conscience.** Rom 2:15; Calvin II.2.22. Edwards ch. 5: natural conscience is a disposition "to
  be uneasy in a consciousness of being inconsistent with himself" (doing to others what one would
  resent); distinct from true virtue but able to "justify God's anger and condemnation". Calvin I.3.2:
  even the most audacious despiser of God is haunted by conscience.
- **Evil/sin.** "Any want of conformity unto, or transgression of, any law of God" (WLC 24).
  Privation (Ench. 11). Pride as root (CoG XIV.13). Original and actual sin (WCF 6.4–6).
- **Agency.** Natural liberty: the will "neither forced, nor, by any absolute necessity of nature,
  determined to good, or evil" (WCF 9.1). But the fallen will "hath wholly lost all ability of will
  to any spiritual good accompanying salvation" (9.3). Calvin's necessity vs. compulsion: man "sins
  not forced or unwilling, but voluntarily … and yet … cannot move and act except in the direction of
  evil" (II.3.5, R-ed); citing Bernard: "guilty because free, and enslaved because guilty". Augustine:
  "by the evil use of his free-will … man destroyed both it and himself" (Ench. 30). Edwards: natural
  inability (cannot if one would) excuses; moral inability (will not, from contrary inclination) does
  not (FW I.4).
- **Desire/affections.** The will's loves are decisive (CoG XIV.7, 28). Augustine's "custom not
  resisted became necessity" (Conf. VIII.5.10). Edwards: all sin "has its source from selfishness, or
  from self-love, not subordinate to a regard to being in general" (*True Virtue* ch. 7).

## 8. Can the condition be remedied?

- **Not from within.** The natural man "is not able, by his own strength, to convert himself, or to
  prepare himself thereunto" (WCF 9.3). Augustine's analogy: a suicide "cannot restore himself to
  life" (Ench. 30). Calvin: the mind's highest part is "so corrupted, that mere cure is not
  sufficient. There must be a new nature" (II.1.9). John 3:3; Eph 2:1–5.
- **By God in Christ.** Effectual calling by Word and Spirit renews the will, "yet so, as they come
  most freely, being made willing by his grace" (WCF 10.1); man "altogether passive therein" until
  quickened (10.2). Restoration of the image: Col 3:10; Eph 4:24; 2 Cor 3:18; Calvin I.15.4 ("partly
  seen in the elect … Its full lustre … in heaven").
- **Gradual and contested in this life.** Sanctification "imperfect in this life … a continual and
  irreconcilable war" (WCF 13.2); the will "made perfectly and immutably free to good alone, in the
  state of glory only" (9.5).
- **Fourfold state** (implicit in WCF 9.2–5): innocence (able to sin and not to sin), fallen (not
  able not to sin, in the spiritual sense), regenerate (able to do good, not perfectly), glorified
  (unable to sin). Augustinian in origin; the WCF wording is verified, the Augustinian formula
  (*posse non peccare* etc., *De correptione et gratia* 33) was NOT read here.

## 9. Dignity: inherent or derived?

**Derived but inalienable.** Dignity is grounded in God's act of creating man in his image, not in
the person's present virtue, capacities or performance. It therefore persists after the Fall:

- Gen 9:6 — after the Flood, murder is forbidden "for God made man in his own image" (R-ed ESV).
- Jas 3:9 — cursing people "who are made in the likeness of God" (present tense, of fallen people).
- Calvin III.7.6 (R-ed): "we are not to look to what men in themselves deserve, but to attend to the
  image of God, which exists in all, and to which we owe all honour and love"; even the enemy, because
  that image "by its beauty and dignity" should "allure us to love". Note that the same chapter
  presupposes that most people, "if estimated by their own merit, are most unworthy".
- Tension to state honestly: Calvin can call the remaining image a "fearful deformity"/"ruin"
  (I.15.4) and also say it "exists in all" and grounds honour (III.7.6). The Reformed resolution is
  that the dignity belongs to what God made and still claims, not to what the sinner has made of it.
  "Inherent" is acceptable only in the sense "inheres in every human being"; it is not
  self-originated.

## 10. What this worldview genuinely explains well

(Honest assessment; claims of fit, not proof.)

1. **Both data are taken at full strength.** The account does not need to downgrade human dignity
   (to illusion or mere utility) or human evil (to ignorance, maladaptation or social conditioning
   alone). Calvin explicitly rejects the Platonic ignorance thesis (II.2.22).
2. **Evil's parasitism on excellence.** It predicts that the most terrible evils will require the
   highest capacities — intelligence, organisation, rhetoric, loyalty — because evil is good power
   misdirected (Ench. 13; CoG XXII.24).
3. **Knowing wrongdoing.** It has a ready category for akrasia and for the stronger phenomenon of
   choosing the worse while approving the better (II.2.23; Conf. VIII.9.21), and for self-deception
   (II.2.23 via Themistius; II.1.2).
4. **Universality with variation.** Universal corruption (Rom 3; WCF 6.4) plus unequal restraint
   and gifts (II.3.3–4) fits both the ubiquity of moral failure and the existence of admirable
   non-Christians, without either denying the admirable or making it a counter-example.
5. **The seriousness of the remedy matches the diagnosis.** If the problem is in the will's loves,
   education or technique cannot cure it; renewal must reach the "heart" (II.1.9; WCF 10.1).
6. **Dignity without merit.** Dignity grounded in the image survives the worst conduct (Gen 9:6;
   III.7.6), giving a principled basis for honouring the criminal, the disabled and the enemy.
7. **Internal integration.** Image, fall, common grace and redemption are one storyline; renewal is
   defined by the original image (Col 3:10), so the "greatness" is both memory and prophecy.

Honest limits (to be developed in the objection/reply, not here): the origin of the first evil
will is declared to have no efficient cause (CoG XII.7) — an explanatory terminus; federal headship
presupposes a historical Adam and a covenant some find morally opaque; the restraint doctrine can
look unfalsifiable if invoked post hoc.

## 11. Strongest representative thinkers for this question

- **Augustine** — supplies the categories the Reformed answer runs on: privation, the evil will as
  defect, pride, two loves, the divided will, evil loved for itself; and the juxtaposition of human
  genius and misery (CoG XXII.22–24).
- **John Calvin** — the precise Reformed formulation: image as ruin; natural gifts corrupted /
  supernatural withdrawn; earthly vs. heavenly knowledge; Spirit as fountain of truth; restraint;
  conscience and knowing sin; necessity without compulsion; image as ground of love.
- **Jonathan Edwards** (specialist) — the sharpest account of *why* the will does what reason
  disapproves (natural vs. moral inability) and of natural virtue vs. true virtue (civil goodness).
- **Herman Bavinck** — the Reformed tradition's own systematic interpreter of Calvin's common grace
  (1909); his *Reformed Dogmatics* would add the "whole person is the image" emphasis (not verified).
- **Herman Witsius** — clearest classical statement of federal headship and Rom 5:12 (I.8.30–35).
- **Van Til** (optional, via Frame) — the image-bearer who knows God, suppresses that knowledge and
  is restrained by common grace; useful because it states the epistemic form of the paradox.

Not recommended for this answer: Turretin (relevant, especially Topic IX on sin and Topic X on free
will, but the Giger/Dennison text could not be read); Owen (relevant to the believer's indwelling sin
and Rom 7, but secondary for this question and not read); Vos, Bahnsen (not specifically needed);
Aquinas/Athanasius/Anselm (interlocutors; not needed); Pascal (not Reformed; omitted).

## 12. Uncertainties and contested interpretations

1. **Romans 7:14–25.** Calvin reads it of "a man already regenerated" and says the conflict "does not
   exist in man before he is renewed" (comm. Rom 7:14–15, R-ed); Augustine in his anti-Pelagian
   period defends the same reading (*Against Two Letters of the Pelagians* I, NPNF chs. 14–24,
   R-ed). Augustine's earlier reading differed — NOT verified here. Many modern exegetes read the "I"
   as the person under the law, Israel or Adam. The draft therefore uses Rom 7 only for the
   believer's division and grounds unbelievers' knowing wrongdoing on Rom 1–2.
2. **Content of the image.** Calvin rejects dominion as the image (I.15.4); much modern Old Testament
   scholarship reads Gen 1:26–28 functionally/royally. Reformed theologians differ in emphasis
   (substantive, relational, functional). Not decisive for this question.
3. **Historical Adam.** Federal headship as confessed (WCF 6.3; 7.2; WLC 22) presupposes Adam as a
   historical person and covenant head. This is the confessional position; it is disputed in
   contemporary evangelical and Reformed scholarship. Mark as the confessional view.
4. **Common grace terminology** (see §1). The draft uses "common grace" with a note on the 1924
   dissent.
5. **Van Til's consistency.** Frame argues Van Til sometimes writes as if the unbeliever knows no
   truth at all, contrary to his own common-grace statements (Frame, WTJ 1995). Use Van Til only for
   the "suppressed knowledge restrained by common grace" formulation.
6. **Edwards and the Confession on freedom.** Edwards' "will is always determined by the strongest
   motive" is a compatibilist determinism; whether WCF 9.1's "natural liberty" and 5.2's "freely, or
   contingently" require or permit it is debated among Reformed philosophers. The draft uses only
   Edwards' natural/moral inability distinction, which is widely shared.
7. **"Total depravity."** Not a confessional phrase in the WCF; the Confession says "wholly
   defiled in all the parts and faculties" (6.2) and "opposite to all good" (6.4), and WLC 25 adds
   "spiritually good". The draft explains the extensive (every faculty) vs. intensive (maximal)
   distinction using these texts rather than the slogan.
8. **Beveridge vs. Battles.** All Calvin quotations are Beveridge (1845). The registry's existing
   `calvin-institutes` (McNeill/Battles) was not read; locators are shared but wording differs
   (e.g. Beveridge's "ruin" in I.15.4). Propose registering Beveridge separately (done in §13).
9. **NPNF dating.** New Advent's source lines give 1887 for NPNF¹ vols. 1–3, 5; library records for
   vol. 1 vary (1886). Record as New Advent states and note the discrepancy.
10. **Calvin II.1.8 and imputation.** Calvin's sentence "This is not liability for another's fault"
    is read by mediate-imputation advocates as support and by immediate-imputation advocates as
    concerning the *ground* in inherent pollution alongside imputation. Do not cite it for either side.

## 15. Thinker profile notes

### Augustine of Hippo (354–430) — existing id `augustine`, primary, displayOrder 1
- **Dates/setting:** Born Thagaste, Roman Africa, 13 Nov 354; converted summer 386; baptised by
  Ambrose Easter 387; priest at Hippo Regius 391, bishop c. 396; died 28 Aug 430.
- **School:** Patristic Latin theology; the Augustinian tradition Reformed theology claims as
  antecedent (Calvin repeatedly cites him in *Inst.* II.1–3).
- **Why relevant:** Supplies the conceptual grammar of the Reformed answer — evil as privation,
  the will's defection and loves, the divided will, the bondage of the will after the fall, and the
  coexistence of human genius and misery.
- **Key ideas:** Evil as privation of good; the evil will has a deficient, not efficient, cause;
  pride as the beginning of sin; two loves and two cities; the divided will and the chain of habit;
  grace as the only remedy for a will that has destroyed its own freedom.
- **Representative works:** *Confessions* (c. 396–400); *City of God* (412–426); *Enchiridion*;
  anti-Pelagian treatises (e.g. *Against Two Letters of the Pelagians*).
- **Reference URL:** https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/augustine/ (dates, conversion, career,
  composition dates); primary texts as above.
- **Draft bio (≈110 words):** Augustine was born at Thagaste in Roman North Africa in 354. Trained in
  rhetoric, he taught in Carthage, Rome and Milan, passed through Manichaeism and Neoplatonism, and
  was converted to Christianity in 386 and baptised by Ambrose in 387. Ordained priest at Hippo Regius
  in 391, he became its bishop around 396 and served there until his death in 430. His *Confessions*
  analyse his own will and loves before God; *The City of God*, begun after the sack of Rome in 410,
  interprets history as the story of two loves. His controversies with the Manichees and the
  Pelagians shaped Western teaching on evil, sin and grace.
- **Significance:** For this site Augustine is the main patristic antecedent of Reformed anthropology:
  will, grace, evil as privation, the two cities.

### John Calvin (1509–1564) — existing id `calvin`, primary, displayOrder 2
- **Dates/setting:** Born Noyon, Picardy, 10 July 1509; legal studies at Orléans and Bourges; converted
  c. 1533 (date debated); first *Institutes* Basel 1536; Geneva 1536–38, Strasbourg 1538–41, Geneva
  from 1541; final Latin *Institutes* 1559; died Geneva 27 May 1564.
- **School:** Reformed (Genevan) theology.
- **Why relevant:** The classic Reformed statement of the "ruined image": created excellence, fall,
  natural gifts corrupted / supernatural withdrawn, common grace and restraint, conscience, and dignity
  grounded in the image.
- **Key ideas:** Knowledge of God and of ourselves; *sensus divinitatis*; image of God as ruin;
  natural vs. supernatural gifts; the Spirit as sole fountain of truth; necessity without compulsion.
- **Representative works:** *Institutes of the Christian Religion* (1536; final Latin 1559);
  commentaries (Romans, 1540).
- **Reference URL:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Calvin (used for dates only; reviewer should
  confirm against a scholarly biography, e.g. Gordon, *Calvin* (Yale, 2009) — not read).
- **Draft bio (≈95 words):** John Calvin was born at Noyon in Picardy in 1509 and trained in law at
  Orléans and Bourges before turning to the Reformation in the early 1530s. The first edition of his
  *Institutes of the Christian Religion* appeared at Basel in 1536. After an initial period in Geneva
  (1536–38) and three years as minister to French refugees in Strasbourg, he returned to Geneva in
  1541, where he worked as pastor, preacher and commentator until his death in 1564. The final Latin
  *Institutes* (1559) became the most influential systematic statement of Reformed doctrine.

### Herman Bavinck (1854–1921) — existing id `bavinck`, primary, displayOrder 5
- **Dates/setting:** Born Hoogeveen 13 Dec 1854; studied at Kampen then Leiden (doctorate 1880, on

  Kuyper at the Free University of Amsterdam; *Gereformeerde Dogmatiek* 1895–1901; died Amsterdam 29

## The Christian view

Reformed theology explains the doubleness of human beings by one history of one nature: created good, fallen, preserved, and restored in Christ. God made humanity in his image, "endued with knowledge, righteousness, and true holiness," with his law written on the heart.<Cite source="wcf" locator="4.2" /> By Adam's sin the race fell from original righteousness and communion with God and became "wholly defiled in all the parts and faculties of soul and body."<Cite source="wcf" locator="6.2" /> Yet the faculties were not destroyed. Calvin, following Augustine, holds that the natural gifts were corrupted while the supernatural gifts were withdrawn: reason and will remain, damaged and misdirected.<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="II.2.12" /> So fallen people still discover truths, practise arts and frame just laws, which Calvin traces to God's Spirit as "the only fountain of truth,"<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="II.2.15" /> while God's providence restrains corruption from breaking out to its full extent.<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="II.3.3" /> Wickedness is not a second nature beside the good one; it is good powers ruled by a love of self "even to the contempt of God."<Cite source="augustine-city-of-god" locator="XIV.28" /> Because the disorder lies in what the will loves, the remedy is not self-improvement but renewal "after the image of its creator."<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Colossians 3:10" />

## What this explains well

The account takes both halves of the phenomenon at full strength. Dignity is not reduced to an illusion, and evil is not reduced to ignorance: Calvin denies that people sin only through ignorance and notes that some see the better course and follow the worse.<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="II.2.23" /> It explains why the worst evils feed on the best capacities; Augustine observes that the same inventiveness that produced building, music and astronomy also devised poisons and "engines of destruction."<Cite source="augustine-city-of-god" locator="XXII.24" /> It accounts for universality together with variation, because a corruption common to all is restrained and gifted unequally.<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="II.3.4" /> And because dignity rests on God's image rather than on performance, it survives the worst conduct: after the Flood the prohibition of murder is grounded in the image.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Genesis 9:6" />

## Deep dive

#### Scope

This answer represents confessional Reformed theology as stated in the Westminster Standards and expounded by Calvin, with Augustine as the patristic source Calvin explicitly follows. Reformed theologians differ over the mechanism by which Adam's sin reaches his descendants and over what to call God's gifts to unbelievers; those differences, noted at the end, change the grounding more than the answer.

#### A ruined image, not a replaced one

Calvin defines the image by what Christ restores — knowledge, righteousness and holiness — and holds that after the fall it was "not utterly effaced and destroyed" but survives as a ruin, partly renewed in believers and complete only in heaven.<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="I.15.4" /> The corruption is real but not substantial: man is "corrupted by a natural viciousness, but not by one which proceeded from nature," a calamity that befell human nature rather than a property God gave it.<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="II.1.11" /> Augustine had put the same point metaphysically. Vices are privations of natural good,<Cite source="augustine-enchiridion" locator="11" /> and since evil can exist only in something good, "an evil man" is "an evil good."<Cite source="augustine-enchiridion" locator="13" /> What is wicked in a person is therefore always something God made, wrongly ordered.

This is why the Reformed doctrine of corruption is extensive rather than maximal. The Larger Catechism says fallen nature is opposite "unto all that is spiritually good,"<Cite source="westminster-larger-catechism" locator="Q. 25" /> and the Confession allows that works of unregenerate people may be things God commands and "of good use both to themselves and others," while denying that they please God, because they do not proceed from faith or aim at his glory.<Cite source="wcf" locator="16.7" /> Every faculty is affected; not every act is as bad as it could be.

#### The same gifts as instruments of sin

Calvin presses the paradox hardest in his account of natural knowledge of God. People "cannot but know" that their own gifts are proofs of God, "and yet they inwardly suppress them," some even using "the seed of Deity" in human nature to deny God.<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="I.5.4" /> This follows Paul's account of those who "knew God" yet did not honor him and "suppress the truth."<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 1:18–21" /> Augustine sets the two catalogues side by side. One lists the crimes that spring "from that root of error and misplaced love which is born with every son of Adam";<Cite source="augustine-city-of-god" locator="XXII.22" /> the other lists the arts, which show the goodness God did not withdraw from a condemned nature, "else it had been annihilated."<Cite source="augustine-city-of-god" locator="XXII.24" /> Sin, on this view, is pride: the soul "abandons Him to whom it ought to cleave as its end, and becomes a kind of end to itself."<Cite source="augustine-city-of-god" locator="XIV.13" />

#### Knowing the better and doing the worse

For Calvin the natural law written on the heart (Rom 2:14–15) is "the judgment of conscience," given to leave people without excuse; he rejects Plato's view that wrongdoing comes only from ignorance.<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="II.2.22" /> He distinguishes two cases. Often the general rule is held while the particular case is misjudged, so the man plotting murder deliberates "as if the thing was good." But sometimes the sinner "rushes into sin knowingly and willingly," and Calvin cites Ovid's Medea and Aristotle's distinction between incontinence and intemperance.<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="II.2.23" />

Augustine describes the inner shape of this. He stole pears he did not want: "It was foul, and I loved it."<Cite source="augustine-confessions" locator="II.4.9" /> At the threshold of conversion he found that "the mind commands itself, and is resisted," because "it wills not entirely";<Cite source="augustine-confessions" locator="VIII.9.21" /> a perverse will had become lust, lust custom, and custom necessity.<Cite source="augustine-confessions" locator="VIII.5.10" /> Edwards gives the analytical version: moral inability is not a lack of faculties but "the opposition or want of inclination," so that "reason may resist present acts of the Will, and its resistance be insufficient."<Cite source="edwards-works-hickman-1" locator="Freedom of the Will I.4" />

Romans 7:15–25 is often read in this connection, but with care. Calvin reads it of the regenerate and says the conflict it describes "does not exist in man before he is renewed," although the ungodly feel the stings of conscience.<Cite source="calvin-commentary-romans" locator="Romans 7:15" /> In Reformed use it describes the believer's continuing war with remaining corruption.<Cite source="wcf" locator="13.2" /> Many modern interpreters read the passage differently.

#### Restraint and civil virtue

Calvin is generous about unbelievers' achievements in law, philosophy, medicine and mathematics, and warns that "in despising the gifts, we insult the Giver."<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="II.2.15" /> The Spirit dwells as sanctifier only in believers, yet "fills, moves, and invigorates all things."<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="II.2.16" /> God holds corruption in check through shame, fear of the laws and self-interest, "yet without rendering it inwardly pure."<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="II.3.3" /> Bavinck, expounding Calvin, calls this the common grace by which God "immediately after the Fall interposed" to curb sin and preserve the world,<Cite source="bavinck-calvin-common-grace" locator="p. 118" /> the Spirit working in unbelievers "as a spirit of life, of wisdom and of power."<Cite source="bavinck-calvin-common-grace" locator="p. 119" /> Edwards adds a moral psychology: natural pity, gratitude and conscience restrain vice and generally serve human good, yet they are not true virtue, which is love to God and to being in general.<Cite source="edwards-works-hickman-1" locator="True Virtue ch. 7" />

#### Adam, freedom and remedy

The universality of corruption is explained by Adam's place as "the root of all mankind," whose guilt was imputed and whose corrupted nature was conveyed to his descendants.<Cite source="wcf" locator="6.3" /> Witsius reads "all sinned" in Romans 5:12 as Adam's covenantal act counted to his posterity, the counterpart of Christ's obedience counted to believers.<Cite source="witsius-economy-covenants" locator="I.8.31" /> Inability does not remove agency. The will is "neither forced, nor, by any absolute necessity of nature, determined,"<Cite source="wcf" locator="9.1" /> and Calvin distinguishes necessity from compulsion: fallen man sins "not forced or unwilling, but voluntarily."<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="II.3.5" /> But he cannot "convert himself, or … prepare himself thereunto."<Cite source="wcf" locator="9.3" /> Renewal is God's work, by which people "come most freely, being made willing by his grace."<Cite source="wcf" locator="10.1" />

Dignity follows the same logic. Calvin tells his readers not to weigh what people deserve but "to attend to the image of God, which exists in all, and to which we owe all honour and love," even in an enemy.<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="III.7.6" />

#### Where Reformed theologians differ

In 1924 the Christian Reformed Church affirmed God's restraint of sin and the civil good of unbelievers as common grace; Herman Hoeksema and others refused, denying that God's gifts to the non-elect are grace, and formed the Protestant Reformed Churches. Van Til affirmed common grace and described the unbeliever's knowledge of God as suppressed by a principle of autonomy, which common grace in turn restrains.<Cite source="frame-van-til-antithesis" locator="ethics of knowledge" /> Seventeenth-century Reformed theologians also debated whether Adam's guilt is imputed immediately or only through inherited corruption. These debates affect how the universality and the restraint of sin are grounded; none removes the basic structure of image, fall, restraint and renewal.
