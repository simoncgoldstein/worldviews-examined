# Steelman research packet: Classical Islam (`islam`) on `great-and-terrible`

Question: *Why is man so great and so terrible?* Lane: Classical Islam. Steelman only; no Christian
critique is formulated here, and no Christian or polemical literature was used as evidence of what
Islam holds. Prepared 2026-10-05.

## 1. Scope

**Default representation: mainstream Sunni thought.** For this question the most developed and
widely received Sunni account of the human condition is the synthesis of Ash'ari kalam and Sufi moral
psychology in al-Ghazali's *Ihya' 'Ulum al-Din*. It is the principal voice of the packet because:

1. The *Ihya'* contains an explicit, systematic answer to almost exactly this question. Book 21
   ("Marvels of the Heart") explains that "lordly" and "demonic" qualities coexist in every heart.
   Book 22 explains how character is formed, deformed and reformed. Book 31 ("Repentance") explains
   why all humans fall and how they return.
2. It is the most widely read ethical-psychological work in Sunni Islam (SEP, al-Ghazali §5), so it
   is representative without being idiosyncratic.

Strands the answer should also represent, because they change the answer materially:

- **Maturidi kalam** (dominant among Hanafis, and so for a very large share of Sunnis historically).
  It holds that reason can know good and evil and that human choice (*ikhtiyar*) is real. This moves
  the explanation of wrongdoing further toward responsible choice against known good.
- **Ibn Taymiyya (traditionalist / Athari-Hanbali).** He has the strongest account of *fitra* as an
  innate disposition to know, love and worship God, which includes basic moral intuitions. He also
  holds that humans *necessarily* love and worship something, which gives a single explanation of
  both devotion and idolatry.
- **Avicennan philosophy**, briefly. Human greatness is the rational soul's capacity for intellectual
  perfection, and evil is privation. Islamic philosophy is not identical with kalam (SOURCES.md
  caution), so it is labelled separately.
- **Ibn Arabi**, as a labelled specialist voice only. Humans are created "in the form of"
  the all-comprehensive divine name and so carry every possibility, high and low. His status is
  contested in Sunni thought.

Represented only as a contrast: the **Mu'tazila**, whose rationalist ethics frame the Ash'ari and
Maturidi positions.

Not represented: modern reformist or modernist theology. **Shi'a** theology is mentioned only where it
matters (section 10). Imami theology broadly shares the "justice-centred" ('Adliyya) rational ethics
of the Mu'tazila, but this does not change the core anthropology (fitra, no inherited guilt, tawba).
That characterization was not verified against a source in this research, so it should not go into
the answer without a citation.

## 2. Anthropology: what a human being fundamentally is

**The Qur'anic baseline (shared by all strands):**

- A creature fashioned from clay into whom God breathed "from My Spirit" (15:29; 32:7–9).
- Given "hearing, sight, and minds" (32:9; 16:78).
- Made a *khalifa* on earth (2:30). Abdel Haleem translates "successor". The meaning is contested
  (section 13).
- Taught "all the names" (2:31), so that Adam knew what the angels did not (2:32–33).
- Honoured as "children of Adam" and favoured "above many of those We have created" (17:70).
- Created "in the finest state" (95:4).
- Created to worship God (51:56) and to be tested (67:2; 76:2).

**The human as a test-bearing creature with a two-sided soul.**

- God formed the soul and "inspired it [to know] its own rebellion and piety" (*fujuraha wa
  taqwaha*, 91:7–8).
- He "point[ed] out to him the two clear ways" (90:10).
- He guided man "whether he was grateful or not" (76:3).
- Humans uniquely took on the Trust (*amana*) that heaven, earth and mountains refused (33:72).

**Al-Ghazali's technical anthropology** (*Ihya'* 21, ch. 1):

- The real human is the **heart** (*qalb*) in its second, non-physical sense: "a subtle tenuous
  substance of an ethereal spiritual sort (*latifa rabbaniyya ruhaniyya*)", which "is the real
  essence of man" and is "addressed, punished, rebuked, and held responsible" (Marvels, p. 6).
- The same subtle reality is also called *ruh*, *nafs* and *'aql*.
- *Nafs* has a second, pejorative sense: the faculty of anger and appetite, the Sufis' "soul that
  must be striven against" (pp. 7–8).
- The Qur'anic soul-states name the one self in different conditions (p. 8):
  - *mutma'inna* ("at rest", 89:27–28);
  - *lawwama* ("upbraiding", 75:2);
  - *ammara bi-l-su'* ("commands to evil", 12:53).
- Man "has a rank between the beasts and the angels". He shares nutrition with plants and sensation
  and movement with animals, but his distinguishing characteristic is "experiential knowledge
  (*ma'rifa*) of the real nature of things" (p. 25).

**Maturidi.** "A person is composed of both a material nature (*tab'*) and intellect (*'aql*)." The
nature "will tend to desire what the intellect rejects", so humans must enquire into each matter to
choose rightly. Rational enquiry is how they pass the divine test as beings "distinguished with
management over the creation" (Harvey, p. 27, reporting *Kitab al-Tawhid* pp. 73–74).

**Ibn Taymiyya.** Humans are "dynamic and goal-oriented beings who necessarily will, love, worship,
and obey something; so, they cannot help but be religious" (SEP, Ibn Taymiyya §5). Their *fitra* is
an "original normative disposition" to believe in and worship God alone, as an infant instinctively
seeks milk (SEP §3.1).

**Ibn Sina.** The human is principally a rational soul, an immaterial substance. Its proper
perfection and real happiness lie in knowledge (SEP, Ibn Sina §4, summarizing *Shifa'*, Metaphysics
IX.7).

## 3. Metaphysical context

- **Strict monotheism (tawhid).** There is one Creator who is wise, merciful and just. God has
  "taken it upon Himself to be merciful" (6:12), and "My mercy encompasses all things" (7:156).
- **Everything other than God is created**, including human acts. On this Ash'aris, Maturidis and
  Ibn Taymiyya agree (37:96). They disagree on what human agency is and how it relates to God's
  creating (section 7).
- **There is no cosmic dualism.** Satan is a creature, not a rival principle. He "had no power over
  you except to call you" (14:22) and has no power over God's devoted servants (15:40–42).
- **This life is a test**, ordered to resurrection and judgment (67:2; 23:115). The anthropology is
  therefore teleological: humans are what they are because they are being tried for an eternal
  destiny.
- **Revelation is a continuing gift**, addressed to a creature who can forget. "When guidance comes
  from Me … there will be no fear for those who follow My guidance" (2:38).
- **Theodicy strands.**
  - Ash'ari: God acts as owner of His creation and is not bound by human standards (SEP, Arabic and
    Islamic Philosophy of Religion §5).
  - Ibn Sina: evil is privation or an unavoidable concomitant of a world of generation and
    corruption (§5).
  - Al-Ghazali: "there is nothing … more wondrously beautiful" than this world (§5).
  - Ibn Taymiyya: God creates even evils for wise purposes (*hikma*), such as nurturing repentance,
    humility and patience, and this is the best of all possible worlds (SEP, Ibn Taymiyya §4.5).

## 4. Why humans possess remarkable capacities

1. **Divine endowment.** God breathed of His spirit into man (15:29; 32:9). Al-Ghazali glosses the
   spirit as "a marvelous and lordly (*rabbani*) affair" (Marvels, p. 7, on 17:85).
2. **Knowledge as the human distinction.** God taught Adam all the names (2:31), "taught by the pen …
   taught man what he did not know" (96:4–5), and "taught him to communicate" (55:4). Al-Ghazali: the
   heart "is innately able to come to know realities, for it is a lordly and noble thing". This
   capacity is exactly what lets man bear the Trust of 33:72, which he identifies as gnosis and
   *tawhid* (Marvels, p. 39).
3. **Moral capacity is built in.** The soul knows its rebellion and piety (91:8). The *fitra* is a
   created orientation to God (30:30; Bukhari 1385; Muslim 2658b; Muslim 2865a, "I have created My
   servants" with a natural inclination to worship). For Ibn Taymiyya the *fitra* includes "basic
   rules of thought and fundamental moral intuitions" (SEP §3.1).
4. **Love, mercy and sacrifice reflect a share of divine mercy.** God divided mercy into a hundred
   parts and sent one down to earth, "and because of that … His Creations are merciful to each other"
   (Bukhari 6000; Muslim 2752a). The Qur'an praises self-preferring generosity: "They give them
   preference over themselves, even if they too are poor" (59:9). It also praises restraint of anger
   and forgiveness (3:134).
5. **Civilization as stewardship.** Humans are carried "by land and sea" and provided for (17:70).
   Ibn Kathir reads 17:70 as honour shown in upright form, hands, and "hearing, sight and a heart" to
   distinguish benefit from harm in worldly and religious matters (Ibn Kathir, abridged, on 17:70).
   On the reading of *khalifa* as one who "judges disputes … forbidding them from injustice" (Ibn
   Kathir on 2:30, citing al-Qurtubi), the office of successor includes governance and justice.
6. **Philosophical version (Ibn Sina).** All humans have the apparatus to acquire intelligible
   knowledge, "but they have to work for it". The rational soul's perfection is to become "an
   intellective world" mirroring the whole (SEP Ibn Sina §4; SEP Ibn Arabi §6 quoting *Shifa'* and
   *Najat*). Prophecy is this capacity raised to its height (SEP Ibn Sina §4).

## 5. Why humans behave destructively or wrongly

The Qur'an gives a cluster of **created liabilities** and **external solicitations**, none of which is
inherited guilt:

- **Created weakness and haste.** "Man was created weak" (4:28); "man is ever hasty" (17:11; 21:37).
  Man was "created anxious", fretful in misfortune and "tight-fisted" in good fortune (70:19–21),
  except those who pray (70:22).
- **Forgetfulness (*nisyan*).** Adam "forgot and We found him lacking in constancy" (20:115). "Do not
  be like those who forget God, so God causes them to forget their own souls" (59:19).
- **Ingratitude and self-sufficiency.** "Man is truly unjust and ungrateful" (14:34). "Man exceeds
  all bounds when he thinks he is self-sufficient" (96:6–7).
- **Desire made alluring and taken as a god.** The love of desirable things "is made alluring"
  (3:14). The one "who has taken his own desire (*hawa*) as a god" (45:23; 25:43). Paradise is for
  whoever "restrained himself from base desires" (79:40–41).
- **The soul inclined to evil.** "Man's very soul incites him to evil unless my Lord shows mercy"
  (12:53; speaker contested, section 13).
- **Satanic whispering and the companion.** Iblis vows to come at humans "from their front and their
  back" (7:16–17). The "slinking whisperer … whispers into the hearts of people" (114:4–5). Every
  person has a jinn-companion (*qarin*) (Muslim 2814a). Satan cannot compel: "I had no power over you
  except to call you … blame yourselves" (14:22).
- **Social formation.** "Every child is born upon the fitra; his parents make him a Jew, a Christian
  or a Magian" (Bukhari 1385; Muslim 2658b, 2658e). "I created My servants" inclined to worship, "but
  it is Satan who turns them away" (Muslim 2865a).
- **Accumulating habit.** "Their hearts are encrusted (*rana*) with what they have done" (83:14).
  Hearts can be sealed (2:7), diseased (2:10) and "blind" (22:46).
- **Rivalry.** People disagreed over revelation "after clear signs had come to them, because of
  rivalry between them" (2:213).
- **Iblis: pride as the paradigm sin.** He refused to bow, "I am better than him" (7:12), "refused
  and was arrogant" (2:34).

**Al-Ghazali's systematization:**

- **Developmental priority of desire.** "The passions are the soldiers of Satan, and the minds those
  of the angels". Because desire matures in childhood "before the perfection of the intellect", "the
  satanic forces have preceded and mastered the situation". So "in every human being passion
  prevails over intellect". Repentance is therefore obligatory for everyone, "prophet or fool"
  (Repentance, p. 43). This explains the **universality** of sin without inherited guilt.
- **Four qualities in every heart** (Marvels, ch. 5, pp. 29–31):
  - *bahimiyya* (brutish): appetite;
  - *sabu'iyya* (predatory): anger;
  - *shaytaniyya* (demonic): discernment used "in the discovery of ways of evil … guile, deceit,
    and cunning", setting forth "evil as though it were good";
  - *rabbaniyya* (lordly): from the divine spirit. Undisciplined, it makes man claim "lordship for
    himself" and love "mastery, superiority, exclusiveness, and despotism".

  "So there are gathered inside of a man's skin, as it were, a pig, a dog, a demon, and a sage"
  (p. 30). The usual human condition is servitude in which the mind "worthy of lordship … [is forced]
  to serve these low impulses": "the possessor possessed, the lord lorded over" (p. 31).
- **Rust on the mirror.** Each sin is "a darkening smoke" on the heart's mirror until it is "dark and
  murky and entirely veiled from God". Al-Ghazali connects this with 83:14 and with the report of the
  "black spot" that repentance polishes away (pp. 32–33).
- **The heart is neutral between angel and demon.** "The heart in its original innate condition is
  fitted to receive the influences of angel and demon alike, with no preponderance of the one over
  the other" (p. 80). Inspiration (*ilham*) and whispering (*waswas*) are both created influences on
  a heart that responds (pp. 78–79).

**Knowingly acting against one's judgment (akrasia).** This strand is especially strong:

- **The Qur'an names the phenomenon outright.** "How can you tell people to do what is right and
  forget to do it yourselves … Have you no sense?" (2:44). "Why do you say things and then do not do
  them?" (61:2). Of Pharaoh's people: "They denied them, in their wickedness and their pride, even
  though their souls acknowledged them as true" (27:14). The one whom God "allows to stray in the
  face of knowledge" (*'ala 'ilm*, 45:23). Iblis is the archetype: he knew God directly and still
  refused out of pride.
- **The hadith gives a phenomenology of conscience.** "Vice is what rankles in your heart and that
  you disapprove that people should come to know of it" (Muslim 2553a). Wrongdoing is often done
  *with* the felt knowledge that it is wrong.
- **Al-Ghazali's four stages of an act** (Marvels, ch. 13, pp. 118–119):
  1. involuntary suggestion (*khatir*);
  2. natural inclination (*mayl al-tab'*);
  3. the heart's judgment that the thing should be done (*i'tiqad*), which comes only once the
     inhibitions (*sawarif*) such as modesty or fear are suppressed, which "may come through
     reflection";
  4. firm resolve (*'azm*, *hamm*).

  Reason is thus **recruited** by desire: stage three is a judgment of the reason that overrides
  inhibition. Accountability attaches to stages three and four, not to the first two.
- **Degrees of moral corruption** (Disciplining the Soul, pp. 24–25). The second of four types "who
  recognises ugly acts for what they are", whose desires "deflect him from his better judgement.
  Despite this … he knows that he is not acting as he should". He is harder to reform than the
  innocent, but still reformable. The third and fourth types have come to believe that vice is good,
  which is the terminal stage of the mirror's corrosion.
- **Knowledge versus certainty.** In *Repentance*, the knowledge that moves to remorse and change is
  knowledge "with utter and decisive certainty, overwhelming his heart". Al-Ghazali glosses it as
  faith and certitude (*yaqin*), whose mastery over the heart produces "the fire of Regret"
  (pp. 31–32). Akrasia is then the state of knowing that a sin is harmful without that knowledge
  having become certainty that rules the heart.
- **Knowledge without practice increases guilt.** "The chastisement of the learned but immoral is
  greater than that of the immoral ignoramus" (Repentance, p. 42).

## 6. Do greatness and wickedness share a common explanation?

Yes. This is the most distinctive and philosophically interesting feature of the classical Sunni
account. Several mechanisms carry it:

1. **The same created endowments are used either way.**
   - *Al-Ghazali.* The "lordly" quality, the divine spirit in man, explains both the thirst to know
     all realities *and* the craving for mastery and despotism (Marvels, p. 29). The discernment that
     separates man from beasts is what makes "demonic" cunning possible (pp. 29–30). If man brings
     these powers under the lordly element, "his heart becomes the abode of such lordly qualities as
     knowledge, wisdom, certainty" (p. 31). In his summary, the ruined man "uses [God's armies] for
     the welfare of his enemies" (p. 26).
   - *Ibn Taymiyya.* Humans "necessarily will, love, worship, and obey something" (SEP §5). Devotion,
     sacrifice and love on one side, and idolatry, including of wealth, power or one's own desire
     (45:23), on the other, have one root: an unavoidable orientation of love, rightly or wrongly
     aimed.
2. **The Qur'anic scenes pair glory and danger.**
   - In 2:30–33 the angels object that the successor "will cause damage and bloodshed", and God
     answers by revealing Adam's knowledge. The same creature is the bloodshedder and the knower.
   - In 33:72 humans take up the Trust that the cosmos refused and are at once called "inept and
     foolish" (Abdel Haleem; Arabic *zalum jahul*). Ibn Kathir: man accepted it "despite the fact that
     he is weak, ignorant and unjust — except for those whom Allah helps" (on 33:72).
   - In 95:4–6 man is created "in the finest state" and then reduced "to the lowest of the low,
     except those who believe and do good deeds".
3. **Freedom and responsibility are the hinge.** A creature capable of the Trust is capable of
   betraying it. Ibn Arabi says that by issuing commands God makes humans "compelled to be free"
   (*majbur fi ikhtiyarihim*). Only because they can choose wrong can they realize the full range of
   divine qualities in themselves (SEP Ibn Arabi §5.3).
4. **Humanity is defined by return.** "Devotion to pure goodness is a trait of the angels … Devotion
   to uncorrected evil is the nature of devils. But return to good after stumbling in evil is
   inevitable in human beings … he who rectifies evil by returning to good, is, in truth, human"
   (Repentance, pp. 30–31). Compare Muslim 2749: if humans did not sin, God would replace them with
   people who sin and seek forgiveness. Greatness and failure are bound together in the specifically
   human vocation of *tawba*.

## 7. Rationality, moral knowledge, conscience, sin, agency, desire

**Rationality.** All strands treat the intellect as the human distinction:

- Al-Ghazali: the intellect is the "sage" and a "guiding counsellor" (Marvels, p. 30; Disciplining,
  p. 19).
- Ibn Sina: rational soul.
- Maturidi: the intellect stands against material nature.
- In Ibn Sina's and al-Ghazali's faculty psychology, estimation (*wahm*), imagination and the
  passions can mislead what an agent would intend "all things considered". Divine law supplies that
  all-things-considered view (SEP, Arabic and Islamic Philosophy of Religion §7.2).

**Moral knowledge: the *husn/qubh* debate.**

- *Mu'tazila* (contrast). Good and bad are objective properties knowable by reason. Revelation
  confirms them, and God is bound by them (SEP, Philosophy of Religion §7.1).
- *Ash'ari*.
  - Moral value is not an intrinsic property known by reason. "Good" and "bad" in the normative
    sense are what revelation commands and forbids.
  - Al-Ghazali holds this in law. Human judgments that lying is bad are driven by "a consideration
    of their benefits". "'Good' actions are those that are rewarded in the afterlife", a connection
    learned only from revelation (SEP al-Ghazali §5).
  - Al-Razi pairs divine-command theory with a hedonist and consequentialist ethic. Pleasure is good
    and pain bad, and God's promise of reward makes obedience rationally prudent (SEP al-Razi §6).
  - The Ash'ari position does not deny that humans have intuitions of good and bad. It denies that
    those intuitions are the ground of moral obligation.
- *Maturidi.* There are two readings:
  - Ayyub Ali reports that Maturidi agrees with the Mu'tazila that knowing God is rationally
    obligatory even without a messenger, that "things are intrinsically good or bad", and that reason
    "can often distinguish right from wrong". But "reason cannot be the final authority for human
    obligation"; revelation is (Ayyub Ali, "Criticism of the Mu'tazilites").
  - McGinnis stresses that for Maturidi these moral judgments are "always shaky given the frailty of
    our cognitive capacities", so religion "is always needed to set our moral judgments on firm
    ground" (SEP, Philosophy of Religion §7.2, citing *Kitab al-Tawhid* 346–51).
  - Harvey argues that Maturidi grounding of morality in God's eternal *wisdom* is neither Mu'tazili
    realism nor Ash'ari anti-realism. "Our natural knowledge reflects what God has — through His
    wisdom — determined to be the good" (Harvey, pp. 217–218).
- *Ibn Taymiyya.* He rejects both Mu'tazili rationalism and classical Ash'ari divine-command theory.
  Moral values depend on outcomes, "benefit or detriment". The *fitra* carries basic moral
  intuitions, and the law points to ultimate benefit (SEP Ibn Taymiyya §6, §3.1).

**Conscience.** It appears in the *nafs lawwama* that reproaches its owner (75:2; Marvels, p. 8), in
the hadith of what "rankles in your heart" (Muslim 2553a), and in the soul's inspired knowledge of
its rebellion and piety (91:8). Al-Ghazali adds the angelic "call" (*lamma*) in the heart (Marvels,
p. 79).

**Evil and sin.**

- Sin is "wronging one's own soul" (*zulm al-nafs*: 7:23; 4:110). It is self-injury as well as
  disobedience.
- For Ibn Taymiyya's later texts, moral failure is rooted in privation, an *omission* of what humans
  were created to do (SEP §4.5).
- For Ibn Sina, evil is non-being or imperfection proper to a thing (SEP, Philosophy of Religion §5).
- Al-Ghazali's medical model: vice is "a sickness and a disorder" of the soul, a departure from
  equilibrium among its faculties (Disciplining, p. 37).

**Agency.**

- *Ash'ari.* God wills and creates all acts, including disobedience. There "cannot be … any
  acquisition (*iktisab*) on the part of human beings that God does not will" (Ibana, p. 104). God
  guides believers and "causes the infidels to err", and good and evil fall under God's decree
  (Ibana, pp. 51–52). *Kasb* (acquisition) names the human relation to an act God creates. Its exact
  content was variously interpreted even within the school (Ayyub Ali, "Relation between God and
  Man"). Al-Ghazali, on Griffel's reading, gives a causal-determinist version: act ← volition ←
  motive (*da'iya*) ← knowledge and conviction. "Humans have only the impression of a free will",
  being "compelled to choose what they deem is the best action" (SEP al-Ghazali §6, §7).
- *Maturidi.* God creates all acts, but He creates them as the human freely chooses (*ikhtiyar*) and
  acquires (*iktisab*) them. God wills evil in the sense of permitting the choice He foreknows, but
  never commands or is pleased by it. Sin violates God's command and pleasure, not His will. Moral
  responsibility rests on freedom of choice conferred on man as a rational being (Ayyub Ali,
  "Relation between God and Man").
- *Ibn Taymiyya.* "God creates the human act by means of the human will and power" (secondary
  causality). Humans are the real agents of their acts (SEP §4.5).
- *Qur'anic tension, held together by all.* "Let those who wish to believe in it do so" (18:29) and
  "it is God who has created you and all your handiwork" (37:96). "Anything bad is [ultimately] from
  yourself" (4:79).

**Desire and appetite (*nafs*, *hawa*, *shahwa*).** Desire and anger are created goods with
purposes. "Should the desire for food cease man would die … should man feel no anger he would not be
able to defend himself". The goal is not extirpation but bringing them "under the command of the
intellect and the Law" (Disciplining, pp. 19, 25; SEP al-Ghazali §5). *Hawa* becomes destructive when
it is taken as a god (45:23), which ranks lower desire over reason and revelation.

## 8. Can the human condition be remedied?

Yes. The remedy is presented as proportionate to a diagnosis of weakness, forgetfulness and
disordered love rather than of inherited corruption:

- **Repentance (*tawba*) and divine acceptance.**
  - Adam "received some words from his Lord and He accepted his repentance" (2:37). "Later his Lord
    brought him close, accepted his repentance, and guided him" (20:122).
  - "Do not despair of God's mercy. God forgives all sins" (39:53). "Turn to God in sincere
    repentance" (66:8). God "loves those who turn to Him" (2:222).
  - God is more pleased with a servant's repentance than a traveller who finds his lost camel in the
    desert (Bukhari 6309).
  - Al-Ghazali: repentance is a sequence of knowledge, state (remorse) and action (Repentance, p. 31).
    It is obligatory for everyone (p. 43). Human evil "cannot be refined except by one of the two
    fires; of penitence, or of Hell" (p. 31).
- **Guidance and law (*huda*, *shari'a*).** Revelation recalls the *fitra* and perfects it.
  - For Ibn Taymiyya, prophecy "perfect[s] the fitra by removing sources of corruption, providing
    correct arguments, and revealing" what natural means cannot know (SEP §3.1). He treats the law as
    coextensive with justice and human benefit (SEP §5).
  - For al-Ghazali, the law's purposes protect religion, life, intellect, offspring and property (SEP
    al-Ghazali §5).
- **Purification (*tazkiya*) and discipline (*riyada*).**
  - "The one who purifies his soul succeeds" (91:9). "Prosperous are those who purify themselves"
    (87:14).
  - Character can change: otherwise "there would be no value in counsels, sermons and discipline"
    (Disciplining, p. 23). Virtue is built by habituation, "first forced and later becoming like a
    second nature" (SEP al-Ghazali §5).
  - Remembrance (*dhikr*) polishes the heart's mirror (Marvels, pp. 32–34; 13:28; 7:201).
- **Mercy over merit.** "The deeds of anyone of you will not save you", not even the Prophet, "unless
  … Allah bestows His Mercy" (Bukhari 6463).
- **Judgment.** "Each soul is responsible for its own actions; no soul will bear the burden of
  another" (6:164; 35:18). "Man will only have what he has worked towards" (53:39). "God does not
  burden any soul with more than it can bear" (2:286).
- **Communal remedy.** The community commands right and forbids wrong (3:110). Mutual counsel in
  truth and steadfastness rescues humans from "loss" (103:2–3).

## 9. Dignity: inherent or derived?

Both, in a structured way.

- **Inherent (given by creation, universal).** The honour of 17:70 is addressed to *all* "children of
  Adam", not only to believers. Ibn Kathir reads it as honour in form and faculties, and the verse
  goes on to prefer humans above much of creation. Dignity rests on what God made humans to be: the
  breath of the spirit (15:29), knowledge of the names (2:31), the *fitra* (30:30). Sin cannot erase
  the *fitra*, because "there is no altering God's creation" (30:30). Ibn Kathir notes the reading
  that all are created with "the same sound Fitrah … there is no disparity among people in this
  regard" (on 30:30).
- **Derived in the sense of grounded in God, not self-possessed.** Human dignity is an honour
  conferred (*karrama*, "We have honoured"). It is not intrinsic in a sense independent of God. Those
  who forget God are made to forget their own souls (59:19).
- **Ranked by piety (moral and achieved dignity).** "In God's eyes, the most honoured of you are the
  ones most mindful of Him" (49:13). Humans can sink "like cattle, no, even further astray" (7:179) or
  to "the lowest of the low" (95:5).
- **Al-Ghazali's version.** The heart is "lordly and noble" in its original constitution (Marvels,
  p. 39). Actual nobility is the realized rule of the lordly element (p. 31).
- **Khilafa.** On the "vicegerent" reading, dignity includes delegated responsibility for the earth.
  On the "successor/generations" reading (Ibn Kathir on 2:30), it is the dignity of a race entrusted
  with the earth in succession.

## 10. Internal school differences that materially change the answer

| Issue | Ash'ari (incl. al-Ghazali, al-Razi) | Maturidi | Ibn Taymiyya (Athari/Hanbali) | Ibn Sina (falsafa) | Ibn Arabi (Sufi metaphysics) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ground of moral knowledge | Revelation grounds obligation. Reason's judgments track benefit (al-Ghazali; al-Razi consequentialist) | Reason can often know good and bad; revelation is final authority (Ayyub Ali). Judgments fragile (McGinnis). Wisdom-grounded realism (Harvey) | *Fitra* carries moral intuitions; values track benefit and harm | Ethical rules are well-known, commonly accepted norms that religion preserves (SEP Phil. Rel. §7.2) | Reason alone lacks criteria; prophetic guidance required (SEP Ibn Arabi §5.3) |
| Agency in wrongdoing | God creates the act; *kasb*. Al-Ghazali: motive-determined choice | Real *ikhtiyar*; God creates what man chooses | God creates acts *through* human will and power; failure as privation | Determinist ontology (contingent in itself, necessary through another) | "Compelled to be free" |
| Why wrong is done | Desire/anger overpower intellect; demonic cunning; rust | Material nature desires what intellect rejects; failure to enquire | Misdirected love and worship; corruption of *fitra* by influences and bad reasoning | Soul turned toward the body away from intelligibles (lightly sourced) | Choosing *batil* over *haqq*; actualizing "severity" possibilities |
| Greatness | Lordly spirit; knowledge (*ma'rifa*); Trust = gnosis | Intellect; management of creation | Godward *fitra*; love of God | Rational soul's perfection | Form of the all-comprehensive name |

Why these differences matter:

- On a strong Ash'ari reading, "knowingly doing wrong" is a matter of motives and of knowledge not
  yet certain, and is ultimately within God's creating.
- On a Maturidi reading, it is more straightforwardly a free choice against what reason already
  sees.
- On Ibn Taymiyya's reading, it is above all *misdirected love*.
- **The Mu'tazila** (contrast) would add that humans create their own acts and that God must do the
  best (*aslah*). This is rejected by all represented schools (SEP Phil. Rel. §5; Ayyub Ali on
  Maturidi's rejection of *aslah*).
- **Shi'a** difference: noted in section 1; unverified, not material to the core answer.

## 11. What this worldview genuinely explains well

1. **Vice as disordered good rather than alien substance.** Cruelty, domination and greed are not
   unexplained additions to human nature. They are the same created powers misdirected:
   - anger needed for self-defence;
   - appetite needed for survival;
   - a God-given longing for knowledge and sovereignty (the "lordly" quality), turned toward
     self-deification.

   This explains with unusual precision why the most gifted people and civilizations are often the
   most dangerous. The engine of achievement and the engine of tyranny are one engine (Marvels,
   pp. 29–31; Disciplining, p. 25).
2. **Universality of wrongdoing without inherited guilt.** The account explains why everyone falls:
   - desire develops before intellect (Repentance, p. 43);
   - social formation away from the *fitra* (Bukhari 1385);
   - created haste and forgetfulness (21:37; 20:115).

   It does this while keeping individual responsibility exact: no soul bears another's burden (6:164).
3. **A detailed phenomenology of akrasia.** The analysis distinguishes involuntary thought,
   inclination, the reasoning that suppresses inhibition, and resolve (Marvels, pp. 118–119). It also
   distinguishes known-but-not-certain from certain knowledge (Repentance, pp. 31–32). This gives a
   credible map of how people do what they believe wrong, and of where responsibility begins.
4. **Progressive hardening.** The rust-on-the-mirror model (83:14; Marvels, pp. 32–33) and the
   four degrees of corruption (Disciplining, pp. 24–25) explain how people pass from knowing wrong to
   calling it good.
5. **Realistic hope.** Character is treated as malleable (Disciplining, p. 23). Repentance is always
   open, and God delights in it (Bukhari 6309). Being "human" is defined by return after failure
   (Repentance, pp. 30–31). The account neither despairs of humans nor idealizes them.
6. **Universal dignity alongside moral ranking.** Every child of Adam is honoured (17:70), but
   honour before God follows piety (49:13). This holds together both the dignity of the worst and the
   real moral difference between people.
7. **Love's ambivalence.** Ibn Taymiyya's thesis that humans necessarily love and serve something
   explains in one stroke both heroic devotion and fanatical or idolatrous attachment (SEP §5).

## 12. Strongest representative thinkers for this question

- **Al-Ghazali** (primary; recommended, principal voice). He gives the fullest classical Sunni
  psychology of the heart, nafs, desire, conscience, akrasia and repentance, and is directly on point.
- **Ibn Taymiyya** (primary; recommended). Strongest account of *fitra* as an innate Godward and
  moral disposition, and of humans as necessarily loving and worshipping beings.
- **Al-Maturidi** (primary; recommended). Represents the large Hanafi-Maturidi strand: reason's
  moral competence, real human choice, nature (*tab'*) against intellect.
- **Al-Ash'ari** (primary; recommended, briefly). Needed to state the Ash'ari framework of divine
  creation of acts, *kasb* and revelation-grounded obligation within which al-Ghazali works.
- **Ibn Sina** (primary; optional, one sentence). The philosophical account of greatness as the
  rational soul's perfection and of evil as privation. Lightly sourced here (SEP only).
- **Ibn Arabi** (specialist; optional, labelled). The human as the comprehensive being, "compelled to
  be free". Contested status; use only as a labelled mystical-metaphysical note.
- **Fakhr al-Din al-Razi** (primary; not recommended for this answer's main text). Relevant for
  consequentialist Ash'ari ethics, but his anthropology could not be read in primary text in this
  research. Cite only via SEP if at all.

## 13. Uncertainties and contested interpretations

1. **The speaker of 12:53.**
   - Abdel Haleem punctuates 12:52–53 as Joseph's speech.
   - Al-Ghazali says the words are those of "Joseph or the wife of the prince" (Marvels, p. 8).
   - Many commentators attribute them to the governor's wife.

   The doctrinal point (the soul's inclination to evil, *ammara bi-l-su'*) survives either way. The
   draft should not attribute the verse to Joseph without qualification.
2. **Meaning of *khalifa* (2:30).**
   - "Successor" (Abdel Haleem): Ibn Kathir reads successive generations and says the reference is
     not to Adam specifically.
   - "Vicegerent" or "deputy" of God: common in modern usage and in some classical exegesis.
   - Ibn Kathir also reports al-Qurtubi's sense of one who judges disputes.

   Present this as contested. Do not write "God's vicegerent" as settled.
3. **Meaning of the Trust (*amana*, 33:72).**
   - Obedience, obligatory duties, religion (Ibn Abbas and others in Ibn Kathir).
   - Gnosis and *tawhid* (al-Ghazali, Marvels, p. 39).

   *Zalum jahul* is rendered "unjust and ignorant" (Ibn Kathir translation) or "inept and foolish"
   (Abdel Haleem).
4. **Content of the *fitra*.** It may mean Islam or *tawhid* itself, a capacity or orientation, or a
   neutral receptivity.
   - Ibn Kathir records more than one reading of "no altering God's creation".
   - The Muhsin Khan translation of Bukhari 1385 glosses *fitra* as "true faith of Islam"; that is a
     translator's interpretation. Quote the Arabic sense or paraphrase.
   - Al-Ghazali speaks of a heart innately receptive to angel and demon "with no preponderance"
     (p. 80) *and* of being born "in equilibrium and with a sound innate disposition" (Disciplining,
     p. 37). These are not identical emphases.
   - Ibn Taymiyya's strong reading (an innate disposition to worship, with moral intuitions) is one
     strand.
5. **Al-Ghazali on free will.** Griffel's reading (SEP §6–7) is that al-Ghazali's position is
   causal-determinist ("only the impression of a free will"). Others read al-Ghazali's occasionalism
   and *kasb* differently. Mark as interpretation.
6. **The *Ibana*'s status.** Al-Ash'ari's *al-Ibana* is a traditionalist creed. Its relation to his
   kalam works (*al-Luma'*) and aspects of its transmission are debated. Klein's introduction
   characterizes Ash'ari as holding an "arbitrary will" view; that is Klein's evaluation, not cited
   here. Cite only the creed's text.
7. **Maturidi on reason.** Ayyub Ali's 1963 chapter presents Maturidi as close to the Mu'tazila on
   intrinsic good and bad. It is also openly partisan in its contrast with al-Ash'ari ("does not
   differ in essence from that of the Determinists"); do not reuse that characterization. McGinnis
   (SEP) stresses the frailty of rational moral judgment in Maturidi. Harvey offers a third,
   constructive reading. Present all three as interpretations. Later Maturidis (Samarqand
   vs. Bukhara) differed on obligation before revelation. Not verified in this research.
8. **Adam's act.** The Qur'an says Adam "forgot" (20:115), was made to "slip" by Satan (2:36), and
   "disobeyed his Lord and was led astray" (20:121). Sunni doctrine of prophetic protection
   (*'isma*) leads many theologians to treat it as a lapse rather than a grave sin. Not
   source-verified here; describe it with the Qur'an's own words.
9. **Hadith reliability.**
   - "Every son of Adam sins, and the best of the sinners are the repentant" (Tirmidhi 2499) is
     graded *da'if* (Darussalam grading on sunnah.com). Do not cite it as sound.
   - "God created Adam in His image" (Bukhari 6227) is variously interpreted (the pronoun's
     referent is disputed). Avoid using it for dignity claims.
10. **Ibn Sina on why humans do wrong.** Not read in primary text. The SEP gives his metaphysics of
    the rational soul and of evil, but not a full moral psychology of vice. Keep any Avicennan
    sentence to greatness and privation.
11. **Dates.**
    - Registry has al-Ghazali born 1058; SEP gives c. 1056.
    - Registry has al-Razi died 1209; SEP gives 1210 (d. 606 AH spans 1209–1210).
    - Ash'ari's dates are approximate (d. 935 or 936).

## 16. Thinker profile notes

**Al-Ghazali (Abu Hamid Muhammad al-Ghazali), c. 1056–1111.**
- *Setting.* Born at Tus (Khurasan). Studied with al-Juwayni at Nishapur and taught at the Nizamiyya
  madrasa in Baghdad. Abandoned his post in 1095 after a spiritual crisis, adopted the Sufi path, and
  later resumed teaching at Nishapur.
- *School.* Shafi'i jurist, Ash'ari theologian, Sufi.
- *Why relevant.* The *Ihya'* gives the fullest classical Sunni moral psychology of heart, nafs,
  desire, conscience, sin and repentance.
- *Key ideas.*
  - The heart (*qalb*) as a "lordly" subtle reality and man's true self.
  - Four qualities (brutish, predatory, demonic, lordly) within each person.
  - The heart as mirror, and sin as rust.
  - Virtue as equilibrium of faculties under intellect and law, acquired by discipline (*riyada*).
  - Repentance as knowledge, remorse and action, obligatory for all.
  - In law, moral value known through revelation (Ash'ari).
- *Works.* *Ihya' 'Ulum al-Din*; *al-Munqidh min al-Dalal*; *Tahafut al-Falasifa*;
  *Kimiya-yi Sa'adat*; *Mizan al-'Amal*.
- *Reference.* https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/al-ghazali/ (SEP gives c. 1056; registry has 1058).

**Ibn Taymiyya (Taqi al-Din Ahmad), 1263–1328.**
- *Setting.* Born at Harran. His family fled the Mongols to Damascus in 1269. Hanbali scholar who
  headed the Sukkariyya madrasa, and was repeatedly tried and imprisoned over theological positions.
- *School.* Hanbali, Athari (traditionalist) theology.
- *Why relevant.* He gives the strongest account of *fitra* as an innate disposition to know and
  worship God that includes moral intuitions, and of humans as necessarily loving beings.
- *Key ideas.*
  - *Fitra* as "original normative disposition", perfected by prophecy.
  - Humans necessarily love and worship something.
  - God creates acts through human will and power; moral failure as privation.
  - God acts for wise purposes; this is the best possible world.
  - Benefit-based (consequentialist) metaethics.
  - Shari'a coextensive with justice.
- *Works.* *Dar' Ta'arud al-'Aql wa-l-Naql*; *al-'Aqida al-Wasitiyya*; *al-Siyasa al-Shar'iyya*;
  *Qa'ida fi al-Mahabba*.
- *Reference.* https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ibn-taymiyya/

**Abu Mansur al-Maturidi, d. 333/944.**
- *Setting.* From Maturid, a quarter or village of Samarqand (Transoxania). Hanafi jurist and
  theologian. His *Kitab al-Tawhid* survives in a single Cambridge manuscript.
- *School.* Eponym of Maturidi kalam.
- *Why relevant.* He represents the Hanafi-Maturidi strand, with stronger confidence in reason's
  moral competence and in human choice.
- *Key ideas.*
  - It is rationally obligatory to know God.
  - Reason can often know good and bad, though revelation is final.
  - The human as material nature (*tab'*) plus intellect (*'aql*).
  - Real choice (*ikhtiyar*) and acquisition within God's creation of acts.
  - Divine wisdom (*hikma*) as an eternal attribute grounding moral order.
  - Sin violates God's command, not His will.
- *Works.* *Kitab al-Tawhid*; *Ta'wilat al-Qur'an*.
- *References.* https://library.oapen.org/handle/20.500.12657/62332 ;
  https://al-islam.org/history-muslim-philosophy-volume-1-book-3/chapter-13-maturidism

**Abu al-Hasan al-Ash'ari, c. 873/4–935/6.**
- *Setting.* Basra and Baghdad. A Mu'tazili and student of al-Jubba'i who broke with the Mu'tazila
  in middle life and defended Sunni doctrine with kalam methods.
- *School.* Eponym of Ash'ari kalam.
- *Why relevant.* He supplies the framework that al-Ghazali inherits: divine creation of all acts,
  *kasb*, and revelation-grounded obligation.
- *Key ideas.*
  - God's will and power extend to all events, including human acts and disobedience.
  - Acquisition (*kasb*/*iktisab*) as the human relation to God-created acts.
  - Good and evil under God's decree.
  - Moral obligation from revelation, not intrinsic properties known by reason.
  - Affirmation of divine attributes "without asking how".
- *Works.* *Maqalat al-Islamiyyin*; *Kitab al-Luma'*; *al-Ibana 'an Usul al-Diyana*.
- *References.* https://archive.org/details/theelucidationofisalamsfoundation ;
  https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/arabic-islamic-religion/ (§5).

**Ibn Sina (Avicenna), 980–1037.**
- *Setting.* Born near Bukhara; worked as physician and vizier in Hamadan and Isfahan.
- *School.* Peripatetic falsafa.
- *Why relevant.* He offers the philosophical account of human greatness as the rational soul's
  perfection through knowledge, and of evil as privation.
- *Key ideas.*
  - Immaterial rational soul.
  - Internal senses, including estimation (*wahm*).
  - Happiness as the intellectual perfection of the soul.
  - Prophecy as supremely developed intellect and imagination.
  - Evil as privation and a necessary concomitant of a world of generation and corruption.
- *Works.* *al-Shifa'*; *al-Najat*; *al-Isharat wa-l-Tanbihat*.
- *Reference.* https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ibn-sina/

**Ibn Arabi (Muhyi al-Din), 1165–1240.** Specialist; contested status.
- *Setting.* Born in Murcia (al-Andalus); travelled widely and died in Damascus.
- *School.* Sufi metaphysics.
- *Why relevant.* He gives the human as the comprehensive being who can realize every divine name,
  and so every possibility, high and low.
- *Key ideas.*
  - Humans created in the form of the all-comprehensive name *Allah*.
  - Deiformity (*takhalluq bi-asma' Allah*).
  - Engendering versus prescriptive command.
  - "Compelled to be free".
  - The Perfect Human (*al-insan al-kamil*).
- *Works.* *al-Futuhat al-Makkiyya*; *Fusus al-Hikam*.
- *Reference.* https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ibn-arabi/

**Fakhr al-Din al-Razi, 1149–1210.** Optional.
- *Setting.* Born at Rayy; worked under Khwarazm-Shah and Ghurid patronage, died at Herat.
- *School.* Ash'ari kalam engaged with Avicenna.
- *Key ideas.*
  - The soul as a unified subject of cognition.
  - Divine-command ethics combined with hedonist consequentialism.
  - Humans differ so greatly in character that one may suspect different essences.
- *Works.* *Mafatih al-Ghayb*; *al-Matalib al-'Aliya*; *Kitab al-Nafs wa-l-Ruh*.
- *Reference.* https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/al-din-al-razi/ (SEP gives d. 1210; registry has
  1209).

## The view

Mainstream Sunni thought explains both sides of human nature by one creation. God breathed of His spirit into the human being,<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="15:29" /> taught Adam "all the names",<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="2:31" /> and honoured every child of Adam.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="17:70" /> Humanity took on the Trust that heaven and mountains refused, and in the same verse is called "inept and foolish".<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="33:72" /> Every child is born on the *fitra*, a God-given orientation toward its Creator, and is then shaped by upbringing.<Cite source="bukhari-sahih-khan" locator="1385" />

The same nature carries real liabilities. Man is created weak and hasty,<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="4:28" /> Adam "forgot",<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="20:115" /> and the soul was inspired with both "its own rebellion and piety".<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="91:8" /> In al-Ghazali's psychology, the "lordly" element in the heart that seeks knowledge is also what craves mastery and despotism. The discernment that raises humans above beasts also enables cunning.<Cite source="ghazali-marvels-heart" locator="pp. 29–30" /> Evil is created power misdirected, not inherited guilt. Adam repented and was forgiven,<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="2:37" /> and no soul bears another's burden.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="6:164" /> The remedy is guidance, repentance and purification of the soul under God's mercy and judgment.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="91:9–10" />

## What this explains well

The account treats vices as disordered goods. Anger is needed for self-defence and appetite for life, so the aim is to govern them, not to destroy them.<Cite source="ghazali-disciplining-soul" locator="p. 25" /> This explains why great gifts and great dangers so often travel together. It explains why everyone falls without positing inherited guilt: desire matures before intellect,<Cite source="ghazali-repentance-stern" locator="p. 43" /> and habit gradually crusts over the heart.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="83:14" /> It gives a precise map of knowingly doing wrong. Inclination arises; reasoning suppresses modesty and fear; resolve follows; and responsibility begins with that judgment.<Cite source="ghazali-marvels-heart" locator="pp. 118–119" /> It holds universal dignity and real moral ranking together.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="49:13" /> And it offers grounded hope: character can change,<Cite source="ghazali-disciplining-soul" locator="p. 23" /> and God delights in repentance.<Cite source="bukhari-sahih-khan" locator="6309" />

## Deep dive

#### Scope

This entry represents mainstream Sunni thought. Its main voice is al-Ghazali's synthesis of Ash'ari theology and Sufi moral psychology in the *Revival of the Religious Sciences*, the classical Sunni work that most directly addresses this question. Maturidi theology, Ibn Taymiyya's traditionalism and the philosophical and mystical strands are noted where they change the answer.

#### Honour, trust and the angels' question

The Qur'an's first scene of human origins already contains the paradox. When God announces a successor on earth, the angels ask how He can place there "someone who will cause damage and bloodshed". God answers, "I know things you do not",<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="2:30" /> and then shows Adam's knowledge of the names, which the angels lack.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="2:31–33" /> Commentators disagree on whether *khalifa* means God's deputy or one generation succeeding another. Ibn Kathir favours the second reading.<Cite source="ibn-kathir-tafsir-abridged" locator="2:30" /> The same pairing recurs elsewhere. Man is created "in the finest state" and then reduced "to the lowest of the low", except those who believe and do good.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="95:4–6" /> Iblis is the paradigm of pride: "I am better than him".<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="7:12" /> Yet in the end Satan can only call, and humans answer: "blame yourselves".<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="14:22" />

#### Fitra without inherited guilt

Adam and his wife confess, "we have wronged our souls",<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="7:23" /> and Adam is "brought close" and guided.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="20:122" /> Nothing like inherited guilt follows. Each child is born on the *fitra*, and a hadith in which God speaks says He created His servants inclined to worship Him, "but it is Satan who turns them away".<Cite source="muslim-sahih-siddiqui" locator="2865a" /> Ibn Taymiyya develops this into his strongest form of the idea. The *fitra* is an original disposition to know and worship God, much as an infant seeks milk, and it includes basic moral intuitions; prophecy perfects it by removing what corrupts it.<Cite source="sep-ibn-taymiyya" locator="§3.1" /> He adds that humans "necessarily will, love, worship, and obey something".<Cite source="sep-ibn-taymiyya" locator="§5" /> Devotion and idolatry are therefore one capacity, rightly or wrongly aimed. The Qur'an names the second case: the one "who has taken his own desire as a god".<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="45:23" />

#### Al-Ghazali's anatomy of the heart

For al-Ghazali, the real human being is the heart, a subtle "lordly" reality that knows and is held responsible.<Cite source="ghazali-marvels-heart" locator="p. 6" /> Man stands between beasts and angels, and his distinguishing mark is knowledge of the real nature of things.<Cite source="ghazali-marvels-heart" locator="p. 25" /> Four kinds of quality meet in every heart: appetite, anger, a demonic cunning that "sets forth evil as though it were good", and a lordly element. Left undisciplined, the lordly element itself "claims lordship for himself and loves mastery … and despotism".<Cite source="ghazali-marvels-heart" locator="pp. 29–30" /> Inside one skin live "a pig, a dog, a demon, and a sage".<Cite source="ghazali-marvels-heart" locator="p. 30" /> When intellect serves appetite, the result is "the possessor possessed, the lord lorded over". When the order is reversed, the heart becomes the home of knowledge, wisdom and certainty.<Cite source="ghazali-marvels-heart" locator="p. 31" /> Each sin darkens the heart's mirror like smoke, and remembrance of God polishes it.<Cite source="ghazali-marvels-heart" locator="pp. 32–33" />

#### How one knowingly does wrong

The Qur'an addresses akrasia directly: "How can you tell people to do what is right and forget to do it yourselves?"<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="2:44" /> The Prophet defined vice as "what rankles in your heart" and what you would not want others to know.<Cite source="muslim-sahih-siddiqui" locator="2553a" /> Al-Ghazali traces an act through four stages: an involuntary thought, a natural inclination, a judgment that the thing should be done, and resolve. The judgment comes only once inhibitions such as modesty or fear have been set aside, sometimes through reflection. Accountability begins there.<Cite source="ghazali-marvels-heart" locator="pp. 118–119" /> One of his four types of moral condition is the person who "knows that he is not acting as he should". His desires have mastered his better judgment, but he remains reformable.<Cite source="ghazali-disciplining-soul" locator="pp. 24–25" /> What finally moves a person to change, al-Ghazali argues, is knowledge that has become certainty "overwhelming his heart".<Cite source="ghazali-repentance-stern" locator="pp. 31–32" />

#### School differences

In Ash'ari theology God creates every human act, including disobedience, and nothing a human acquires lies outside His will.<Cite source="ashari-ibana-klein" locator="p. 104" /> Moral obligation is grounded in revelation rather than in properties that reason detects.<Cite source="sep-arabic-islamic-religion" locator="§7.2" /> On Frank Griffel's reading, al-Ghazali holds that a person's motives and knowledge determine what he chooses.<Cite source="sep-al-ghazali" locator="§6" /> Maturidi theology describes the person as material nature and intellect, with nature desiring what intellect rejects, so that rational enquiry is how the human test is passed.<Cite source="harvey-transcendent-god" locator="p. 27" /> Interpreters differ on how far al-Maturidi trusts unaided moral reason, but he clearly affirms real human choice (*ikhtiyar*) within God's creation of acts. Ibn Taymiyya holds that God creates human acts *through* human will and power, and traces moral failure to a privation: the failure to do what one was created for.<Cite source="sep-ibn-taymiyya" locator="§4.5" />

#### Philosophical and mystical strands

Ibn Sina's philosophy locates human greatness in the rational soul. Its real happiness is perfection through knowledge, which it has to work for.<Cite source="sep-ibn-sina" locator="§4" /> In the contested but influential metaphysics of Ibn Arabi, humans, made in the form of the all-comprehensive divine name, are "compelled to be free". Only because they can choose wrong can they realize the full range of divine qualities in themselves.<Cite source="sep-ibn-arabi" locator="§5.3" />

#### Remedy

The remedy matches the diagnosis. Al-Ghazali argues that if character could not change, "there would be no value in counsels, sermons and discipline".<Cite source="ghazali-disciplining-soul" locator="p. 23" /> Repentance has three parts: knowledge, remorse and action.<Cite source="ghazali-repentance-stern" locator="p. 31" /> In his formulation, angels hold to pure good and devils to unrepented evil, but "he who rectifies evil by returning to good, is, in truth, human".<Cite source="ghazali-repentance-stern" locator="pp. 30–31" /> The Qur'an forbids despair of God's mercy,<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="39:53" /> and the Prophet taught that no one is saved by deeds alone, only by that mercy.<Cite source="bukhari-sahih-khan" locator="6463" />
