## Framing decisions

- Every thesis answers the question directly: Yes (Reformed, Judaism, Islam, each with its own qualification); "no good reason to think" (naturalism, framed as a burden-of-evidence stance, not a discovered impersonal entity); "It depends on the school" (Hindu, with all three schools in the thesis); a three-part answer (Buddhist: no personal creator; Buddhas revered as persons; nirvāṇa/emptiness neither persons nor impersonal gods).
- Reformed: "God is personal" and "God is three persons" are kept distinct (dossier caveat). The strongest objection is Maimonides' negative theology, with Advaita noted. The reply uses simplicity, analogy and the eternal love of Father and Son, and states its dependence on revelation.
- Judaism: the biblical and liturgical answer comes first; Maimonides is explicitly "does not deny that God is personal"; Halevi is the counterweight.
- Islam: Q 2:255, 59:22–24 and 42:11; the Ash'ari creed *bilā kayfa*; the attributes debate; al-Ghazali's condemnation of "God knows only universals"; Ibn Taymiyya's voluntary attributes. The Christian "eternal love" question is framed as a theological question dependent on Christian revelation, with the Sunni answer stated.
- Hindu: shared texts with both personal (Taitt II.6; BAU III.7; Śvet VI.13) and apophatic (BAU II.3.6, III.9.26) language; then the three schools.
- Buddhist: dossier caveat respected. Buddha-nature and Pure Land are given a personal face but are not made a creator; *dharmakāya* is not God; nirvāṇa is not personal and not an impersonal source.

## Self-review

- Thesis test: each thesis gives the elementary answer a 16-year-old could follow; the Hindu thesis is the longest (39 words) because it must name the school split.
- Representation: Maimonides is not made to deny personality; the Islamic God is not caricatured as will without love; Advaita's personal Lord is real at the empirical level; Pure Land and Buddha-nature are not turned into theism.
- Criticism: explanatory-adequacy questions (persons from an impersonal base; Advaita's grounding of persons; Buddhist conventional persons) and theological disagreements (Trinity) are kept distinct and their premises stated.
