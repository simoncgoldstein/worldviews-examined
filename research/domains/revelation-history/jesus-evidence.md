# Shared historical evidence packet: Jesus

Research packet, 2026-10-07. This is the **common evidentiary base for all six `jesus` cells**. The lane packets
interpret this evidence. They do not rebuild it.

Marks: **NEW** (read this pass), **NEW SRC** (registered or upgraded this pass), **REUSE**.

**Ground rules for production:**

1. Keep four levels apart:
   - (a) what a source says;
   - (b) what it establishes historically;
   - (c) which explanation best accounts for (b);
   - (d) what follows theologically if (c) is true.
2. Use no consensus percentages. "Most scholars" may be written only where a read source characterizes the field, and
   it must be attributed: e.g. Martin (L13) on Jesus' existence; Habermas (an apologist's survey) on the empty tomb and
   the appearances.
3. Do not use "criteria of authenticity" (multiple attestation, dissimilarity) as settled rules. Martin uses them as a
   teaching heuristic; they are contested in the field. Present them as one historian's method.

## C. 1 Corinthians 15:3–8

- **Text** (ESV, NEW): "For I delivered to you as of first importance what I also received" (v. 3). The content runs: died for
  our sins "in accordance with the Scriptures"; buried; raised the third day; appeared to Cephas, then to the twelve;
  then to more than five hundred, "most of whom are still alive"; then to James; then to all the apostles; "last of
  all... to me".
- **Formula.** "Received / delivered" is tradition language. Craig: the formula has "non-Pauline and Semitic
  characteristics" and matches Acts' sermons (NTS 1985, §1).
- **Dating the tradition:**
  - Craig: received at Damascus or at the Jerusalem visit three years after the call (Gal 1:18), so "within the first
    five years".
  - Habermas: most who answer place Paul's reception in the mid-30s.
  - Ehrman (2006) objects: Paul writes some 25 years later and does not name Joseph of Arimathea.
- **What it attests:**
  - The content of the earliest shared proclamation (v. 11, "so we preach").
  - The *claim* of named appearances, including a group and Paul himself.
  - Paul's knowledge of the leading witnesses (Cephas and James, Gal 1:18–19).
- **What it does not attest:**
  - It is not a transcript of AD 30.
  - It is not a narrative of the tomb (Paul has only "buried").
  - It does not describe what the appearances were like.
  - Paul's own appearance is listed with the others although Acts presents it as a heavenly vision. Ehrman's point: an
    "appearance" in antiquity need not mean a reanimated body.
- **The evidentiary chain:**
  1. Paul writes in the early-to-mid 50s.
  2. He reports a tradition he had received earlier.
  3. His chronology (Gal 1–2) puts his contact with Cephas and James three years after his call.
  4. Therefore the tradition plausibly goes back to the early 30s and to Jerusalem leaders.

  Each step is an inference. State them as such.

## D. Galatians 1–2

- Gal 1:15–16: God "was pleased to reveal his Son to me". Paul presents his call as revelation, not as instruction from
  the apostles (1:11–12).
- Gal 1:18–19: "after three years I went up to Jerusalem to visit Cephas and remained with him fifteen days. But I saw
  none of the other apostles except James the Lord's brother."
- Gal 2:9: James, Cephas and John, "pillars", gave Paul the right hand of fellowship about fourteen (or seventeen)
  years later.
- Martin (L5 chs. 2–3):
  - Paul "swears" he saw only Peter and James;
  - Galatians and Acts are hard to harmonize;
  - Martin's illustrative reckoning: crucifixion c. 30; call c. 34; the meeting about seventeen years later.
- **Significance:** first-person contact between Paul and two named witnesses of 1 Cor 15:5, 7, within a few years of
  the events, and an independent link between Jesus and a known historical brother (Josephus 20.200).

## E. Crucifixion

What each source adds:
- **Paul:** crucifixion as the core of the message (1 Cor 15:3; 1 Cor 1–2, not reread this pass).
- **Mark:** the passion narrative and the titulus "The King of the Jews" (Mark 15:26).
- **John:** an independent titulus (John 19:19, per Martin).
- **Tacitus:** a hostile Roman notice: execution under Tiberius by the procurator Pilate.
- **Josephus 18.63** (if the core is authentic): condemned to the cross by Pilate at the instigation of leading Jews.
- **The Talmud** (Sanh. 43a) independently presupposes that Jesus was executed. It gives a different mode (stoning and
  hanging by a Jewish court), which is legendary on Jewish-scholarly assessment (Jewish Encyclopedia).

**Assessment:** no ancient source, hostile or friendly, before the Qur'an denies that Jesus was executed. Martin calls the
execution "certain" with the baptism and treats the trial accounts as unhistorical (L13).

## F. Empty tomb

- **For** (Craig NTS 1985, arguments 1–8):
  - Paul implies it ("buried... raised");
  - a pre-Markan passion source (Pesch, dated within seven years);
  - "first day of the week", not "third day";
  - an unadorned narrative;
  - women as discoverers, an embarrassment;
  - the disciples' investigation;
  - Jerusalem proclamation impossible with the body available;
  - Jewish polemic (Matt 28:11–15) presupposes an empty tomb.

  Habermas: the women argument is the most popular in the literature.
- **Against / caution:**
  - Lowder §§1–2:
    - the pre-Markan source and its contents are speculative;
    - Paul's "buried" is neutral;
    - the women argument does not establish Mark's story;
    - the reburial hypothesis (Joseph moves the body to the criminals' graveyard; Mishnah Sanhedrin 6:5) has equal
      explanatory power and greater scope, so the story could be historical but is not shown to be.
  - Ehrman (2006):
    - Mark is 35–40 years later;
    - Paul never mentions a tomb or Joseph;
    - an alternative scenario (family reburial, Roman soldiers) is improbable but more probable than a miracle.
  - Martin (L6): Mark ends at 16:8 with no appearance.
- **Weighting rule:**
  - "Women's testimony proves the empty tomb" is an overclaim.
  - The women argument raises the probability that the women's discovery was not invented. That is an argument from
    embarrassment, contested in force.
  - The Matthean guard story is late and apologetic, though its report of a Jewish counter-claim is itself evidence of a
    dispute about a missing body.
- **Status:** a real historical debate. Lowder (a skeptic) grants the tomb was more likely than not empty. Habermas
  reports majority acceptance. Ehrman disputes it.

## G. Appearance claims

- Distinguish:
  - (i) people *claimed / experienced* appearances: strongly established;
  - (ii) the appearances were perceptions of a bodily raised Jesus: inference;
  - (iii) they were visions caused by grief, expectation, or religious experience: inference.
- **Alternatives (strongest forms):**
  1. **Bereavement visions** (Ehrman, blog preface 2015, partial; debate 2006 closing):
     - visions of the dead are common and believed by those who have them;
     - "only a few" (Peter, Paul, Mary, probably James) had visions; others believed their reports;
     - group appearances are "stories of visions" (the 500).

     Ehrman's later work (*How Jesus Became God*, 2014; not read) puts visions first.
  2. **Scripture-driven vindication belief** (Ehrman 2006):
     - a crucified Messiah needed explanation;
     - Isa 53 and Ps 22 show the righteous sufferer vindicated;
     - exaltation, then resurrection stories, then visions.
  3. **Legendary development** (Lowder; Martin on the Gospels): later narratives (Luke 24 physicality, the Matthew guard)
     grow from simpler claims.
  4. **Religious-experience / revelatory visions** (Hurtado, not a naturalist): experiences of the exalted Christ drove
     worship. A naturalist can accept the experiences while denying their object.
  5. **Cognitive dissonance after a failed messianic expectation:** named by the brief. **Not researched this pass**
     (Festinger-type models; Komarnitsky). Gap.
- **Christian replies in sources read:**
  - Paul distinguishes appearances from visions (Craig 2006).
  - Group appearances are hard for individual hallucination (Craig 2006).
  - Hurtado: principal-agent traditions do not explain *worship* of a figure.
  - Specialist psychiatric literature: the open-access Neotestamentica 2019 article is behind MUSE; Bergeron &
    Habermas 2015 was not read. Gap.

## H. Origin of resurrection belief

- **Jewish expectation:**
  - Resurrection as an end-time, general event (Dan 12:2 REUSE; 1 Cor 15:20, 23 "firstfruits... then at his coming").
  - Hurtado: Jesus as "the one man singled out for eschatological glorification".
  - Ehrman: "No Jew, prior to Christianity, thought that the Messiah was to be crucified."
- **Does this strengthen or weaken the alternatives?**
  - *Christian argument* (Wright-type; Wright's own articles were blocked, ntwrightpage.com Cloudflare): an individual
    bodily resurrection before the end was not an available category, so the claim requires something to have
    happened.
  - *Critical reply* (Ehrman): exaltation or vindication language (Enoch, Elijah) was available, and resurrection
    language followed from eschatological expectation ("Christ the firstfruits").
  - *Hurtado's middle position*: Jewish categories supplied the frame (principal agent); experiences supplied the
    pressure.
- **Overclaim to avoid:** "unprecedented, therefore true". Uniqueness raises the explanatory demand. It does not settle
  the explanation.

## I. Early Christology

- **Earliest datable evidence:**
  - 1 Cor 8:6 (c. 52–55), "one Lord, Jesus Christ, through whom are all things", alongside the Shema-like "one God, the
    Father";
  - 1 Cor 16:22 *marana tha* (Aramaic invocation);
  - Phil 2:6–11 (equality with God; universal acclamation applying Isa 45:23);
  - Rom 1:3–4 (Son of God "in power" by resurrection);
  - Rom 9:5 ("God over all": punctuation disputed; flag it).
- Hurtado: six devotional practices, including prayer, hymns, the name and the Lord's supper, show "a conscious and
  significant inclusion of Christ into the devotional life" within monotheism, "within the first two decades".
- **Disputes:**
  - whether Phil 2 is pre-Pauline ("perhaps", Hurtado);
  - whether early Christology was "exaltation" (adoptionist-leaning) or "incarnational" (Ehrman's model, via the debate
    only);
  - Bavinck (vol. 3 p. 503) reads Rom 1:4 as public installation, not first reception of divinity, against adoptionist
    readings.
- **Production rule:** establish the *chronology and level* (worship of Jesus within decades, by Jews), not Nicene
  ontology.

## J. Resurrection inference

**Facts common to both sides:**
- the crucifixion;
- burial (probable);
- early proclamation;
- appearance experiences, including those of Paul and James;
- early worship.

**The case for bodily resurrection** (Craig 1985/2006; SEP Miracles §2.2.3):
- it explains the facts together (scope);
- the alternatives each fail on some fact: hallucination for groups; theft for the disciples' sincerity; wrong tomb;
  apparent death (rejected by both Lowder and Craig);
- the belief arose among Jews for whom this category was unusual;
- the evidence comes from witnesses close in time.

The argument becomes strong only if God's existence is not excluded beforehand (SEP Miracles §4.2). In the Reformed
lane it is framed by revelation, not offered as neutral proof (Bavinck I §14).

**The case against** (Ehrman 2006; Lowder 2001; Hume E 10.12–13, 10.36–38):
- the sources are late, partisan, divergent and legend-prone;
- naturalistic combinations (visions plus legend plus reburial or uncertainty) are each improbable but jointly more
  probable than a miracle, which is "the least probable occurrence";
- a historian, as historian, cannot conclude that God acted;
- religious miracle claims face Hume's special discount (E 10.38).

**Where the evidence ends:** at claims 1–10. The step to 11 depends on:
- the prior probability of a theistic act, which is a metaphysical judgement, not historical method;
- whether historical method may include agents beyond nature;
- how much weight the convergence of facts carries.

That is where each lane's metaphysics enters.

**Christian revelation enters** at step 12: what the resurrection *means* (Rom 1:4; Acts 2:36; 17:31). See
`christianity.md` §3a.
