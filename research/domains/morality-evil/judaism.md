# Rabbinic Judaism — Morality, Evil & the Human Problem

Domain packet. Codes: **KG**, **FG**, **EV**, **SU**, **DE**, **SD** (see README). **P3** = Phase 3;
**NEW** = mined here; **NEW SRC** = newly registered.

## 1. Core account

Humans are free and responsible creatures made in God's image. They hold a created inclination
(*yetzer*) that powers both building and sin, to be governed through Torah, commandments and
*teshuvah*. Moral knowledge comes partly from reason and partly from revelation. Some commandments
"would have been logical to write" had they not been written (Yoma 67b). All humanity is bound by
the Noahide laws (Sanhedrin 56a). Torah is the full statement. Failure to do the good is the
inclination ("yeast in the dough", Berakhot 17a) growing by habit (Sukkah 52). "There is not one good
person on earth who… doesn't err" (Eccl 7:20). Evil is not a rival power. God forms light and
darkness, "weal and… woe" (Isa 45:7). Moral evil is chosen, and most human evils are self-inflicted
(Maimonides, *Guide* III.12). On suffering the tradition holds several voices in tension and does not
reduce them to one. They are retribution ("no suffering without iniquity", Shabbat 55a), the
"afflictions of love" (Berakhot 5a), the refusal "neither they nor their reward" (Berakhot 5b),
acknowledged ignorance ("it is not in our hands", Avot 4:15), Job's rebuke of the friends (Job
42:7), and Maimonides's transformed knowledge of God (*Guide* III.23). Death is Adam's legacy of
mortality, not inherited guilt (Shabbat 55b). It is even "very good" (BR 9:5). It comes "against your
will" and leads to judgment (Avot 4:22). Self-deception is the inclination's blinding and the
self-approval of one's own ways (Prov 16:2). Habit makes sin seem "as if permitted" (Yoma 86b), and
the remedy is daily self-reckoning (*Mesillat Yesharim* 2–3).

## 3. Question map

### KG
- **Main answer.** Reason grasps much of morality. Saadia: reason requires gratitude and forbids
  wronging others (III.1, P3). Some ordinances would have been logical to write had they not been
  written: idolatry, forbidden relations, bloodshed, theft, blasphemy (Yoma 67b). All humans are
  commanded the seven Noahide laws (Sanhedrin 56a). Revelation specifies and grounds. Maimonides
  rules that a gentile counts among "the pious among the gentiles" only when keeping the Noahide laws
  *because God commanded them*, not "out of intellectual conviction" (MT Kings 8:11). Moral knowledge
  is "very close… in your mouth and in your heart" (Deut 30:11–14, P3). Maimonides, *Guide* I.2
  (P3): good and bad are "apparent" (conventional) truths, distinct from demonstrative truth.
- **Internal distinction.** Rationalist (Saadia: rational vs. traditional commandments) vs. Maimonides'
  demand for revealed grounding (Kings 8:11; note the reported manuscript variant, unchecked) vs.
  the *Guide* I.2 reading of good/bad as convention (contested, P3 §13).
- **Uncertainty.** Kings 8:11 variant: verify against a critical edition before relying on the
  printed reading in public.

### FG
- As in Phase 3 (concise): Berakhot 17a; Sukkah 52a–b; Kiddushin 30b; *Eight Chapters* 6; Avot 2:4.
  Add Eccl 7:20 for universality and Avot 4:1 ("Who is mighty? He who subdues his inclination").

### EV
- **Main answer.** One Creator of everything, including "woe" (Isa 45:7). The *yetzer* is part of
  "very good" (BR 9:7), so evil is misgoverned created energy. Maimonides gives three kinds of evil:
  from matter, from human against human, and the largest class, self-inflicted (*Guide* III.12, P3).
  Merits come from form and vices from matter (III.8, P3). "God made human beings plain, but they
  have engaged in too much reasoning" (Eccl 7:29).
- **Internal distinction.** Aggadic one-energy view vs. Maimonidean matter/form account (P3).
  Kabbalistic "other side" not researched; omit or note.

### SU
- **Main answer: plural, deliberately.**
  1. *Retribution and self-examination.* Rav Ami: "no death without sin… no suffering without
     iniquity" (Shabbat 55a). One who suffers "should examine his actions" (Berakhot 5a). The Gemara
     refutes the death half (Shabbat 55b, P3).
  2. *Afflictions of love.* Suffering without transgression is from love (Prov 3:12). It cleanses
     and atones, by a fortiori from the slave's tooth and eye (Berakhot 5a).
  3. *Refusal.* Rabbi Ḥiyya bar Abba and Rabbi Elazar: "I welcome neither this suffering nor its
     reward". The healer lifts them up (Berakhot 5b).
  4. *Acknowledged ignorance.* "It is not in our hands" to explain the security of the wicked or the
     afflictions of the righteous (Avot 4:15).
  5. *Job.* God rebukes the friends' retribution theology (Job 42:7) and answers from creation (Job
     38:4). For Maimonides the book teaches that God's providence is unlike ours. Job's suffering
     ends in true knowledge of God, where "no earthly trouble can disturb" felicity (*Guide* III.23).
  6. *Self-inflicted evils* dominate (*Guide* III.12).
  7. Atonement through suffering and death (m. Yoma 8:8, P3).
- **Best passages.** Berakhot 5a–b; Shabbat 55a–b; Avot 4:15; Job 38:4, 42:7; *Guide* III.12, III.23;
  Ps 90:10.
- **Internal distinction.** The plurality *is* the answer. Do not collapse it into one theodicy.
  Post-Holocaust theology (Berkovits, Fackenheim, Rubenstein) is relevant for modern streams but was
  not researched. Note it as a limit if raised.

### DE
- **Main answer.** Mortality is the human condition, "to dust you shall return" (Gen 3:19). The
  Talmud concludes there is "death without sin": four died "due to Adam's sin with the serpent"
  though themselves free of sin (Shabbat 55b, P3), so death is Adam's legacy, not inherited guilt. Rabbi Meir: "very good" means
  "death is good" (BR 9:5). One view says Adam was penalized with mortality foreseeing descendants who
  would deify themselves (BR 9:5). Lower traits: humans die like animals (BR 8:11, P3). "Against your
  will you will die, and against your will you will give an account" (Avot 4:22). "Teach us to count
  our days rightly, that we may obtain a wise heart" (Ps 90:12). One hour of repentance in this world
  outweighs the world to come (Avot 4:17).
- **Best passages.** Gen 3:19; Shabbat 55a–b; BR 8:11, 9:5; Avot 4:17, 4:22; Ps 90:10–12.
- **Internal distinction.** Death as penalty (Rav Ami; BR 9:5 second view) vs. death as natural or
  even good (R. Meir). World to Come and resurrection belong to `after-death`.

### SD
- **Main answer.** "People look approvingly on their own ways, but God probes motives" (Prov 16:2).
  "Most devious is the heart" (Jer 17:9). Once a person repeats a transgression "it becomes to him as
  if it were permitted" (Yoma 86b). The inclination is a seducer that grows from guest to master
  (Sukkah 52, P3) and assures one "that the grave is a place of refuge" (Avot 4:22). Luzzatto: the
  inclination "literally blinds his eyes". Worldly darkness both hides stumbling blocks and distorts
  sight "until they literally see evil as if it were good", even producing "convincing evidences" for
  it. The inclination keeps people too busy to reflect. The remedy is daily, scheduled self-reckoning
  (*Mesillat Yesharim* 2–3, citing Eruvin 13a, Bava Batra 78b).
- **Best passages.** Prov 16:2; Jer 17:9; Yoma 86b; Avot 4:22; *Mesillat Yesharim* 2–3; Maimonides,
  *Teshuvah* 3:4 (the shofar "wake up", P3).
- **Internal distinction.** None material. Musar emphasis (Luzzatto) is broadly representative.
