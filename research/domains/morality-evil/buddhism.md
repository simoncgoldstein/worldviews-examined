# Buddhist traditions — Morality, Evil & the Human Problem

Domain packet. Codes: **KG**, **FG**, **EV**, **SU**, **DE**, **SD** (see README). **P3** = Phase 3;
**NEW** = mined here; **NEW SRC** = newly registered (none in this lane).

Baseline: the Pali discourses and Buddhaghosa. Śāntideva supplies Mahāyāna ethical psychology.
Other schools appear only where they change the answer: Yogācāra for SD, Madhyamaka for agency and
Tiantai for EV, all P3.

## 1. Core account

This is the domain where Buddhism is most at home. Suffering (*dukkha*) is the first truth and
craving its origin (SN 56.11, P3). Aging-and-death is the last link of dependent origination (SN
12.1, P3). Moral knowledge is practical discernment of what is "unskillful… criticized by sensible
people… [and] lead[s] to harm" (AN 3.65), supported by the native guardians of shame and moral dread
(AN 2.9, P3). Failure to do the good: understanding does not uproot latent tendencies until a
superior, non-sensual joy is found (MN 14, P3). Evil is unskilful action rooted in greed, hate and
delusion (AN 3.69, P3), not a substance or cosmic power. Pain is inevitable for the embodied, but the
"second arrow" of mental suffering is added by aversion, craving and ignorance (SN 36.6). Death is
certain and is to be reviewed daily to cut "the vanity of life" (AN 5.57; Vism VIII). The cure is to
end craving and rebirth, not to survive. Self-deception is the four "perversions" of perception,
thought and view: impermanent as permanent, suffering as happiness, not-self as self, foul as
beautiful (AN 4.49). Ignorance "conceals" (Vism XVII.51, P3). Remedy: the path to nirvāṇa.

## 3. Question map

### KG
- **Main answer.** Know for yourselves, not by tradition, lineage, scripture or bare logic alone,
  which things "are unskillful, blameworthy, criticized by sensible people", and lead to harm and
  suffering when undertaken. Greed, hate and delusion arise for harm (AN 3.65:4.1–7.1). The ten
  unwholesome courses of action are the conventional content (MN 9, P3). Shame (*hiri*) and moral dread
  (*ottappa*) are "guardians of the world" (AN 2.9; Vism XIV.142, P3). Reflection before, during and
  after acting asks whether an act harms self or others (MN 61, P3).
- **Caution.** The Kālāma discourse is not a charter for autonomous free inquiry. It joins personal
  verification to the judgment of "sensible people" and to the consequences, and in context it
  leads to the Buddha's teaching on the roots.
- **Internal distinction.** None material at this level. Dharmakīrti's epistemology is out of scope.

### FG
- As in Phase 3 (concise): MN 14.2–4 (Mahānāma); "overcome by greed" (AN 3.65, 3.69); BCA 4.24–28;
  latent tendencies (MN 64.3; Vism XXII.60).

### EV
- As in Phase 3: three unwholesome roots and domination (AN 3.69:2.3); craving (SN 56.11); latent
  tendencies; "all injury, fear and pain… from grasping at a self" (BCA 8.134). School variation:
  Tiantai's inherent evil (SEP Tiantai, P3); Dōgen's "evils have no fixed nature" (P3). Māra as
  personification is optional and not mined.

### SU
- **Main answer.** Ordinary people and noble disciples both feel pain. The difference is that the
  ordinary person, "struck with an arrow", is "struck with a second arrow". Bodily pain is followed
  by mental sorrow because aversion resists it, craving seeks escape only in sensual pleasure, and
  ignorance does not understand feeling's origin and escape. The noble disciple "experience[s] one
  feeling: physical, not mental" (SN 36.6). Dukkha in the full sense is the five clinging aggregates,
  with craving as origin and cessation possible (SN 56.11, P3). Buddhaghosa distinguishes pain (bodily)
  from grief (mental). Grief produces bodily suffering in turn, even self-harm (Vism XVI.50–51).
  Mahāyāna adds that suffering has value: without suffering there is no going out from saṃsāra.
  Endurance grows by practice from small pains to great ones. Suffering's "other virtue" is that its
  agitation (*saṃvega*) removes pride and breeds compassion, fear of evil and longing for the Buddha
  (BCA 6.12, 6.14, 6.21, Sanskrit, paraphrased).
- **Best passages.** SN 36.6; SN 56.11; SN 12.1; Vism XVI.49–52; BCA 6.12–21.
- **Internal distinction.** Early/Theravāda: end suffering by ending craving. Mahāyāna: also use
  suffering as a means of compassion. Both share SN 36.6's distinction.
- **Caution.** Do not reduce Buddhism to "life is suffering" (SOURCES.md research caution).

### DE
- **Main answer.** Death is conditioned by birth (SN 12.1, P3). It is the interruption of the life
  faculty within one existence, timely by exhaustion of merit or lifespan, untimely by interrupting
  karma (Vism VIII.1–3). Everyone should often review "I am liable to die, I am not exempt from
  death", because beings "drunk on the vanity of life" do wrong, and reflection reduces that vanity.
  It also generalizes: all beings die, and "the path is born in them" (AN 5.57). Recollecting death
  wisely, with mindfulness, urgency (*saṃvega*) and knowledge rather than sorrow or anxiety, brings
  diligence, disenchantment, freedom from attachment to life and a death "undeluded" rather than in
  terror (Vism VIII.4–7, 41). Death is like a murderer with a raised sword, since "it comes with birth
  and it takes away life" (VIII.9–10). Śāntideva: death strikes without waiting for done or undone.
  "I did not know that I must go, leaving everything". "I did not reflect, 'I am a guest here'", and
  so committed many evils through delusion, attachment and aversion. Day and night life drains away
  (BCA 2.33–42, paraphrased). *Why death seems wrong:* craving for existence (SN 56.11). The cure is
  the deathless, not survival.
- **Best passages.** AN 5.57; Vism VIII.1–10, 41; SN 12.1; BCA 2.33–42.
- **Internal distinction.** None material. Rebirth and what continues belong to `after-death`.

### SD
- **Main answer.** Four perversions (*vipallāsa*) "of perception, thought, and view": taking
  impermanence as permanence, suffering as happiness, not-self as self, ugliness as beauty. Beings are
  "deranged, out of their minds" and keep transmigrating until they "get their mind back" (AN 4.49).
  Ignorance's function is "to confuse… manifested as concealing" (Vism XVII.51, P3). It works like the
  crazed cow drinking hot water that harms her (XVII.39, P3). Craving "conceals faults" (Dharmakīrti,
  P3, numbering caution). Yogācāra: the afflicted mentation continuously misconstrues a self beneath
  awareness (Triṃśikā 6, P3). "Vanity" of youth, health and life (AN 5.57) is a further form.
- **Best passages.** AN 4.49; Vism XVII.39, 51; Triṃśikā 6; AN 5.57.
- **Internal distinction.** Yogācāra locates self-deception below conscious choice. The early
  baseline treats it as ignorance and the perversions. Both are compatible.
