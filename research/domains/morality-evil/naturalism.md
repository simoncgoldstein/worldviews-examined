# Naturalism — Morality, Evil & the Human Problem

Domain packet. Codes: **KG** `know-the-good`, **FG** `fail-the-good`, **EV** `evil`, **SU** `suffering`, **DE** `death`, **SD** `self-deception`. **P3** = established in Phase 3; **NEW** =
mined here; **NEW SRC** = newly registered.

## 1. Core account

Humans are evolved social animals. Moral capacities (sympathy, conscience, norm-following) have
natural histories (Darwin, Hume). Whether moral facts exist is a separate question: realists such
as Railton and anti-realists such as Mackie disagree, and the evolutionary story does not settle it
(SEP Morality and Biology §1.1). Failure to do the good is conflict between motives: violent vs. calm
passions, momentary impulse vs. enduring social instinct, discounting. Evil is no metaphysical
principle. It names extreme wrongs and the dispositions behind them, explained by limited sympathy,
competition, ideology and mismatch. Some naturalists would drop the word altogether. Suffering and
death have no purpose. Pain is a biological signal. Senescence follows from the declining force of
selection with age. Nature's indifference is the best explanation of the distribution of suffering
(Hume, *Dialogues* XI). Death ends the person. The philosophical question is whether death is bad for
the one who dies (Epicurus vs. deprivationists) and whether immortality would even be desirable
(Williams). Self-deception is motivated belief, possibly adaptive. The remedy is gradual and partial:
institutions, education, widening sympathy, reflection, medicine.

## 3. Question map

### KG — Why do we know or seek the good?
- **Main answer.** Evolved social instincts plus intelligence yield conscience (Darwin IV p. 98). The
  moral sentiments are shared and correctable from a general point of view (Hume T 3.3.1.15; EPM
  9.5–9.6). Then comes the metaethical fork. Realists say moral facts are what impartial, fully
  informed approval would track, learned by feedback (Railton pp. 190–194). Anti-realists say "there
  are no objective values" and morality is invented but serves a real function (Mackie pp. 15,
  42–43, 108). Evolutionary debunking (Street, Joyce) vs. autonomy-of-reflection replies (SEP
  Morality and Biology §4.1).
- **Best passages.** Darwin IV pp. 97–98, 113–114; Hume T 3.2.2, EPM 9.4–9.6; Railton pp. 169,
  190–203; Mackie pp. 15, 42–43, 108; SEP Morality and Biology §§1.1, 4.1.
- **Internal distinction.** Realism vs. anti-realism must be stated. Mackie's error theory is a
  minority view and must not stand for naturalism (P3 §13).
- **Uncertainty.** Hume's is–ought paragraph (T 3.1.1.27) not read; optional.

### FG — Why do we fail to do the good we know?
- **Main answer.** Competing motives: "Men often act knowingly against their interest" when violent
  passions overcome calm ones (Hume T 2.3.3.10). The stronger momentary impulse wins, then the
  enduring social instinct returns as remorse (Darwin IV pp. 113–114). Discounting, resolution
  failure and dual-process psychology are in SEP Weakness of Will §§2, 3.3, 3.4.2. Dennett's
  "partially coherent agency" also applies.
- **Best passages.** As in Phase 3. Concise answer.
- **Internal distinction.** Free-will skeptics (Caruso, Pereboom) vs. compatibilists (Hume EHU 8.23,
  Dennett) on blame. Mention only.

### EV — What is evil?
- **Main answer.** No evil substance or force. In secular moral theory "evil" is either extreme
  wrongdoing, with debate over what more than wrongness it requires, or a concept to be retired:
  evil-skeptics say it imports supernatural commitments, explains nothing and is dangerous
  (SEP Concept of Evil §§1–1.2, 3.1). Human evil is explained causally by limited sympathy, malevolence
  and competition (Mackie p. 108; Hume T 3.2.2.18), proactive coalitionary aggression (Wrangham) and
  parochial loyalty (Darwin V p. 130). Natural evil is not a problem for naturalism. It is evidence
  for it: suffering's distribution fits an indifferent source better than a good one (*Dialogues*
  11.14–15). Philo grants that evil is *consistent* with a good God but denies that it permits
  *inferring* one (11.4–5).
- **Best passages.** *Dialogues* 10.25 (Epicurus's questions), 11.5–11.15; SEP Concept of Evil §§1,
  3.1; Mackie p. 108; Wrangham; Darwin V p. 130.
- **Internal distinction.** (a) Realists (evil is objectively wrong) vs. anti-realists (evil-talk
  expresses or projects attitudes). (b) Evil-skepticism vs. revivalism. (c) Logical vs. evidential
  problem of evil. Hume's Philo already concedes the logical form and presses the evidential one.
- **Caution.** The *Dialogues* speaks through characters. Attribute lines to Philo or Demea, not
  flatly to Hume.

### SU — Why do we suffer?
- **Main answer.** There is no "why" in the sense of purpose. Pain exists because it motivates
  self-preservation. Hume's Philo argues that the system's evils depend on four circumstances, none
  apparently necessary: pain as motive where diminished pleasure would serve; general laws; frugal
  endowment; inaccurate workmanship (*Dialogues* 11.6–11.11). The whole suggests "a blind Nature…
  pouring forth from her lap, without discernment or parental care, her maimed and abortive children"
  (11.13). The "true conclusion" is that the source of things is "entirely indifferent" to good and
  ill (11.14–15). Human-caused suffering is explained as under EV. Remedies are medicine, institutions
  and sympathy (Russell ch. 4, P3; Darwin IV pp. 122–123).
- **Best passages.** *Dialogues* 10.3–4, 10.8–9 (misery catalogue, spoken by Demea and Philo),
  11.6–11.15; Darwin; Russell.
- **Internal distinction.** None deep. Optimistic humanists (Russell, Kitcher on progress) differ from
  pessimistic naturalists (Schopenhauer-style; not researched) in tone, not account. Mention
  briefly if at all.
- **Uncertainty.** No primary scientific source on pain's evolutionary function was registered. The
  point is uncontroversial and can be carried by Hume 11.6 plus general statement; register a pain
  science source only if a published claim needs it.

### DE — Why do we die, and why does death seem wrong?
- **Main answer.** *Why we die:* senescence evolves because the force of natural selection declines
  with age. Hence mutation accumulation (Medawar), antagonistic pleiotropy (Williams) and the
  disposable soma (Kirkwood). Aging "is not a programmed process" (Flatt & Partridge, "Why does aging
  evolve?", "Key lessons"; SEP Death supplement). *Why death seems wrong:* the evolved drive to
  survive and the loss death inflicts. *Is it bad?* Epicurus says no: "death is nothing to us…
  when we are, death is not come, and, when death is come, we are not" (DL X.124–125). Lucretius
  adds the symmetry argument (SEP Death §5.2). Deprivationists (Nagel, Feldman) say death is bad
  because it deprives one of the goods one would otherwise have had (§3.2). Williams says immortality
  would be tedious, so mortality is good although premature death is a misfortune (§7.2). Russell:
  "when I die I shall rot… Happiness is none the less true happiness because it must come to an
  end" (ch. 1).
- **Best passages.** Flatt & Partridge (two sections); SEP Death §§3.2, 5.2, 7.2, supplement; DL
  X.124–126; Russell ch. 1.
- **Internal distinction.** Epicurean vs. deprivationist vs. Williams. This is the material
  intra-naturalist disagreement on whether death is an evil. The biology is consensus.
- **Caution.** Epicurus is not an atheist. He is the historical source of an argument naturalists
  inherit (see registry note).

### SD — Why can humans know truth and still deceive themselves?
- **Main answer.** The intentionalist model generates paradoxes: how can one hold contradictory
  beliefs (static), and how can one intend to deceive oneself without the intention defeating itself
  (dynamic)? (SEP Self-Deception §1). Deflationary accounts (Mele): motivated, biased belief
  formation driven by desire, fear or anxiety, with no intention required (§3.1). Evolutionary
  explanation: either self-deception is adaptive, through positive illusions or deceiving others
  better (von Hippel & Trivers), or it is a by-product (spandrel); the evidence is contested (§6).
  Mackie's projection of attitudes as objective (pp. 42–43, P3) and Russell's "inferior clay"
  rationalization of cruelty (ch. 4, P3) apply this to morality.
- **Best passages.** SEP Self-Deception §§1, 3.1, 6; Dennett (P3).
- **Internal distinction.** Intentionalist vs. deflationary; adaptation vs. spandrel.
