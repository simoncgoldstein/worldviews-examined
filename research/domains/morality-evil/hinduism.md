# Hindu traditions — Morality, Evil & the Human Problem

Domain packet. Codes: **KG**, **FG**, **EV**, **SU**, **DE**, **SD** (see README). **P3** = Phase 3;
**NEW** = mined here; **NEW SRC** = newly registered.

Centre of gravity: the Gītā read through the three Vedānta schools, with Upaniṣadic and
Dharmaśāstra foundations. Mīmāṃsā enters only for KG, and Nyāya and Yoga only where they sharpen SU.
Do not add other schools.

## 1. Core account

Across the major Vedānta traditions, the self (*ātman*) is conscious and does not die. Bodies are
put on and cast off "as a man casts off worn-out clothes" (Gītā 2.22). Death is certain for the born
and birth for the dead (2.27). Action shapes future birth (BAU IV.4.5). Moral knowledge (*dharma*) is
known chiefly from scripture: "the scripture is thy authority" (Gītā 16.24). Manu lists Veda,
tradition, the conduct of the virtuous and one's own satisfaction (Manu 2.6, 2.12), and Mīmāṃsā holds
that dharma is known only by injunction (P3). Failure to do the good is desire and wrath, born of
*rajas*, veiling knowledge through senses, mind and reason (3.36–43, P3). Evil is grounded in
ignorance (Advaita: superimposition, "the cause of all evil") or karma-contracted knowledge
(Viśiṣṭādvaita). It is never an independent principle, and God is not partial or cruel because
inequalities track beings' own karma (BSBh II.1.34–36, P3). Suffering is karmic consequence and
the felt character of a life of attachment: "to the discriminating all is nothing but pain" (YS
II.15). The task is to endure transient pleasure and pain (Gītā 2.14) and escape *future* pain (YS
II.16). Self-deception is structural: the intellect "enveloped in darkness, sees adharma as dharma"
(Gītā 18.32). Remedy by school: knowledge (Advaita), devotion and surrender with grace (Rāmānuja,
Madhva).

## 3. Question map

### KG
- **Main answer.** Dharma is known primarily from revelation. "The scripture is thy authority in
  deciding as to what ought to be done and what ought not to be done" (Gītā 16.24). Neglecting it,
  one "acts under the impulse of desire" (16.23), which Śaṅkara glosses as the Veda's injunctions and
  prohibitions. Manu: "The whole Veda is the (first) source of the sacred law, next the tradition and
  the virtuous conduct of those who know", the customs of holy men and "self-satisfaction" (2.6). The
  "fourfold means" are Veda, tradition, the customs of the virtuous and "one's own pleasure" (2.12).
  Bühler's commentators limit self-satisfaction to the virtuous and to unsettled or optional cases.
  Knowledge of dharma is for those "not given to the acquisition of wealth and to the gratification
  of their desires" (2.13). Reasoners who scorn śruti and smṛti are condemned (2.11). Mīmāṃsā: dharma
  is not knowable by perception or inference, only by injunction (Ślokavārttika, Codanā 16–18, P3).
  Natural moral discernment: "The good and the pleasant approach man: the wise… distinguishes them"
  (Kaṭha I.2.1–2).
- **Internal distinction.** Mīmāṃsā (dharma = injunction) vs. Vedānta (dharma serves liberation;
  Advaita finally transcends dharma and adharma, Śaṅkara on 18.66, P3). Bhakti schools stress the
  inner Ruler (18.61, P3).
- **Uncertainty.** "Self-satisfaction" (*ātmatuṣṭi*) is a contested category. Present it with
  Bühler's commentators, not as an autonomous conscience.

### FG
- As in Phase 3 (concise): Gītā 3.36–43 with Śaṅkara on 3.37, 3.39; Rāmānuja on 3.37; 2.62–63;
  6.5–6.

### EV
- As in Phase 3: adhyāsa as "cause of all evil" (BSBh intro); karma explains unequal lots without
  divine partiality (BSBh II.1.34–36); guṇa dynamics (Gītā 14, 16); Nyāya faults (NS 4.1.3–6);
  Madhva's graded souls (contested, IEP §3). Gītā 16.21's triple gate (lust, wrath, greed) is the
  textual centre for moral evil.

### SU
- **Main answer.** Suffering follows from past action (karma). Inequality of lots tracks beings'
  merit and demerit, so God is neither partial nor cruel (BSBh II.1.34–36, P3). It is also built into
  conditioned existence: "as being the pains which are mutations and anxieties and subliminal
  impressions… to the discriminating all is nothing but pain" (YS II.15). "That which is to be
  escaped is pain yet to come" (II.16). The Nyāya chain runs from misapprehension through faults,
  activity and birth to pain (NS 1.1.2, P3). The Gītā's response is endurance and equanimity:
  sense-contacts cause heat, cold, pleasure and pain; "they come and go, they are impermanent. Them
  endure bravely" (2.14). The wise "is not distressed" (2.13). Ultimately suffering belongs to the
  body-mind wrongly identified as self (adhyāsa).
- **Internal distinction.** Advaita: suffering ends with knowledge that the Self was never the
  sufferer. Theistic Vedānta: suffering is real for the real soul and ended by grace.
- **Best passages.** Gītā 2.13–14; YS II.15–16; BSBh II.1.34–36; NS 1.1.2.

### DE
- **Main answer.** The body dies and the self does not. "He is not born, nor does He ever die… He
  is not slain when the body is slain" (Gītā 2.20; cf. Kaṭha I.2.18–19). Bodies are exchanged like
  clothes (2.22). "To that which is born, death is indeed certain; and to that which is dead, birth is
  certain. Wherefore… thou oughtst not to grieve" (2.27). What carries over is action and knowledge:
  "as is his desire, so is his will… so is his deed… that he will reap" (BAU IV.4.5). The self moves
  to a new body like a caterpillar between blades of grass (IV.4.3). The Kaṭha frames death as the
  great question. Naciketas asks Death whether a man exists after dying and refuses every substitute
  boon: long life, wealth, pleasures, because "these things last till to-morrow" (Kaṭha I.1.20–29).
  Death is thus wrong only as the outcome of rebirth (saṃsāra). The goal is release from the cycle,
  not merely survival.
- **Internal distinction (material).** On 2.12 ("never did I not exist, nor thou, nor these
  rulers") Śaṅkara says the plural refers to bodies and "does not mean that there are more than one
  Self". Rāmānuja cites the same verse for a real, eternal plurality of selves that persists even in
  release (on 5.16, pp. 182–183). This decides whether death's ultimate answer is identity with
  Brahman or the individual's eternal life with God.
- **Best passages.** Gītā 2.12–13, 2.20, 2.22, 2.27; Kaṭha I.1.20–29, I.2.18–19; BAU IV.4.3–5.

### SD
- **Main answer.** Superimposition of non-self on Self is "natural" and beginningless (BSBh intro,
  P3), the paradigm of self-misidentification. The intellect can itself be corrupted. The rajasic
  intellect "wrongly understands dharma and a-dharma". The tamasic intellect, "enveloped in darkness,
  sees adharma as dharma and all things perverted" (Gītā 18.31–32, with Śaṅkara: "a perverted view of
  all things"). Desire veils wisdom "as fire… by smoke, as a mirror by rust" (3.38–40, P3). The
  demonic person's self-justifying worldview: the world "without a Lord… brought about by lust" (16.8,
  P3).
- **Best passages.** Gītā 18.31–32; 3.38–40; 16.7–18; BSBh intro pp. 3–9.
