**Lane method.** Pāli canon (early Buddhism and Theravāda baseline) → the epistemological school (Dignāga via SEP;

## 1. Core account

The canon is neither credulous nor rationalist. Its epistemic ideal is **direct knowledge** (*abhiññā*): seeing
"for yourselves" what is skilful and unskilful (AN 3.65), and ultimately "directly realizing the ultimate truth and
seeing it with penetrating wisdom" (MN 95:20). Faith in a teacher has a place, but it should be tested and grounded:
- the Buddha himself may be investigated, and faith "settled, rooted, and planted… with these words and phrases" is
  "grounded faith that's based on evidence" (MN 47:16);
- disciples speak only of "what you have known and seen and realized for yourselves", not "out of esteem for our
  teacher" (MN 38:24).

The **Kālāma Sutta** (AN 3.65), read in context, is advice to people "doubting and uncertain" amid rival teachers:
- it lists ten insufficient grounds, including oral transmission, lineage, scripture, logic and inference, and the
  teacher's status (4.1);
- it gives a practical test: whether things are "unskillful, blameworthy, criticized by sensible people", leading to
  harm (4.3);
- it adds two "consolations" that hold whether or not there is rebirth (43–44).

It is not a charter for autonomous free inquiry in general (ME caveat). It applies the test to ethical
practice and invokes "sensible people" as a social check.

**MN 95 (Caṅkī)** gives the canon's theory of grounds. Five bases (faith, endorsement, oral transmission, reasoned
reflection, acceptance of a view) "can… turn out in two different ways": "well deliberated" views can be "vacuous,
hollow, and false". So one "preserves truth" by saying "this is my faith" without concluding "only this is true"
(95:14–15). Awakening to truth comes through a path that starts with testing a teacher, placing faith, listening,
examining and striving (95:20–22).

The Buddha taught only what is useful for liberation: the siṃsapā leaves (SN 56.31). The order of conditioned arising
holds "whether Realized Ones arise or not" (SN 12.20, REUSE UR). He is to be known through the Dhamma: "one who sees
the teaching sees me" (SN 22.87).

The **epistemological school** makes this rigorous. Dharmakīrti's treatise opens: "All successful human action is
preceded by right knowledge" (NB I.1). There are two sources of knowledge, perception and inference (NB I.2). This
corresponds to two kinds of object: unique particulars and constructed generalities (SEP Dharmakīrti §2.1; Dignāga).
A pramāṇa is "non-deceptive cognition", glossed as establishing causal efficacy (*pramāṇam avisaṃvādi jñānam
arthakriyā-sthitiḥ*, PV II.1, our translation). Good inference requires a mark with three features (*trairūpya*, NB
II.5ff.). Necessary connection rests only on identity of nature (*svabhāva*) or causation (*kārya*). "A fact which is
not so dependent upon another one cannot be invariably and necessarily concomitant with" it (NB II.21–24).

Scripture's role is limited:
- it is not to be used on matters decidable by perception or reasoning;
- on "radically inaccessible" matters (karmic details) it is relied on "because there is no other way", and even then
  it is fallible (PV I.213–217 per SEP §4);
- the Buddha is a pramāṇa "in that way" (*tadvat pramāṇaṃ bhagavān*, PV II.7), meaning reliable, not an eternal
  authority (PV II.8–10 deny any eternal pramāṇa).

**Madhyamaka** turns epistemology on itself. Nāgārjuna asks how the pramāṇas are established:
- not by other pramāṇas, which would regress (Vv 31–33);
- not by themselves (Vv 40);
- not mutually, by their objects, or by chance (Vv 51).

He claims to have no thesis of his own (Vv 29). The two truths remain: the ultimate is not taught without the
conventional (MMK 24.8–10, REUSE UR). **Yogācāra** holds that what is conceptually constructed "does not exist", and
"all this is cognition-only" (Triṃśikā 17).

## 4. Question map

- **KP (anchor).**
  - *Main answer:* knowledge arises causally. Perception is a non-conceptual, non-erroneous contact with momentary
    particulars (NB I.4, I.12); inference constructs generalities that are reliable when the mark is grounded in real
    identity or causation (NB II.19–24). A cognition is valid when it is non-deceptive, i.e. it leads to the object's
    expected causal efficacy (PV II.1). The ultimate knowledge is direct insight into conditionality, impermanence and
    non-self, verified in practice (MN 95:20).
  - *Texts:* AN 3.65; MN 95; MN 47.
  - *Interpreters:* Dharmakīrti NB I–II, PV II.1; SEP §2.1.
  - *Internal differences:*
    - Theravāda canon (practical verification) vs. the Dharmakīrtian school (formal pramāṇa theory);
    - Yogācāra (objects as cognition-only, Tvk 17) vs. Sautrāntika-style realism about particulars;
    - Madhyamaka: the pramāṇas themselves are not established intrinsically (Vv 31–33, 51), so knowledge is
      conventional all the way down.
  - *Exact caveat:* do not assume correspondence realism. SEP describes Dharmakīrti's truth theory as a "weak form of
    correspondence" with a pragmatic theory of justification (§2.1). Also, "right knowledge is uncontradicted
    knowledge" is Dharmottara's gloss (NBṬ on I.1), not Dharmakīrti's sūtra.
  - *Christian comparative issue:* who knows, if there is no enduring self (MH: no-self)? Momentariness makes the
    knower a causal stream. Does that explain the unity of judgment that inference requires (NB II's "reflection")?
- **UA (anchor).**
  - *Main answer:* the Dhamma, not the person of the teacher, is the final reference:
    - "Let the teaching be your island" (DN 16:2.26); "The teaching and training… shall be your Teacher" (DN 16:6.1);
    - claimed Buddha-word is checked against the discourses and the training (AN 4.180);
    - verification is by direct knowledge (AN 3.65; MN 38; MN 47).

    In the epistemological school, scripture is admissible only for radically inaccessible matters and is fallible
    (SEP §4). The Buddha is authoritative as a reliable pramāṇa (PV II.7), not as an eternal one (II.8–10).
  - *Internal differences:*
    1. Early canon (Dhamma-Vinaya as reference) vs. Mahāyāna sūtra authority (not researched here; Revelation &
       History).
    2. Dharmakīrti's "surprisingly fallibilist" view of scripture (SEP §4) vs. devotional traditions.
    3. Madhyamaka's "no thesis" (Vv 29).
  - *Exact caveat:* the Kālāma Sutta is not "believe nothing on authority". The same canon commends faith in a tested
    teacher (MN 95:20, 33) and requires checking against the received discourses (AN 4.180). Present both.
  - *Christian comparative issue:* the final court is the Dhamma as verified in experience. What verifies the claim
    that liberating insight is veridical rather than a practised state? MN 95's "preserving truth" principle is a
    natural point of contact.
- **LB (medium).**
  - *Main answer:* Buddhism has a sophisticated logic. It is not "anti-logic". Validity is a matter of good reasons
    (*saddhetu*) satisfying the triple criterion, ascertained by the knower (SEP §3.1; NB II.5ff.). Necessity rests
    on real relations of identity or causation (NB II.19–24), so logic binds because it tracks how dharmas are
    actually related. On Dharmakīrti's nominalism those relations are between particulars, with universals
    conceptual (SEP §1.1).
  - *Internal difference:*
    - Madhyamaka uses reductio (*prasaṅga*) without asserting a thesis (Vv 29);
    - Nāgārjuna asks what establishes the pramāṇas at all (Vv 31–33, 51);
    - MN 76 warns that a teacher relying on logic may reason well or badly, truly or falsely.
  - *Exact caveat:* the canon's cautions are about logic *as a basis for metaphysical views* (AN 3.65; DN 1, REUSE),
    not about valid inference. SEP warns against equating Buddhist "logic" with formal validity (§3.1).
  - *Christian comparative issue:* if necessity rests on causal identity between momentary particulars, what makes
    *those* relations necessary and universal? SEP §1.5 shows the tension inside Dharmakīrti himself.
- **IN (medium).**
  - *Main answer:* inference to the unobserved is warranted when grounded in *svabhāva* or *kārya* relations (NB
    II.19–24). These are established by observation of co-presence and co-absence (*anvaya-vyatireka*; the triple or
    fivefold examination), and "a fact which is not so dependent… cannot be invariably and necessarily concomitant"
    (NB II.21). At the canonical level, the order of dependent origination holds "whether Realized Ones arise or not"
    (SN 12.20).
  - *Internal difference:* whether Dharmakīrti escapes an induction problem. Gillon argues he does not. Tillemans
    reads him as distinguishing how we discover causal relations (fallible) from what causality is (natures that
    necessitate) (SEP §1.5).
  - *Exact caveat:* this is not a reply to Hume's problem in Western terms. It is a theory of why inference
    sometimes yields certainty. Do not say "Buddhism solved Hume", and do not say "Buddhism has no account."
  - *Christian comparative issue:* SN 12.20's impersonal law "persists" without a lawgiver. The comparative question
    is what grounds *that* stability (cf. UR OR cell).

## 6. Important internal distinctions

- **Canonical direct knowledge vs. Dharmakīrti's pramāṇa theory vs. Madhyamaka critique of pramāṇa** (KP, LB).
- **Tested faith (MN 95, 47) vs. freedom from authority (AN 3.65)**: one teaching, two emphases.
- **Dhamma-Vinaya as reference (AN 4.180) vs. later sūtra authority** (deferred to Revelation & History).
- **Dharmakīrti's nominalism vs. necessitating causal natures** (SEP §1.5): an internal tension for IN.
- **Yogācāra cognition-only vs. Sautrāntika externalism**.

## Cross-domain consistency

- **Fits UR.** Dependent origination (SN 12.20) and momentariness (SEP Dharmakīrti §1.2; heading only, not mined here) are the metaphysics behind Dharmakīrti's
  causal theory of inference. The two truths (MMK 24.8–10) reappear as Nāgārjuna's conventional pramāṇas (Vv 51).
- **Real tension 1 (no-self).** Inference requires the knower to remember the pervasion and connect it to the subject.
  Uddyotakara's *liṅga-parāmarśa* (SEP Epistemology India §5) assumes a unity of cognition across moments. Buddhist
  epistemologists explain this as a causal continuum. Whether that suffices is the natural pressure point. Record it,
  do not resolve it.
- **Real tension 2 (nominalism vs. necessity).** If universals are conceptual constructions, the necessity of
  *svabhāva* inference is hard to locate in reality. SEP §1.5 shows Dharmakīrti appealing to causal natures "in"
  things, which sits uneasily with strict nominalism.
- **Real tension 3 (Madhyamaka vs. Dharmakīrti).** Nāgārjuna's Vv 51 denies that pramāṇas are established at all.
  Dharmakīrti builds on them. Later Indo-Tibetan syntheses reconcile them at the conventional level. Present them as
  two Buddhist positions.
