## 1. Core account

Hindu epistemology is organized around **pramāṇa**, the "means of right knowledge". Schools differ on how many there
are:
- Cārvāka: perception only;
- Vaiśeṣika and Buddhists: perception and inference;
- Sāṅkhya: plus testimony;
- Nyāya: plus comparison, making four;
- Mīmāṃsā and Vedānta: also presumption and non-cognition

(Vidyabhusana's note to NS 1.1.3, pp. 2–3).

Nyāya gives the canonical definitions:
- perception arises from contact of sense and object and is determinate and "non-erratic" (NS 1.1.4);
- inference is "preceded by perception" (NS 1.1.5), as from smoke to fire;
- testimony is "the instructive assertion of a reliable person" (*āpta*), whether sage, Ārya or outsider (NS 1.1.7).

When challenged that pramāṇas need further pramāṇas, Nyāya answers with the lamp. As a lamp illumines objects and is
itself seen, pramāṇas establish both their objects and, in their turn, one another (NS 2.1.19).

Knowledge is generally treated as **realist and defeasible**. A cognition counts until a defeater (*bādhaka*)
arises. Suppositional reasoning (*tarka*) adjudicates conflicts between apparent sources (SEP Epistemology in
Classical Indian Philosophy §§5, 8). Mīmāṃsā makes this a principle: validity is **intrinsic** (*svataḥ*
*prāmāṇya*). "Authoritativeness is inherent in all Means of Right Notion." Falsity comes only from a later
contradicting cognition or a detected defect in its cause (Ślokavārttika, Sūtra II vv. 47–61).

The Veda's authority is the decisive question for UA, and the schools split:
- **Mīmāṃsā:** the Veda has no author, so there are no authorial defects, so it cannot be faulted (vv. 62–68).
- **Nyāya:** the Veda is reliable "like the spell and medical science, because of the reliability of their authors"
  (NS 2.1.69). Vidyabhusana's gloss rejects authority-by-eternity.
- **Vedānta:** Brahman, being beyond the senses and without marks, can be known only from śruti. Reasoning that
  "disregards the holy texts" has "no proper foundation", because logicians refute one another endlessly. Reasoning
  is admissible "in so far as it does not contradict the Vedānta-texts" and to understand them (Śaṅkara on BS I.1.2,
  II.1.11; Rāmānuja on I.1.3; Madhva on I.1.3).

Even so, the Upaniṣads themselves rank the Vedas as "lower knowledge". The "higher knowledge is that by which the
Indestructible is apprehended" (Muṇḍaka I.1.4–5). Brahman "is not understood by those who understand it" (Kena II.3).
For Advaita, then, final knowledge is **direct realization** (*anubhava*), which śruti occasions but which ends the
whole pramāṇa apparatus. The pramāṇas, scripture included, "have for their object that which is dependent on
Nescience" (Śaṅkara, BS introduction).

## 4. Question map

- **KP (anchor).**
  - *Main answer (by school):*
    - **Nyāya** (and Viśiṣṭādvaita/Dvaita): the self is a real knower; sense–object contact yields non-erratic
      perception; inference, comparison and testimony extend it; error is corrected by further pramāṇa and *tarka*.
    - **Mīmāṃsā:** every cognition is intrinsically valid until defeated.
    - **Advaita:** empirical knowledge is valid *within* *vyavahāra* (the conventional, transactional level), but
      presupposes the superimposition of self on body. The final knowledge of Brahman is non-dual realization, which
      no pramāṇa can objectify.
  - *Texts:* Kena II.1–3; Muṇḍaka I.1.4–5; BAU II.4.5.
  - *Interpreters:* NS 1.1.3–7, 2.1.19; Ślokavārttika II.47–61; Śaṅkara intro, I.1.2; Rāmānuja I.1.1; Madhva via IEP.
  - *Internal difference:* Rāmānuja's realist objection to Advaita. If all cognition were erroneous, that very
    cognition would be too (IEP, "Argument from Epistemology", citing the *Great Siddhānta*). All pramāṇas, even indeterminate perception, present
    differenced objects (pp. 40–41). Madhva argues that if perception is not innately valid, scripture itself, known
    through perception, collapses (IEP).
  - *Exact caveat:* do not assume correspondence realism for Advaita. Its pramāṇas are realist *within* the empirical
    level and sublated by Brahman-knowledge (UR: two levels).
  - *Christian comparative issue:* why mind and world correspond. Nyāya has a creator (Udayana) who could ground it.
    Advaita grounds it in the one Self but then makes the knower–known duality itself a product of *avidyā*.
- **UA (anchor).**
  - *Main answer:* for supersensible matters (dharma, Brahman), **śruti** is the final authority. "The scripture is thy
    authority" (Gītā 16.24). Śruti and smṛti "must not be called into question", and whoever scorns them by "the
    Institutes of dialectics" is cast out "as an atheist and a scorner of the Veda" (Manu II.10–11). Reasoning serves
    interpretation (Madhva I.1.3; Śaṅkara I.1.2).
  - *Internal differences:*
    1. *Why* the Veda is authoritative: authorless (Mīmāṃsā) vs. reliable author (Nyāya NS 2.1.69).
    2. *Scope*: the Gītā and Mīmāṃsā authority of śāstra concerns "what ought to be done". Vedānta extends it to
       Brahman.
    3. *Canon*: Madhva counts the Mahābhārata and Mūla Rāmāyaṇa as *śāstra*.
    4. *Finality*: Advaita treats even śruti as lower knowledge relative to realization (Muṇḍaka I.1.4–5; Śaṅkara
       intro). Theistic Vedānta keeps scripture as the permanent norm.
  - *Exact caveat:* do not say "Hinduism accepts the Vedas" and stop. The same śruti yields three incompatible Vedānta
    readings (UR) and no school-neutral court adjudicates them. That structural point belongs in the comparative
    response, without overclaiming.
  - *Christian comparative issue:* self-attestation (autopistia, WCF 1.4) vs. intrinsic validity (*svataḥ
    prāmāṇya*). A personal speaker (Nyāya, Reformed) vs. authorless revelation (Mīmāṃsā).
- **LB (medium).**
  - *Main answer:* logic is treated as the theory of valid inference within epistemology (SEP §5). Nyāya's
    five-membered argument (NS 1.1.32) and its fallacies (NS 1.2.4–9) govern public reasoning. *Tarka* uses reductio to
    eliminate rival theses (NS 1.1.40). The binding force of inference rests on *vyāpti*, a real relation of pervasion
    in the world, which Nyāya realism regards as discovered, not conventional.
  - *Exact caveat:* do not project the modern formal/material distinction. Indian schools count a valid but unsound
    argument as fallacious, because no knowledge results (SEP §5). There is no developed theory of "why logic binds"
    in the Western sense. The nearest is Mīmāṃsā's intrinsic validity and Nyāya's realism about pervasion.
  - *Christian comparative issue:* Nyāya agrees that logical relations are discovered features of a real world. The
    question is what grounds that world's intelligibility (Udayana's Īśvara is the Hindu answer most like the
    Reformed one).
- **IN (medium).**
  - *Main answer:* inference depends on knowing *vyāpti* (e.g. wherever smoke, fire). The Cārvāka argues it can never
    be known:
    - perception does not reach past and future cases;
    - inference would regress;
    - testimony and comparison fail;
    - the test for hidden conditions (*upādhi*) is circular;

    so the success of inference is "accidental" (*Sarvadarśanasaṃgraha* pp. 5–9). Nyāya answers that pervasion is
    established by repeated observation of co-presence and co-absence, the absence of known counterexamples or
    conditions, and *tarka* (SEP §§5, 8).
  - *Interpreters:* Śaṅkara's opponent appeals to everyday action assuming "past, present, and future are uniform" (BS
    II.1.11). Śaṅkara accepts this for worldly reasoning while denying reason any foundation regarding Brahman.
  - *Exact caveat:* this is the Indian form of the problem, not a translation of Hume. Answer it in terms of
    *vyāpti*/*upādhi*.
  - *Christian comparative issue:* the Cārvāka objection is answered by method (observation, *tarka*). The Hindu lane
    offers no providential guarantee unless one takes Udayana's theistic route or the order of *ṛta*/*dharma* (UR).

## 6. Important internal distinctions

- **Pramāṇa counts by school** (NS 1.1.3 note).
- ***Svataḥ* (Mīmāṃsā, Vedānta) vs. *parataḥ* (Nyāya) validity** (SEP §3; Ślokavārttika II).
- **Authorless Veda (Mīmāṃsā) vs. reliable-author Veda (Nyāya) vs. śruti-only for Brahman (Vedānta).**
- **Advaita two levels vs. Viśiṣṭādvaita/Dvaita realism** about what pramāṇas deliver.
- **Śruti as permanent norm (theistic Vedānta) vs. śruti as "lower knowledge" superseded by realization (Advaita,
  Muṇḍaka I.1.4–5).**
- **Cārvāka scepticism about inference vs. Nyāya's method for *vyāpti*.**

## Cross-domain consistency

- **Fits UR.** Advaita's two levels (UR, BS II.1.14) now appear epistemically: pramāṇas are valid at the
  *vyāvahārika* level and sublated at the *pāramārthika*.
- **Real tension (Advaita).** If pramāṇas, *including the Vedic texts*, "have for their object that which is dependent
  on Nescience" (intro), how can śruti yield knowledge of Brahman? Śaṅkara's answer is that śruti removes ignorance
  without objectifying Brahman, and that the final intuition is self-validating. This is coherent within Advaita but
  is the lane's deepest epistemic tension, and Rāmānuja presses it (IEP; Śrī Bhāṣya I.1.1). Record it, don't resolve
  it.
- **Real tension (Nyāya vs. Vedānta).** Nyāya proves Īśvara by inference (UR, Udayana). Rāmānuja and Śaṅkara deny
  that inference reaches the Lord (I.1.3; II.1.11). The same Hindu family disagrees on whether natural theology is
  possible.
