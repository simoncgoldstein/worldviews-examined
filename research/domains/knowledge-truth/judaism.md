## 1. Core account

Jewish epistemology is built around a **covenantal event attested by a people**. Israel "saw with your own eyes" and
heard God's voice at Horeb, and is commanded to make it known to children and grandchildren (Deut 4:9–10). The
argument of Deut 4:32–35 is explicitly evidential: "has anything as grand as this ever happened…? It has been
clearly demonstrated to you that the ETERNAL alone is God." The foundation of belief in Moses is therefore not
miracles, which magic could imitate, but the shared experience of Sinai. "Our eyes saw, and not a stranger's"
(Maimonides, YT 8:1–2). Halevi makes the same point: the Rabbi begins not with "the Creator of the world" but with
"the God of Abraham… who led the children of Israel out of Egypt". Israel knew this "first from personal experience,
and afterwards through uninterrupted tradition, which is equal to the former" (*Kuzari* I.25).

Reason is a genuine and commanded source. Saadia's prolegomena give the classic list of the roots of knowledge
(*Emunot* Intro 5): sense perception; rational knowledge, such as that truth is good and lying base; and **necessary
inference**, "what must be believed lest the sensed or the rational be denied" (smoke → fire; soul from its acts). To
these the monotheists add a fourth: **reliable tradition** (*ha-haggadah ha-ne'emanah*), built on the first three.
Each has criteria for avoiding error (Intro 5.3–5.6). Inquiry is permitted and even commanded: Isa 40:21, "Have you
not discerned how the earth was founded?" (Intro 6.1).

Why revelation, if reason can reach the truths? Because inquiry takes time, many cannot complete it, and doubt
disturbs it. So God gave the truths at once through prophets with public signs. Women, children and those unable to
reason are not left without religion (Intro 6.3).

Authority is layered:
- **Written Torah**, given at Sinai and "not in the heavens" (Deut 30:12);
- **Oral Torah**, transmitted in a chain (Avot 1:1);
- the **authorized court** (Deut 17:8–11), whose ruling is to be followed "even if he tells you of the right that it
  is left" (Rashi as quoted by Ramban on Deut 17:11);
- the majority of the sages, who rule even against a heavenly voice ("It is not in heaven", BM 59b).

Reason tests prophetic claims: a prophet who urges what reason finds impossible is not asked for a miracle, "for
there is no miracle for the impossible" (Saadia III.8). A prophet who adds to or changes the Torah is false whatever
wonder he performs (YT 9:1).

Limits are real. "Concealed acts concern the ETERNAL our God" (Deut 29:28). Human intellect has a boundary
(*Guide* I.31). What God *is* is known only by negation and through his acts (UR, *Guide* I.51–60).

## 4. Question map

- **KP (anchor).**
  - *Main answer:* God made humans with senses and reason that genuinely know the world. Saadia's roots of knowledge
    are reliable when used with care and their errors can be corrected (mirror images, bent sticks in water, dreams:
    Intro 5.3). Wisdom begins in "the fear of GOD" (Prov 1:7) and is God's gift (Prov 2:6).
  - *Texts:* Prov 1:7; 2:6; Isa 40:21; Avot 3:14 (image).
  - *Interpreters:* Saadia Intro 5; Maimonides YT 1:1, *Guide* I.31; Halevi I.25.
  - *Internal difference:* Saadia (confident: reason will reach what prophecy gives) vs. Halevi (speculation "open to
    many doubts", I.13; historical knowledge prior) vs. Maimonides (demonstration within bounds; what lies beyond is
    received from prophecy, *Guide* I.31, II.25).
  - *Exact caveat:* Jewish epistemology is neither pure rationalism nor pure traditionalism. Saadia insists the two
    converge (Intro 6.2).
  - *Christian comparative issue:* the shared Hebrew-Bible ground (creation, image, wisdom) is common to both lanes.
    The question is whether Sinai testimony alone, without the Christological fulfilment, explains the knowledge the
    Tanakh itself anticipates.
- **UA (anchor).**
  - *Main answer:* the God who spoke at Sinai, through the Written and Oral Torah as authoritatively interpreted by the
    sages. Reason is the instrument of interpretation and a test of claimed prophecy, not a rival authority.
  - *Texts:* Deut 4:9–10, 32–35; 17:8–11; 30:12; Avot 1:1; BM 59b.
  - *Interpreters:* YT 8:1–3, 9:1; Ramban on Deut 17:11; Halevi I.25; Saadia III.8.
  - *Internal differences:*
    1. Maimonides holds that a demonstrated truth governs how Scripture is read (*Guide* II.25), while Halevi
       subordinates speculation to tradition.
    2. Ramban: follow the court even if it errs (Deut 17:11), because the Torah was given "as taught by them".
    3. Karaites (outside the rabbinic lane) rejected the Oral Torah. Mention only as context.
  - *Exact caveat:* do not frame the Oral Torah as "human tradition added to Scripture" in the lane's own voice. In its
    own terms it is the transmission of Sinai (Avot 1:1). Comparative evaluation belongs in Revelation & History.
  - *Christian comparative issue:* where final interpretive authority lies (court and majority vs. Scripture
    interpreting Scripture, WCF 1.9–10); BM 59b's majority over a heavenly voice is the sharpest contrast.
- **LB (medium).**
  - *Main answer:* there is no rabbinic doctrine of logic, but the philosophers are explicit that the impossible is
    not subject to God's power:
    - Saadia: God is not praised for making five greater than ten, or undoing yesterday, "for all this is nothing"
      (II.13);
    - Maimonides: the impossible "has a permanent and constant property, which is not the result of some agent"
      (*Guide* III.15).

    Reason grasps this by a faculty distinct from imagination (*Guide* I.73).
  - *Texts:* Deut 32:4 ("A faithful God, never false"); Jer 10:10, which Saadia II.13 cites. JPS renders it "the
    ETERNAL is truly God", while Saadia's Hebrew reads *Adonai Elohim emet*.
  - *Exact caveat:* Maimonides grounds the impossible in a stable nature "not the result of some agent". He does not
    say it is grounded in God's nature or intellect. Do not assimilate this to Augustine's "origin with God". Also
    flag that the Talmudic hermeneutical rules (*middot*) are a logic of interpretation, not a theory of logical
    necessity.
  - *Christian comparative issue:* both lanes reject voluntarism about the impossible. The difference is whether
    logical necessity is *grounded in* God (Reformed) or is simply the fixed boundary within which divine power acts
    (Maimonides).
- **IN (medium).**
  - *Main answer:* nature's regularity is the Creator's ordinance ("My covenant with day and night—the laws of heaven
    and earth", Jer 33:25). Ordinarily "the world follows its course" (*olam ke-minhago noheg*, AZ 54b), even when
    people sin.
  - *Interpreters:* Maimonides defends stable natures against the kalām's view that regularity is mere "habit" like a
    king's custom of riding (*Guide* I.73 tenth premise). Nachmanides stresses hidden miracles within the natural
    order (Exod 13:16, REUSE UR).
  - *Exact caveat:* no classical Jewish source addresses Hume's problem. The material gives a theological ground for
    expecting regularity, not an argument against inductive scepticism. Do not overclaim.
  - *Christian comparative issue:* Jer 33:25 is shared ground. AZ 54b's "world follows its course" is a useful parallel
    to WCF 5's ordinary providence.

## 6. Important internal distinctions

- **Saadia's convergence vs. Halevi's priority of tradition vs. Maimonides' demonstration-with-limits** (KP, UA).
- **Written vs. Oral Torah vs. the authorized court vs. philosophical reason** (UA). They are layered, not rivals,
  within the rabbinic lane. Distinguish *interpretive* authority (court, majority) from *revelatory* authority
  (Sinai).
- **Maimonides vs. kalām on natural regularity** (*Guide* I.73): stable natures vs. divine habit. This is the Jewish
  side of the debate the Islam packet records from the other side.
- **Impossible as uncaused boundary (Maimonides) vs. as "nothing" (Saadia):** compatible, but differently worded.

## Cross-domain consistency

- **Negative theology (UR).** Maimonides' *Guide* I.51–60 (UR) limits what can be *known of God's essence*, yet YT 1:1
  makes knowing God the foundation of foundations. Not a contradiction: what is known is that God exists and acts,
  and what he is not. A public answer should not imply positive knowledge of the divine essence on Maimonides' terms.
- **Halevi vs. Maimonides (UR lesson).** The UR review noted that Halevi shared the act/negative attribute theory. Here
  the difference is epistemic (tradition vs. demonstration), not about attributes.
- **Real tension.** Saadia's claim that inquiry will reach everything the prophets taught (Intro 6.2) sits uneasily
  with Maimonides' boundary on the intellect (*Guide* I.31) and his admission that creation is not demonstrable
  (II.25). The lane's internal disagreement is real and should be shown.
