## 6. Physicalist accounts examined

- **Papineau 2001, pp. 3–4, 8–9 and 30–33.**
  - Physicalism is an ontological thesis, held in subtle forms: supervenience, realization and token identity.
  - The completeness of physics supplies the key premise of the causal argument.
  - Papineau treats completeness as empirically established (the argument from physiology), but grants that there is "no knock-down argument".
- **Papineau 2011.**
  - pp. 6–8: mind–brain identities are a posteriori.
  - The common account is that phenomenal concepts are not descriptive, so conscious facts cannot be derived a priori from physical ones. The gap lies in our concepts, and most philosophers reject Chalmers' and Jackson's inference to the falsity of materialism.
  - pp. 11–13: Papineau's own view is that identities leave nothing unexplained and the felt gap is an "intuition of dualism", "a problem for materialists, not for materialism".
  - pp. 18–19: the cause of that intuition is an open empirical question.
- **Representational and higher-order theories.** Only Frankish §1.1 places them ("conservative realism"). They are named in one sentence and not developed.

**Public text.** The view says most naturalists are physicalists (Papineau pp. 3–4, consistent with `ultimate-reality`), who hold that experience is a brain process "or is realized in one". The Deep dive gives the phenomenal-concept reply and Papineau's view. Physicalism is never described as "just chemicals", and no physicalist is said to deny experience.

## 7. Illusionism examined

- **Frankish 2016.**
  - Strong illusionism denies that experiences have phenomenal properties and sets the "illusion problem": why they seem to have them (§§1.1–1.2).
  - It does not deny conscious experience in an inclusive sense, nor an inner life; experiences are "like something" in a functional sense (§§1.6–1.7).
  - Illusionism "remains a minority position" (opening).
- **Dennett 2015.**
  - §1: the "consciousness is an illusion" slogan misreads his view.
  - §2: what he denies is a second "medium" of qualia.
  - §3: Hume's "strange inversion" explains the seeming.
  - §5: a "user-illusion" explains why qualia seem simple.

**Public text.** "Illusionists deny that experience has the special inner qualities it seems to have" (view). "They do not deny perception, feeling or an inner life, only that these consist in irreducible qualities" (Deep dive). Dennett's own disclaimer is reported. The bare phrase "illusionists believe consciousness is an illusion" is not used.

## 8. Panpsychism and Russellian monism examined

  - p. 15: protophenomenal properties.
  - p. 18: Russellian monism is "broad physicalism with the denial of narrow physicalism"; whether it counts as physicalism is "a largely verbal question".
  - pp. 22–23: the combination problem.
- **SEP Panpsychism.**

## 11. Chalmers and the hard problem

- **What Chalmers argues.** Explaining functions (discrimination, integration, report, attention, wakefulness) leaves "a further unanswered question": why their performance is accompanied by experience (§3). This is a conceptual point that further neuroscience "will not affect" (§5). His positive view is naturalistic dualism (§6).
- **How the site presents it.** Chalmers is described as "a critic of that view" (physicalism), and his argument as "a conceptual argument, not a laboratory finding". The text says the metaphysical question is settled by neither empirical success nor conceivability arguments alone. It also says few accept his step to the falsity of physicalism.
- **Avoided.** The site nowhere says science has shown that consciousness cannot be physical.

## 19. Cleanthes attribution

**Text.** Davidhume.org, D 9.1–9.11, read 2026-10-12.
- Demea gives the a priori argument (9.1, 9.3).
- Cleanthes answers it (9.4–9.9).
- Philo then addresses him: "the reasonings, which you have urged, Cleanthes" (9.10).

**Findings:**
1. The speaker is Cleanthes.
2. The argument is that in an eternal succession each member is caused by its predecessor, so the "whole" needs no further cause. Uniting the parts is "an arbitrary act of the mind" (9.9).
3. The statement is part of the theological dispute: a theist rejecting an a priori proof of God, while defending the design argument.
4. The page previously called him only "Hume's Cleanthes" and implied a general thesis about wholes.
5. The observation can serve as a philosophical resource if the speaker and his causal point are stated.

**Correction:**
- **View.** "A more deflationary thought appears in Hume's *Dialogues*, voiced by Cleanthes, the dialogue's theist…"; "Many treat organisms and systems as genuine unities" now comes first.
- **Deep dive "Hume's whole".** It names him the defender of the design argument and gives the context (9.4–9.9). It says his point concerns causes, and that the wider application is "an extension beyond Cleanthes' own argument". Hume's own view is not inferred.
- **PQ2.** It was "If wholes are only groupings made by the mind…", which pressed a view most naturalists do not hold. It now presses the naturalist account of organisms as real unities: what makes their organization a unity in the thing rather than a pattern we pick out?
- **Unchanged.** PQ1 and the Christian response do not depend on the quotation.
