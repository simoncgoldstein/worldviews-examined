## 5. Saadia, *Emunot ve-De'ot* VII and IX

**Source.** Ibn Tibbon's Hebrew (Leipzig 1864), read on Sefaria. The whole of Treatise VII (chapters 1–8) and Treatise IX, chapters 1–6, were read. Treatise VII survives in two Arabic recensions. Ibn Tibbon renders one of them, and Rosenblatt's Yale translation also renders the variant. Only the Ibn Tibbon Hebrew is cited, and all English is our paraphrase of the Hebrew.

1. **How he establishes resurrection.** VII.1 tests the belief against four sources: nature, reason, Scripture and tradition.
2. **Bodily.** Yes. A decomposed or eaten body's parts are preserved by God for the "time of visitation", and nothing created can annihilate a body (VII.1). The person raised knows "that he is the one who lived, died and revived" (VII.4). VII.6 reads Deuteronomy 32:39: the one who wounded is the one who heals, so the one revived is the one who died.
3. **Scriptural and rational grounds.** Ezekiel 37, Isaiah 26:19, Daniel 12:2 and Deuteronomy 32. VII.2 sets the plain-sense rule: Scripture is read literally unless the senses, reason, another verse or tradition compel otherwise. VII.5 argues that allegorizing the resurrection texts would license allegorizing the commandments, creation and the miracles. VII.3 answers verses such as Job 14:12 as statements of human inability.
4. **Divine power.** Whoever accepts creation from nothing and the exodus miracles cannot deny resurrection, which is "easier and nearer" than creation (VII.1).
5. **Redemption and recompense.** The resurrection happens at the redemption, in this world, for Israel's righteous and penitent (VII.4, VII.8). Those raised eat, drink and possibly marry, do not die again, and pass into the World to Come (VII.8, questions 2, 6 and 7).
6. **Treatise IX.** The World to Come is a new heaven and earth for people who are "bodies and souls" (IX.6). Reward is light and punishment fire, one created substance (IX.5). Bodies are sustained without food, as Moses was on the mountain (IX.5). Reward and punishment belong to body and soul together, "because they are one agent" (IX.5).

The site does not claim that Saadia holds every later eschatological detail. His messianic-age resurrection is distinguished from Nachmanides' post-messianic resurrection.

## 6. Nachmanides, *Sha'ar ha-Gemul*

**Source.** The final gate of *Torat ha-Adam*, in the Hebrew edition Sefaria records as "Mossad Harav Kook, Jerusalem 1963". Whether this is the Chavel *Kitvei Ramban* volume was not confirmed and is not claimed. The text was read through the Sefaria API and is cited by Sefaria paragraph (¶) of 87. A separate tora.ws transcription with an unstated base edition was not used. Chavel's English (1983) was not read and is not quoted.

- **¶64.** Two rabbinic rewards besides the goods of this world. Gan Eden comes immediately after death, as Gehenna does for the wicked. The World to Come is the principal reward.
- **¶¶69–73.** The Mishnah's "World to Come" is ambiguous. Sanhedrin 90a's "measure for measure" (the denier of the resurrection loses a share in it) shows that the World to Come is the world of the resurrection, not the world of souls. Beit Hillel: formation in the World to Come begins with skin, flesh, sinews and bones.
- **¶¶75–77.** Berakhot 17a's "no eating or drinking … enjoying the radiance of the Shekhinah" describes resurrected people. The body is sustained like the soul, as Moses was on the mountain. The objection that such a body would be purposeless is answered: the Creator wills to keep what he formed.
- **¶78, summary.** Souls are rewarded in Gan Eden. The messianic days belong to this world. At their end come the judgment and the resurrection, "the reward that includes body and soul", the great principle and the hope of all who hope. This is the World to Come, everlasting, in which the soul cleaves to supernal knowledge beyond its state in Gan Eden.
- **¶80.** He praises Maimonides' account of the soul's delight in knowing God as "pure words".
- **¶¶82–84 and 87.** Maimonides makes the World to Come bodiless and limits the duration of the resurrection. "All agree" on the resurrection. Nachmanides keeps the people of the resurrection alive forever. With the Spanish authors who call the world of souls the World to Come, the difference is partly one of names.

Intermediate existence (Gan Eden or Gehenna) and final blessedness are kept distinct. The difference in how the authors use "World to Come" is stated in `final-end`'s Deep Dive.

## 7. Maimonides: thirteenth principle and *Treatise on Resurrection*

**Introduction to Helek.** Read in Abelson's public-domain translation (*JQR* 19, 1906, pp. 24–58), with page numbers confirmed from the scan.

- p. 42: resurrection "is one of the cardinal doctrines of the Law of Moses", "the reward of the righteous only"; "man is bound to die and become dissolved".
- pp. 42–44: the days of the Messiah, and the World to Come as the final goal.
- pp. 53–54: the eighth principle.
- p. 56: the thirteenth principle, in full: "The resurrection of the dead. We have already explained this."

**Liturgical distinction.** The *Ani ma'amin* no. 13 in the Metsudah siddur was read in Hebrew and is presented as "a separate liturgical formula, not his wording". Its date and author are not asserted.

**Treatise on Resurrection.** Ibn Tibbon Hebrew, Leipzig 1859, read in full; the text is undivided, so locators are descriptive.

- Written c. 1191 against people in Damascus and Yemen who used his work to deny resurrection.
- Resurrection is "a pillar of the Torah", the return of soul to body, not figurative.
- Denial leads to denying miracles and is heresy; Daniel 12:2 admits no other reading.
- Those raised eat, marry, beget and die after long lives; the deathless life is the bodiless World to Come.

**Editorial rule applied.** Maimonides is never described as denying bodily resurrection. The public text says: "Maimonides does not deny bodily resurrection: he denies that it is the final state." The tension Nachmanides presses is left as Nachmanides' critique and as `after-death` PQ1; the site does not resolve it.

## 10. Revised `final-end`

The final good is presented positively in both forms:

- **Maimonides:** incorporeal knowledge of God, with the raised dying again.
- **Saadia and Nachmanides:** an embodied World to Come.
  - Saadia: a new heaven and earth for people who are bodies and souls, sustained by created light.
  - Nachmanides: the everlasting world of the resurrection, the body sustained by the divine radiance and the soul cleaving more fully to knowledge of God.

The page states the common ground explicitly: the final good is nearness to and knowledge of God, not restored pleasures or political power. The dispute is whether the body shares in it, and Nachmanides praises Maimonides on the soul's delight. The messianic distinction and Nachmanides on the heart (Deuteronomy 30:6) are kept.

**Deep Dive:**

- the political goal (Kings 12, Teshuvah 9:2);
- "Three sequences, one term", on the differing senses of "World to Come";
- "Knowledge, holiness and the body", covering Maimonides' crowns, Nachmanides' answer to the purposeless-body objection and Saadia's light and fire.

The final end is not reduced to political order, earthly prosperity, permanent disembodiment, or resurrection without blessedness.

## 12–14. Modern movements

**Orthodox** (`ultimate-authority`, `revelation`):

- Maimonides' eighth principle: the whole Torah in our hands is from God through Moses, "like a scribe writing from dictation", and its interpretation is of divine origin (Abelson pp. 53–54).
- Hirsch, *Nineteen Letters* (1899 English of the 1836 German), Letter 17, p. 171: "not, however, lowering the Torah to the level of the age".
- Soloveitchik, *Lonely Man of Faith* p. 10: he "unreservedly" accepts the "unity and integrity of the Scriptures and their divine character" and rejects the documentary hypothesis.
- The page says Orthodoxy "maintains" the classical structure. Modern Orthodox–Haredi differences are not characterized, because no source was checked for them.

**Conservative/Masorti** (*Emet Ve-Emunah*, 1988):

- *Revelation, pp. 19–20.* Revelation is "the uncovering of an external source of truth emanating from God". The statement rejects relativism and also literalism without a human component.
- *Three views, reported as a range, not a consensus:*
  - verbal and normative as rabbinically defined;
  - an ineffable encounter put into human words;
  - continuing discovery through nature and history.
- *Halakhah, pp. 21–23.* It is indispensable as "what the Jewish community understands God's will to be". Its sanctity attaches to "the body of the law, not to each law separately". Change comes through rabbinic decision, with the burden of proof on change. Conservative Judaism is therefore not presented as relaxed Orthodoxy.

**Reform:**

| Document | What it says |
|---|---|
| Pittsburgh Platform, 1885, art. 3 | Only the moral laws are binding |
| Pittsburgh Platform, 1885, art. 7 | Bodily resurrection rejected (used in `after-death`) |
| Centenary Perspective, 1976, §3 | "Torah results from the relationship between God and the Jewish people" |
| Centenary Perspective, 1976, §4 | "individual autonomy, choosing and creating on the basis of commitment and knowledge" |
| Statement of Principles, 1999 | "God's ongoing revelation" and the record of the relationship; study "the whole array of mitzvot" and fulfil those that address individuals and community |

The page presents Reform as relocating authority, not rejecting revelation. Each platform is dated, so the development is visible.

## 18. Post-Holocaust theology
