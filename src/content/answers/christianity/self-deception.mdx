## The Christian view

Paul describes people knowing God yet suppressing truth, not merely lacking information.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 1:18–21" /> Jeremiah describes a deceitful heart whose self-assessment cannot simply be trusted.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Jeremiah 17:9" /> The Reformed account therefore locates the problem in the direction of desire as well as the contents of belief. Augustine describes loving truth when it illuminates what he wants, but hating it when it exposes him.<Cite source="augustine-confessions" locator="X.23.33–34" /> Recognition can survive while its unwelcome implications are evaded.

## What this explains well

This explains selective honesty: a person may reason accurately about another's fault while avoiding the same scrutiny of himself. Augustine recalls knowing his condition but placing himself out of his own sight.<Cite source="augustine-confessions" locator="VIII.7.16–18" /> The failure concerns what one permits knowledge to disclose, not the disappearance of every true belief.

## Strongest objection

Does this diagnosis make disagreement with Christianity evidence of dishonesty by definition? The psychological literature includes motivated errors which require no conscious intention to deceive.<Cite source="sep-self-deception" locator="§3.1" /> If every sincere objection is labeled suppression, the diagnosis risks insulating the Christian's own beliefs from examination.

## Christian reply

The doctrine is not a warrant to infer an opponent's hidden motives. Westminster applies deceitful hearts to believers too: their temptations can expose their dependence and humble them.<Cite source="wcf" locator="5.5" /> The Christian must distinguish a biblical account of humanity from a demonstrated diagnosis of a particular dispute. Arguments still require evidence; Christian confidence does not make its bearer immune to self-serving interpretation.

## Deep dive

#### Knowledge can be resisted without a conscious plot

The biblical account need not mean that someone simultaneously recites two contradictory beliefs in full awareness. Suppression can appear in what a person declines to examine or in how he interprets what he already recognizes. Owen’s treatment of indwelling sin describes deceit presenting things misleadingly, hiding consequences, and proceeding gradually.<Cite source="owen-works-goold-6" locator="Indwelling Sin VIII, pp. 211–215" /> This supplies a moral account of the direction of those processes without replacing their psychological explanation.

Calvin similarly treats self-love as an obstacle to accurate self-examination.<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="II.1.2; II.8.1" /> But invoking that obstacle does not establish that any specific argument is false. A Christian's flattering interpretation of his own conduct must face the same scrutiny he asks of others. Confessional assurance and personal infallibility are different claims.

The wider distinction between created capacities and their corruption belongs in <QuestionLink question="great-and-terrible" worldview="christianity">the flagship anthropology</QuestionLink>. <QuestionLink question="fail-the-good" worldview="christianity">Knowing good while doing wrong</QuestionLink> addresses contrary action; this question addresses the further effort to conceal or redescribe its meaning.
