## The Christian view

The Christian final end is more than avoiding death. The promised life includes fellowship with God. Paul connects the believer's destiny with conformity to the Son, while John joins future likeness to seeing him.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 8:29–30; 1 John 3:1–3" /> Salvation completes the creature's relation to God rather than replacing it with independence or divine identity.

The promised setting is renewed creation. Revelation joins God's dwelling with his people to the end of death, grief and pain; its final vision includes worship, the divine presence and continuing service.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Revelation 21:1–8; 22:1–5" /> The body is transformed in resurrection, and creation's release belongs to the same hope.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="1 Corinthians 15:42–49; Romans 8:18–23" /> Westminster's chief-end formula locates fulfillment in glorifying and enjoying God, not merely in indefinite survival.<Cite source="westminster-larger-catechism" locator="Q. 1" />

Glorification therefore includes holiness, fellowship and perfected creaturely life. Personal distinction remains; God is not absorbed into the world or the redeemed into God. Bavinck emphasizes that final communion includes the saints' own identities and active service.<Cite source="bavinck-reformed-dogmatics-monergism-4" locator="Vol. 4, §56.11, PDF pp. 641–643" /> The confessional account also includes exclusion and everlasting punishment. Its final state is not universal blessedness.<Cite source="wcf" locator="33.2" />

## What this explains well

This joins desires for truthful relationship, moral healing and embodied life rather than satisfying only the wish to continue. The final person is freed from sin, not simply supplied with unlimited opportunities to repeat present vices. The hope already calls for purification: John connects anticipated likeness with a changed response now.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="1 John 3:1–3" /> Future completion thus bears on present character.

## Strongest objection

The account promises a healed creation while excluding some people from blessedness forever.<Cite source="wcf" locator="33.2" /> How can that exclusion belong to the triumph of goodness, and how could the redeemed's joy be complete alongside others' punishment? The objection concerns the moral character of the proposed end, not a trivial complaint that heaven would lack entertainment.

## Christian reply

The Reformed reply makes communion depend on righteous reconciliation, not on declaring evil harmless. Revelation includes both the promised removal of grief and a judgment distinguishing outcomes.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Revelation 21:1–8" /> The doctrine appeals to God's justice and perfected holiness; it does not give a complete account of every future feeling. Its moral burden remains substantial and cannot be discharged simply by repeating that the saved are happy.

## Deep dive

#### Completion without absorption

The promise of likeness does not erase the Creator–creature distinction. God's children become like Christ while remaining persons who receive, know and love.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="1 John 3:1–3" /> The central good is fellowship in holiness, not the discovery that separate creatures never ultimately existed. This makes the contrast with nondual liberation precise, while also distinguishing the Christian account from ordinary immortality.

Bavinck resists conceiving blessed rest as inactivity. He appeals to worship, service and the continuing distinctness of the redeemed, while acknowledging Scripture's limited details about their activity.<Cite source="bavinck-reformed-dogmatics-monergism-4" locator="Vol. 4, §56.11, PDF pp. 641–643" /> That caution rules out invented schedules, occupations or descriptions of every relationship. It does not reduce final life to abstract consciousness without a body or community.

#### A hope with moral content

Revelation's new creation is not merely the old condition extended without a deadline. Death and suffering are removed; God's presence gives the life its center.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Revelation 21:1–4; 22:1–5" /> The resurrection body belongs to that completion, while the person's earthly weakness and corruption do not define its final qualities.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="1 Corinthians 15:42–49" />

The judgment difficulty should remain visible rather than being hidden behind attractive language about restoration. Westminster affirms everlasting punishment as well as joy.<Cite source="wcf" locator="33.2" /> A critic may regard this as a morally defective end; the Christian argues that holiness and justice cannot be completed through indifference to evil. That exchange concerns basic convictions about God, responsibility and judgment. No detailed psychological solution is asserted for the question of how the blessed understand every person's fate.
