## The Christian view

The Christian final end is more than avoiding death. The promised life is fellowship with God. Believers are destined to be conformed to the image of the Son, and when he appears they will be like him, because they will see him as he is.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 8:29–30; 1 John 3:1–3" /> Salvation completes the creature's relation to God rather than replacing it with independence or divine identity.

The promised setting is renewed creation. Revelation joins God's dwelling with his people to the end of death, grief and pain; its final vision includes worship, the divine presence and continuing service.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Revelation 21:1–8; 22:1–5" /> The body is transformed in resurrection, and creation's release belongs to the same hope.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="1 Corinthians 15:42–49; Romans 8:18–23" /> Man's chief end is to glorify God and fully to enjoy him forever: fulfillment lies there, not in indefinite survival.<Cite source="westminster-larger-catechism" locator="Q. 1" />

Glorification therefore includes holiness, fellowship and perfected creaturely life. Personal distinction remains; God is not absorbed into the world or the redeemed into God. Bavinck emphasizes that final communion includes the saints' own identities and active service.<Cite source="bavinck-reformed-dogmatics-monergism-4" locator="Vol. 4, §56.11, PDF pp. 641–643" /> The confessional account also includes exclusion and everlasting punishment. Its final state is not universal blessedness.<Cite source="wcf" locator="33.2" />

## What this explains well

This joins desires for truthful relationship, moral healing and embodied life rather than satisfying only the wish to continue. The final person is freed from sin, not simply supplied with unlimited opportunities to repeat present vices. The hope already calls for purification: everyone who hopes to be like Christ purifies himself now.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="1 John 3:1–3" /> Future completion thus bears on present character.

## Strongest objection

The account promises a healed creation while excluding some people from blessedness forever.<Cite source="wcf" locator="33.2" /> How can punishment without end be proportionate to sins committed in a finite life? How can that exclusion belong to the triumph of goodness, and how could the redeemed's joy be complete alongside others' punishment? The objection concerns the moral character of the proposed end, not a trivial complaint that heaven would lack entertainment.

## Christian reply

The Reformed reply begins from what the final good is: communion with a holy God in a creation healed of evil. Such a creation cannot include evil left unjudged. Revelation sets the end of grief beside a judgment that distinguishes outcomes,<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Revelation 21:1–8" /> and Jesus sets eternal punishment beside eternal life in the same sentence.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Matthew 25:31–46; 2 Thessalonians 1:6–10" /> The Westminster Confession presents the last day as manifesting both the glory of God's mercy in the salvation of the elect and the glory of his justice in the damnation of the reprobate, "who are wicked and disobedient."<Cite source="wcf" locator="33.2" />

Punishment, on this account, is not indifference to persons. It treats them as responsible agents who give account for what they have done in the body.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="2 Corinthians 5:10" /><Cite source="wcf" locator="33.1" /> On proportion, the Reformed answer is that every sin, as a transgression of God's righteous law, of its own nature binds the sinner over to God's wrath, "with all miseries spiritual, temporal, and eternal."<Cite source="wcf" locator="6.6" /> Edwards argues the point directly: a crime's heinousness is measured by the obligation it violates, not by the time it takes to commit, and sin violates the obligation owed to a God of infinite majesty and authority.<Cite source="edwards-works-hickman-1" locator="Justice of God in the Damnation of Sinners, Doctrine, I.1" /> That is the actual disagreement. A critic who denies that the dignity of the one wronged can make a finite act deserve endless punishment will reject the conclusion, and the reply does not demonstrate the premise to him. What it shows is that the doctrine follows from an account of justice and of God's majesty, not from indifference to persons.

Scripture pictures the redeemed praising God because his judgments are true and just,<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Revelation 19:1–2" /> so their joy is presented as agreement with judgment, not ignorance of it. Beyond that, Scripture says little about their future feelings, and the doctrine claims no more. It does not remove the weight of the objection.

## Deep dive

#### Completion without absorption

The promise of likeness does not erase the Creator–creature distinction. God's children become like Christ while remaining persons who receive, know and love.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="1 John 3:1–3" /> The central good is fellowship in holiness, not the discovery that separate creatures never ultimately existed. This makes the contrast with nondual liberation precise, while also distinguishing the Christian account from ordinary immortality.

Bavinck resists conceiving blessed rest as inactivity. He appeals to worship, service and the continuing distinctness of the redeemed, while acknowledging Scripture's limited details about their activity.<Cite source="bavinck-reformed-dogmatics-monergism-4" locator="Vol. 4, §56.11, PDF pp. 641–643" /> That caution rules out invented schedules, occupations or descriptions of every relationship. It does not reduce final life to abstract consciousness without a body or community.

#### A hope with moral content

Revelation's new creation is not merely the old condition extended without a deadline. Death and suffering are removed; God's presence gives the life its center.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Revelation 21:1–4; 22:1–5" /> The resurrection body belongs to that completion, while the person's earthly weakness and corruption do not define its final qualities.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="1 Corinthians 15:42–49" />

Attractive language about restoration does not hide the judgment difficulty. The Westminster Confession affirms everlasting punishment as well as joy.<Cite source="wcf" locator="33.2" /> A critic may regard this as a morally defective end; the Christian argues that holiness and justice cannot be completed through indifference to evil. That exchange concerns basic convictions about God, responsibility and judgment.
