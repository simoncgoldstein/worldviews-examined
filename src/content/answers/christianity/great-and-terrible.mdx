## The Christian view

Reformed theology explains the doubleness of human beings by one history of one nature: created good, fallen, preserved, and restored in Christ. God made humanity in his image, "endued with knowledge, righteousness, and true holiness."<Cite source="wcf" locator="4.2" /> Through Adam's sin the race became "wholly defiled in all the parts and faculties of soul and body."<Cite source="wcf" locator="6.2" /> Yet the faculties were not destroyed. Calvin, following Augustine, holds that the natural gifts were corrupted while the supernatural gifts were withdrawn: reason and will remain, damaged and misdirected.<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="II.2.12" /> So fallen people still discover truths, practise arts and frame just laws by gifts of God's Spirit, "the only fountain of truth,"<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="II.2.15" /> while his providence keeps corruption from breaking out to its full extent. Wickedness is not a second nature beside the good one. It is good powers ruled by a love of self "even to the contempt of God."<Cite source="augustine-city-of-god" locator="XIV.28" /> Because the disorder lies in what the will loves, the remedy must reach the will itself: renewal "after the image of its creator."<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Colossians 3:10" />

## What this explains well

The account holds both halves of the phenomenon at full strength. Human dignity rests on God's image rather than on performance, so no crime erases it.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Genesis 9:6" /> Wrongdoing can be knowing and willing, not only mistaken: some see the better course and follow the worse.<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="II.2.23" /> It explains why the worst evils feed on the best capacities, since the inventiveness that produced building, music and astronomy also devised poisons and "engines of destruction."<Cite source="augustine-city-of-god" locator="XXII.24" /> And it accounts for universality together with variation: a corruption common to all is restrained and gifted unequally.<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="II.3.4" />

## Strongest objection

The objection grants the darkness but denies that the Reformed mechanism explains it. Kant affirms a universal propensity to evil,<Cite source="kant-religion-greene-hudson" locator="Bk. I, §III" /> yet insists that a person "must make or have made himself" whatever he is morally, "for otherwise he could not be held responsible for it."<Cite source="kant-religion-greene-hudson" locator="Bk. I, General Observation" /> John Taylor pressed this against federal headship: to be "reputed guilty" for another's act, "all this before I am born," is "unreasonable."<Cite source="taylor-original-sin-supplement" locator="pp. 108–109" /> Hence a dilemma. If inherited corruption leaves us unable to do good, our cruelty is misfortune, not guilt. If we are truly guilty, the guilt must be our own, and Adam's does no work.

The Confession sharpens the difficulty. God ordains "whatsoever comes to pass"<Cite source="wcf" locator="3.1" /> and passes by the rest of mankind.<Cite source="wcf" locator="3.7" /> Is voluntary wrongdoing enough to ground blame when God ordains its occurrence and controls the only remedy? And unbelievers reason well, love sacrificially and die bravely. Either the capacity for good is intact and "inability" is false, or, once qualified by common grace, the doctrine predicts nothing observable.

## Christian reply

Edwards distinguishes natural inability, a lack of capacity, which "wholly excuses," from moral inability, the will's own contrary inclination, which "never can excuse," because commands address the will itself.<Cite source="edwards-works-hickman-1" locator="Freedom of the Will III.4" /> Ought implies can, then, for natural ability. Against Taylor he argues that if every sin had to flow from a prior sinful choice, the first such choice could not be sin.<Cite source="edwards-works-hickman-1" locator="Original Sin IV.1" />

To the decree, the Confession answers that God ordains all things without being "the author of sin" or offering "violence … to the will of the creatures."<Cite source="wcf" locator="3.1" /> That answers the charge that God sins or coerces. It does not settle whether responsibility is compatible with a comprehensive decree, which the Confession itself calls a "high mystery."<Cite source="wcf" locator="3.8" /> On imputation, the Reformed case rests on Paul's parallel between one man's disobedience and another's obedience,<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 5:18–19" /> an argument from revelation, not from neutral premises.

The empirical strand targets a narrower claim. The confessed inability concerns "spiritual good accompanying salvation."<Cite source="wcf" locator="9.3" /> The image remains, the faculties are corrupted rather than annihilated, and God distributes "special gifts" to the unregenerate,<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="II.3.4" /> so civil virtue is expected. The doctrine's claim concerns the will's settled direction toward God, not the frequency of crimes.<Cite source="edwards-works-hickman-1" locator="Original Sin I.1.1" /> How far that can be tested remains disputed.

## Deep dive

#### Total in extent, not maximal

The Confession states the corruption without softening: "From this original corruption, whereby we are utterly indisposed, disabled, and made opposite to all good, and wholly inclined to all evil, do proceed all actual transgressions."<Cite source="wcf" locator="6.4" /> The Standards gloss the sentence themselves. The Larger Catechism specifies opposition "unto all that is spiritually good,"<Cite source="westminster-larger-catechism" locator="Q. 25" /> and the Confession allows that works of unregenerate people may be things God commands and "of good use both to themselves and others," while judging them sinful because they do not proceed from faith or aim at God's glory.<Cite source="wcf" locator="16.7" /> The doctrine is total in extent, reaching every faculty and every act in its relation to God. It does not claim that every act is as bad as it could be. Calvin accordingly calls the fallen image a ruin, "not utterly effaced and destroyed."<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="I.15.4" />

#### Knowing the better and doing the worse

For Calvin the law written on the heart is "the judgment of conscience," and he rejects the view that wrongdoing comes only from ignorance.<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="II.2.22" /> Often the general rule is held while the particular case is misjudged, but sometimes the sinner "rushes into sin knowingly and willingly."<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="II.2.23" /> Augustine stole pears he did not want: "It was foul, and I loved it."<Cite source="augustine-confessions" locator="II.4.9" /> Later he found that "the mind commands itself, and is resisted," because "it wills not entirely."<Cite source="augustine-confessions" locator="VIII.9.21" /> Behind both stands Paul's account of people who "knew God" yet "suppress the truth."<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 1:18–21" /> On this view the greatest gifts become instruments of sin because the will has made itself, in Augustine's phrase, "a kind of end to itself."<Cite source="augustine-city-of-god" locator="XIV.13" />

#### Restraint and the gifts of unbelievers

Calvin admires unbelievers' law, philosophy, medicine and mathematics, warning that "in despising the gifts, we insult the Giver."<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="II.2.15" /> God checks corruption through shame, fear of the laws and self-interest, "yet without rendering it inwardly pure."<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="II.3.3" /> Edwards adds a moral psychology: natural pity, gratitude and conscience restrain vice and serve human good, yet are not true virtue, which is love to God.<Cite source="edwards-works-hickman-1" locator="True Virtue ch. 7" /> This is what the reply means by civil good that falls short of spiritual good.

#### Where Reformed theologians differ

The Confession explains universality by Adam's place as "the root of all mankind," whose guilt was imputed and whose corrupted nature was conveyed to his descendants.<Cite source="wcf" locator="6.3" /> That is the confessional position. The historicity of Adam is debated among contemporary Reformed scholars, and seventeenth-century theologians already disputed whether guilt is imputed immediately or only through inherited corruption. Edwards's own account of how Adam and his descendants are one, resting on God's constitution of all created identity, is distinctive and contested; the confessional doctrine does not depend on it. On common grace, the Christian Reformed synod of 1924 affirmed that God, "without renewing the heart," restrains sin and enables civil good, against the objections of Danhof and Hoeksema.<Cite source="crc-acts-synod-1924" locator="Art. 132, pp. 145–147" /> These debates affect how universality and restraint are grounded, not the structure of image, fall, restraint and renewal.
