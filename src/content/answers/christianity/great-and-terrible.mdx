## The Christian view

The Bible explains human greatness and human wickedness by one history of one nature: created good, fallen, preserved, and restored in Christ.

The story begins with creation, not with sin. God formed the man from the dust and gave him breath and life,<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Genesis 2:7" /> so human beings are creatures, embodied and dependent. Yet God made them "in the image of God," male and female, blessed them and gave them the earth to fill and to rule.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Genesis 1:26–28" /> The psalmist marvels that so small a creature is crowned with glory and set over the works of God's hands.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Psalm 8:3–8" /> Human greatness is not an achievement but a gift: creatures made to know God, reflect his goodness and represent his rule, created, as the Westminster Confession says, in knowledge, righteousness and true holiness.<Cite source="wcf" locator="4.2" />

Misery enters with a human act. The first man and woman distrusted God's word and reached for a wisdom of their own; fear, blame, pain and death followed.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Genesis 3" /> Paul reads that act as the turning point of human history, and Adam as more than a private individual: "sin came into the world through one man, and death through sin," and death spread to all because all sinned.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 5:12" /> He sets Adam's one trespass, which brought condemnation, beside Christ's one act of obedience, which brings righteousness and life.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 5:15–19" /> So the corruption belongs to fallen humanity, not to human nature as God created it.

The corruption reaches the center of the person. "The heart is deceitful above all things."<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Jeremiah 17:9" /> Paul gathers the psalms and prophets into a single indictment: no one seeks God, and the mind's turning away shows itself in deceitful speech and quick violence.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 3:10–18" /> Apart from Christ, he says, people are dead in trespasses, following their own desires, and by nature under wrath.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Ephesians 2:1–3" /> The whole person is turned from God: in the Westminster Confession's words, "wholly defiled in all the parts and faculties of soul and body," a corruption passed from our first parents to all their descendants.<Cite source="wcf" locator="6.2–3" /> Yet wickedness is not a second nature beside the good one. It is good powers ruled by a disordered love, a love of self "even to the contempt of God," as Augustine puts it.<Cite source="augustine-city-of-god" locator="XIV.28" />

The Fall did not unmake the creature. Murder and cursing are still forbidden because every person bears God's image.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Genesis 9:6; James 3:9" /> God sends sun and rain on the just and the unjust, and has not left the nations without witness to his kindness.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Matthew 5:45; Acts 14:17" /> As Calvin explains, the natural gifts were corrupted while the supernatural gifts, faith, love of God and holiness, were withdrawn; reason and will remain, damaged and misdirected.<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="II.2.12" /> So fallen people still discover truths, practice the arts and frame just laws by gifts of God's Spirit, "the only fountain of truth,"<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="II.2.15" /> while his providence restrains corruption.<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="II.3.3" /> Scripture teaches the kindness; Reformed theology gathers it, with the restraint and the gifts, into the doctrine of common grace: real goodness that preserves and gifts human life without reconciling the heart to God.

Because the disorder lies in what the will loves, the remedy must reach the will itself: renewal "after the image of its creator."<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Colossians 3:10" /> That is the work of Christ, the second Adam (see <QuestionLink question="guilt" worldview="christianity">guilt and atonement</QuestionLink> and <QuestionLink question="self-salvation" worldview="christianity">salvation</QuestionLink>).

## What this explains well

The account holds both halves of the phenomenon at full strength. Dignity rests on God's image rather than on performance, so no crime erases it. Wrongdoing can be knowing and willing, not only mistaken: some see the better course and follow the worse.<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="II.2.23" /> It explains why the worst evils feed on the best capacities, since the inventiveness that produced building, music and astronomy also devised poisons and "engines of destruction."<Cite source="augustine-city-of-god" locator="XXII.24" /> And it accounts for universality together with variation: a corruption common to all is restrained and gifted unequally.<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="II.3.4" />

## Strongest objection

The objection grants the darkness but denies that the Reformed mechanism explains it. Kant affirms a universal propensity to evil,<Cite source="kant-religion-greene-hudson" locator="Bk. I, §III" /> yet insists that a person "must make or have made himself" whatever he is morally, "for otherwise he could not be held responsible for it."<Cite source="kant-religion-greene-hudson" locator="Bk. I, General Observation" /> John Taylor pressed this against federal headship: to be "reputed guilty" for another's act, "all this before I am born," is "unreasonable."<Cite source="taylor-original-sin-supplement" locator="pp. 108–109" /> Hence a dilemma. If inherited corruption leaves us unable to do good, our cruelty is misfortune, not guilt. If we are truly guilty, the guilt must be our own, and Adam's does no work.

The Westminster Confession sharpens the difficulty. God ordains "whatsoever comes to pass"<Cite source="wcf" locator="3.1" /> and passes by the rest of mankind.<Cite source="wcf" locator="3.7" /> Is voluntary wrongdoing enough to ground blame when God ordains its occurrence and controls the only remedy? And unbelievers reason well, love sacrificially and die bravely. Either the capacity for good is intact and "inability" is false, or, once qualified by common grace, the doctrine predicts nothing observable.

## Christian reply

Edwards distinguishes natural inability, a lack of capacity, which "wholly excuses," from moral inability, the will's own contrary inclination, which "never can excuse," because commands address the will itself.<Cite source="edwards-works-hickman-1" locator="Freedom of the Will III.4" /> Ought implies can, then, for natural ability. Against Taylor he argues that if every sin had to flow from a prior sinful choice, the first such choice could not be sin.<Cite source="edwards-works-hickman-1" locator="Original Sin IV.1" />

To the decree, the Westminster Confession answers that God ordains all things without being "the author of sin" or offering "violence … to the will of the creatures," and that the liberty of second causes is not "taken away, but rather established."<Cite source="wcf" locator="3.1" /> That answers the charge that God sins or coerces, and it affirms that the decree and real responsibility stand together. How they cohere the Confession does not claim to explain; it treats predestination as a "high mystery" to be handled with special care.<Cite source="wcf" locator="3.8" /> On imputation, the Reformed case rests on Paul's parallel between one man's disobedience and another's obedience,<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 5:18–19" /> an argument from revelation, not from neutral premises. The same parallel carries the Christian account of how Christ's obedience counts for his people, so the two stand or fall together.

The empirical strand targets a narrower claim. The confessed inability concerns "spiritual good accompanying salvation."<Cite source="wcf" locator="9.3" /> The image remains, the faculties are corrupted rather than annihilated, and God distributes "special gifts" to the unregenerate,<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="II.3.4" /> so civil virtue is expected. The doctrine's claim concerns the will's settled direction toward God, not the frequency of crimes.<Cite source="edwards-works-hickman-1" locator="Original Sin I.1.1" /> How far that can be tested remains disputed.

## Deep dive

#### Total in extent, not maximal

The Westminster Confession states the corruption without softening: "From this original corruption, whereby we are utterly indisposed, disabled, and made opposite to all good, and wholly inclined to all evil, do proceed all actual transgressions."<Cite source="wcf" locator="6.4" /> The Standards gloss the sentence themselves. The Larger Catechism specifies opposition "unto all that is spiritually good,"<Cite source="westminster-larger-catechism" locator="Q. 25" /> and the Confession allows that works of unregenerate people may be things God commands and "of good use both to themselves and others," while judging them sinful because they do not proceed from faith or aim at God's glory.<Cite source="wcf" locator="16.7" /> The doctrine is total in extent, reaching every faculty and every act in its relation to God. It does not claim that every act is as bad as it could be. Calvin accordingly calls the fallen image a ruin, "not utterly effaced and destroyed."<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="I.15.4" />

#### Knowing the better and doing the worse

Wrongdoing does not come only from ignorance.<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="II.2.22–23" /> Augustine stole pears he did not want: "It was foul, and I loved it."<Cite source="augustine-confessions" locator="II.4.9" /> Later he found that "the mind commands itself, and is resisted," because "it wills not entirely."<Cite source="augustine-confessions" locator="VIII.9.21" /> Behind such experience stands Paul's account of people who "knew God" yet "suppress the truth."<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 1:18–21" /> On this view the greatest gifts become instruments of sin because the will has made itself, in Augustine's phrase, "a kind of end to itself."<Cite source="augustine-city-of-god" locator="XIV.13" />

#### Restraint and the gifts of unbelievers

Calvin warns that in despising unbelievers' gifts "we insult the Giver," and says God checks corruption through shame, fear of the laws and self-interest, "yet without rendering it inwardly pure."<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="II.2.15; II.3.3" /> Edwards adds a moral psychology: natural pity, gratitude and conscience restrain vice and serve human good, yet are not true virtue, which is love to God.<Cite source="edwards-works-hickman-1" locator="True Virtue ch. 7" />

#### Adam, the first covenant head

Scripture presents Adam as the first man, formed from the dust and placed under God's command, with life and death set before him.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Genesis 2:7, 15–17" /> God made every nation from that one man.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Acts 17:26" /> Paul's argument in Romans 5 turns on one man's trespass set against one man's obedience,<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 5:12–19" /> and he writes that "as in Adam all die, so also in Christ shall all be made alive."<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="1 Corinthians 15:21–22" /> Christ is the last Adam because Adam was the first.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="1 Corinthians 15:45" />

The Westminster Standards follow that reading. The first covenant was a covenant of works, in which life was promised to Adam, "and in him to his posterity," on condition of perfect obedience.<Cite source="wcf" locator="7.2" /> The Larger Catechism explains that the covenant was made "with Adam as a public person, not for himself only, but for his posterity," so that all mankind descending from him "sinned in him, and fell with him in that first transgression."<Cite source="westminster-larger-catechism" locator="Q. 22" /> Because our first parents were "the root of all mankind," two things pass to their descendants: the guilt of that first sin, imputed, and a corrupted nature, conveyed by ordinary generation.<Cite source="wcf" locator="6.3" /> The doctrine therefore presupposes a real first man who acted as covenant head, whose transgression stands in representative relation to his descendants as Christ's obedience stands to his people (see <QuestionLink question="guilt" worldview="christianity">guilt and atonement</QuestionLink>).

#### Where Reformed theologians differ

Reformed theologians have differed in explaining parts of this doctrine. Josué de la Place of Saumur taught that Adam's guilt reaches his descendants only through their inherited corruption; Turretin, with most Reformed theologians, held that it is imputed immediately, prior to that corruption, and treated the alternative as a departure from the received doctrine.<Cite source="turretin-institutio-1847" locator="IX.9.4, 15 (vol. 1, pp. 555–558)" note="Latin; paraphrased" /> Edwards's own account of how Adam and his descendants are one, resting on God's constitution of all created identity, is distinctive and contested; the confessional doctrine does not depend on it. On common grace, the Christian Reformed synod of 1924 affirmed that God, "without renewing the heart," restrains sin and enables civil good, against the objections of Danhof and Hoeksema.<Cite source="crc-acts-synod-1924" locator="Art. 132, pp. 145–147" /> These debates affect how universality and restraint are grounded, not the structure of image, fall, restraint and renewal.
