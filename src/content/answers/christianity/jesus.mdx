## The Christian view

The Christian claim is that in Jesus God himself came among us: "the Word became flesh and dwelt among us."<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="John 1:14" /> In him "the whole fullness of deity dwells bodily."<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Colossians 2:9" /> He is the Messiah promised to Israel, who came "to give his life as a ransom for many"<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Mark 10:45" /> (on how his death deals with guilt, see <QuestionLink question="guilt" worldview="christianity">guilt</QuestionLink>). He was raised and "declared to be the Son of God in power... by his resurrection from the dead."<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 1:3–4" /> Westminster summarizes: two whole natures, divine and human, "inseparably joined together in one person, without conversion, composition, or confusion."<Cite source="wcf" locator="8.2" />

If this is true, God has acted decisively in history: Jesus is vindicated as "both Lord and Christ,"<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Acts 2:36" /> and his resurrection is "the firstfruits" of the defeat of death.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="1 Corinthians 15:20" />

The claim is staked on history, so it must be weighed as history. As the critical historian Dale Martin puts it, "reputable historical scholars all admit that Jesus of Nazareth existed."<Cite source="martin-yale-new-testament" locator="L13 ch. 2" /> He was crucified under Pontius Pilate, as Paul, the Gospels and the Roman historian Tacitus attest.<Cite source="tacitus-annals-church-brodribb" locator="15.44" /> Within a few years of his death his followers were proclaiming that God had raised him. Paul passes on a tradition he had "received": Christ died, was buried, was raised and appeared to Cephas, the Twelve, more than five hundred, James and finally Paul himself.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="1 Corinthians 15:3–8" /> Several of his followers, and Paul, had experiences they understood as appearances of the risen Jesus. Paul had met Cephas and "James the Lord's brother" in Jerusalem.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Galatians 1:18–19" /> Within about two decades Jewish followers were praying to Jesus and calling him Lord alongside the one God.<Cite source="hurtado-worship-of-christ" locator="Early devotion to Christ" />

The Christian argument is that bodily resurrection best explains these facts together: early proclamation, varied appearance experiences, Paul's conversion, James's leadership, early worship and, on the stronger form of the case, an empty tomb.<Cite source="craig-empty-tomb-nts" locator="summary" />

## What this explains well

The claim accounts for the origin of a movement that proclaimed a crucified man as risen Lord, and joins that history to the biblical story of creation, sin and redemption.

## Strongest objection

The strongest objection grants the facts: Jesus was crucified, and his followers sincerely believed he had appeared to them. But visions of the dead are common, and grieving followers who believed God must vindicate his Messiah could have had them. Bart Ehrman suggests that belief in Jesus' exaltation arose from Scripture, that visions followed, and that stories of visions grew into the Gospels' narratives "30, 40, 50, 60 years later."<Cite source="craig-ehrman-debate-2006" locator="Ehrman, conclusion" /> Whether his tomb was found empty is genuinely debated. Above all, a miracle is "the least probable occurrence," so a historian can never conclude that one probably happened.<Cite source="craig-ehrman-debate-2006" locator="Ehrman, opening statement" />

## Christian reply

First, a natural explanation must account for all the facts at once. Grief visions explain individuals; they fit less easily with appearances reported to groups, to Paul, who was an enemy, and to James, whose family, the Gospels report, had not followed Jesus in his lifetime.<Cite source="ej-jesus" locator="The Name, Birth, and Death Date" /> Jews expected resurrection for all the righteous together at the end, so the claim that one man had already been raised needs explaining. And Jews who prayed to the one God began within two decades to pray to Jesus as well, which Jewish traditions about God's exalted agents do not by themselves explain.<Cite source="hurtado-worship-of-christ" locator="Historical factors (ii)" />

Second, the prior. Ehrman's argument shows that a historian, as historian, will not affirm a miracle, not that none occurred. If God exists and has reason to reveal himself, a resurrection is not intrinsically absurd, and the question becomes which explanation best fits the evidence. Background beliefs matter on both sides.<Cite source="sep-miracles" locator="§4.2" /> Nor does history alone produce the Christian confession. It can show that the resurrection is a serious explanation; that Jesus is the eternal Son and Mediator is taught by the Scripture the resurrection vindicates. Bavinck warns that historical argument by itself yields only a human, revisable conviction.<Cite source="bavinck-reformed-dogmatics-monergism-1" locator="Vol. 1, §14, PDF p. 537" />

## Deep dive

#### The sources and their dates

Paul's letters are the earliest Christian writings; 1 Thessalonians dates from about 50, and Mark, the earliest Gospel, from about 70, some forty years after the crucifixion.<Cite source="martin-yale-new-testament" locator="L2 ch. 1" /> The tradition in 1 Corinthians 15 is older than the letter, because Paul says he received it. William Lane Craig argues that he probably received it in Damascus or on his first Jerusalem visit, and that it reaches back "within the first five years" after the crucifixion.<Cite source="craig-empty-tomb-nts" locator="argument (1)" /> The dating is an inference from Paul's chronology, and the formula is a summary, not an eyewitness transcript. Galatians and Acts are also hard to harmonize on Paul's movements.<Cite source="martin-yale-new-testament" locator="L5 ch. 2" />

Outside the New Testament, the Roman historian Tacitus, writing in the early second century, records that "Christus... suffered the extreme penalty during the reign of Tiberius at the hands of one of our procurators, Pontius Pilatus."<Cite source="tacitus-annals-church-brodribb" locator="15.44" /> He confirms the execution and the movement's spread, not the resurrection. Josephus' famous passage about Jesus (*Antiquities* 18) has been reworked by Christian hands; scholars debate whether it was altered or wholly interpolated.<Cite source="posen-testimonium-flavianum" locator="headnote" /> His brief notice of the execution of "the brother of Jesus, who was called Christ, whose name was James" (*Antiquities* 20) is a separate matter; the Encyclopaedia Judaica treats it as Josephus' own.<Cite source="josephus-antiquities-whiston" locator="XX.9.1" /><Cite source="ej-jesus" locator="opening" />

#### The empty tomb

Paul's tradition says he "was buried";<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="1 Corinthians 15:4" /> the details of the burial are debated. Craig offers eight arguments that the tomb was found empty, among them that women are named as the discoverers, an unlikely invention, and that the resurrection could not have been preached in Jerusalem with the body available.<Cite source="craig-empty-tomb-nts" locator="summary" /> The sceptic Jeffery Jay Lowder replies that none of these shows Mark's story to be historical, and offers an alternative in which the body was moved to a criminals' burial ground; yet he puts the odds that the tomb was empty at "just slightly better than 50%."<Cite source="lowder-empty-tomb-jhc" locator="n. 3; §2" /> Gary Habermas, a Christian scholar surveying the literature, reports that acceptance of the empty tomb is "far from being unanimously held" but held by "a fairly strong majority."<Cite source="habermas-resurrection-research" locator="Some Specific Research Trends" /> The question is genuinely debated, and the Christian case does not rest on it alone.

#### Where the belief came from

Ehrman's reconstruction runs in a particular order. No Jew expected a crucified Messiah; Jesus' followers searched Scripture for the righteous sufferer whom God vindicates (Isaiah 53, Psalm 22), concluded that God had exalted Jesus to heaven, as Elijah and Enoch had been, and then began to speak of resurrection and to have visions.<Cite source="craig-ehrman-debate-2006" locator="Ehrman, conclusion" /> Hurtado, a historian sympathetic to the Christian case, agrees that Jewish categories supplied the frame, but argues that powerful experiences of the risen Jesus supplied the pressure: he was "the one man singled out for eschatological glorification."<Cite source="hurtado-worship-of-christ" locator="Historical factors (ii)" /> That the belief was unusual raises the demand for an explanation. It does not by itself settle which explanation is right.

#### Early worship

Paul's "one God, the Father... and one Lord, Jesus Christ" places Jesus within the Jewish confession of the one God,<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="1 Corinthians 8:6" /> and the Aramaic prayer *marana tha*, "Our Lord, come!", preserved untranslated in a Greek letter, points to the earliest Aramaic-speaking communities.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="1 Corinthians 16:22" /> Larry Hurtado concludes that this devotion "begins within the first two decades of the church."<Cite source="hurtado-worship-of-christ" locator="Early devotion to Christ (iii)" /> It shows how early Jesus was given a place beside God. It does not show that the later creeds' formulations were already in place.

#### What history cannot settle

Calvin saw why the doctrine matters: "The case was certainly desperate, if the Godhead itself did not descend to us, it being impossible for us to ascend."<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="II.12.1" /> The critical historian Dale Martin puts the limit plainly: there is no way that history "by the normal historiographical tools" could tell whether God was in Jesus Christ.<Cite source="martin-yale-new-testament" locator="L13 ch. 2" /> Calvin agrees from the other side. Without the resurrection, he says, everything else "would be defective,"<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="II.16.13" /> but faith in it rests finally on the Spirit's persuasion, not on argument alone (see <QuestionLink question="revelation" worldview="christianity">revelation</QuestionLink>). Turretin argued against Jewish interpreters that the prophecies of Genesis 49:10 and Daniel 9 required the Messiah to come before the fall of the Second Temple.<Cite source="turretin-institutio-1847" locator="XIII.1" note="Latin; paraphrased" /> Jewish interpreters read those texts differently, and that dispute is taken up in <QuestionLink question="jesus" worldview="judaism">the Jewish answer</QuestionLink>.
