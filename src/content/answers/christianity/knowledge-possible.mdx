## The Christian view

Reformed Christianity answers that the knower and the known come from the same Maker. God founded the earth by wisdom, so it has an order that can be understood.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Proverbs 3:19; Psalm 104:24" /> He also made the faculties that understand it: "The hearing ear and the seeing eye, the LORD has made them both."<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Proverbs 20:12" /> Human beings bear his image, creatures fitted for their world (see <QuestionLink question="what-is-man" worldview="christianity">what man is</QuestionLink>).<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Genesis 1:26–27" /> Bavinck puts the point simply: the same divine Word created both the reality outside us and the laws of thought within us, so the two correspond.<Cite source="bavinck-reformed-dogmatics-monergism-1" locator="Vol. 1, §7, PDF p. 217" />

Our knowledge is real but creaturely. God knows all things completely and depends on nothing he has made for his knowledge.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Psalm 147:5" /><Cite source="wcf" locator="2.2" /> We know truly, but in part: "Now I know in part."<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="1 Corinthians 13:12" /> Human knowledge is neither a copy of God's knowledge nor a rival to it. It is a finite creature's grasp of a world that God already understands fully.

Sin does damage, but it does not switch the mind off. Paul says that people "suppress the truth" about God that is plain to them: the trouble is refusal more than incapacity.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 1:18–21" /> Calvin calls fallen reason a "shapeless ruin," yet as a natural gift it "could not be entirely destroyed," and he traces pagan achievement in law, philosophy, medicine and mathematics to God's Spirit, "the only fountain of truth."<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="II.2.12–16" /> So the Christian view does not say that unbelievers know nothing. The distortion falls hardest on the knowledge of God and of ourselves, where <QuestionLink question="self-deception" worldview="christianity">self-deception</QuestionLink> has most to gain.

There God adds his word. Calvin compares Scripture to glasses: it gathers our confused impressions of God and lets us read clearly.<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="I.6.1" /> "In your light do we see light."<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Psalm 36:9" />

## What this explains well

It explains why the world is intelligible and why our minds fit it, so that ordinary perception can be trusted and mathematics worked out on paper describes the planets. It also explains both sides of human knowing: our knowledge is reliable yet fallible, and brilliant minds can still deceive themselves.

## Strongest objection

The naturalist answers that no God is needed. Perception, memory and inference are evolved capacities. Quine observed that creatures "inveterately wrong in their inductions" tend to die out before reproducing, so selection favours reliable minds, and science then corrects them in public.<Cite source="sep-epistemology-naturalized" locator="§2" note="Quine 1969c, p. 126, as quoted by SEP" /> The theist's appeal also looks circular. The Christian uses his faculties to learn about the God who is supposed to guarantee those faculties.

## Christian reply

The Christian need not deny the natural story, since God ordinarily works through means. The disagreement is over what the story explains. Selection rewards behaviour that helps survival. Whether that makes our minds reliable about galaxies, mathematics and moral truth, where survival is not at stake, is contested, and <QuestionLink question="knowledge-possible" worldview="naturalism">naturalists have serious replies</QuestionLink>.

On circularity, every account of knowledge has to use the faculties it describes; no one can step outside their own mind to check it. Bavinck therefore starts from the ordinary certainty that we do know things, and asks which account of reality makes that certainty intelligible.<Cite source="bavinck-reformed-dogmatics-monergism-1" locator="Vol. 1, §7, PDF p. 204" /> A mind made by a knowing God, for a world made by the same God, is the kind of mind we should expect to know. That is a claim of comparative explanatory adequacy, not a proof that leaves no room for reply.

## Deep dive

#### God's knowledge and ours

Reformed theology distinguishes God's own knowledge, called *archetypal*, from the knowledge he communicates to creatures, called *ectypal*.<Cite source="turretin-institutio-1847" locator="I.2.6" note="Latin; paraphrased" /> Bavinck calls our knowledge of God an imprint of God's self-knowledge, given in a creaturely mode. He warns against reading the difference as merely a matter of quantity, as if ours were a smaller slice of the same thing.<Cite source="bavinck-reformed-dogmatics-monergism-1" locator="Vol. 1, §6.2, PDF pp. 186–188" /> The Westminster Confession calls God's knowledge "infinite, infallible, and independent upon the creature."<Cite source="wcf" locator="2.2" />

#### Illumination and realism

The tradition explains the mechanism in more than one way. Augustine found a certainty that sceptical doubt cannot touch: "if I am deceived, I am."<Cite source="augustine-city-of-god" locator="XI.26" /> He held that the mind sees intelligible truths "by a sort of incorporeal light of an unique kind," as the Creator arranged, much as the eye sees bodies in bodily light.<Cite source="augustine-trinity" locator="XII.15.24" /> Bavinck takes a more Aristotelian route, which he openly borrows in part from Aquinas. The mind has no innate ideas; the senses awaken the intellect, and the laws of thought come with its nature.<Cite source="bavinck-reformed-dogmatics-monergism-1" locator="Vol. 1, §7, PDF p. 208" /> God, he says, makes the eye as well as the light.<Cite source="bavinck-reformed-dogmatics-monergism-1" locator="Vol. 1, §8, PDF p. 271" /> The two accounts differ on mechanism, not on dependence: in both, the faculties are real and work because God made them and the world for each other.

#### Van Til's restatement

In the twentieth century Van Til restated the doctrine for apologetics. Human knowledge, he wrote, is "analogical of divine knowledge": God's is original, ours derivative, true but never comprehensive.<Cite source="van-til-defense" locator="pp. 56–61" /> Sin is "an ethical and not a metaphysical question," because it does not cancel our dependence on God.<Cite source="van-til-defense" locator="p. 57" /> Asked whether he meant that non-Christians do not discover truth, he answered, "we mean nothing so absurd as that." His claim was that they cannot use their own methods consistently.<Cite source="van-til-defense" locator="p. 120" /> That stronger apologetic claim needs its own argument, and it is taken up under <QuestionLink question="logic-binding" worldview="christianity">logic</QuestionLink>. The doctrine of created, finite knowledge is older and broader than it.

#### Where the confessions place this

The Westminster Confession says that the light of nature and the works of creation and providence show God's goodness, wisdom and power so far as to leave people without excuse, but not enough for saving knowledge.<Cite source="wcf" locator="1.1" /> The Belgic Confession speaks of two books, creation and Scripture.<Cite source="belgic-confession" locator="Art. 2" /> Both summarize the biblical account rather than adding to it.
