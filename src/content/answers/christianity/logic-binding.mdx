## The Christian view

Christians regard the laws of logic as discovered, not invented: they are neither human conventions nor accidents of evolution. Augustine put it plainly: "the validity of logical sequences is not a thing devised by men, but is observed and noted by them... for it exists eternally in the reason of things, and has its origin with God."<Cite source="augustine-christian-doctrine" locator="II.32.50" />

Scripture offers no theory of logic, but it everywhere relies on it. God's word is true because it is "impossible for God to lie."<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Hebrews 6:18" /> Its writers reason from premises to conclusions and expect their readers to feel the force. Paul tells the Corinthians that if there is no resurrection of the dead, "then not even Christ has been raised."<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="1 Corinthians 15:13" /> And if Christ has not been raised, the apostles' preaching and the Corinthians' faith are in vain, and the apostles have misrepresented God.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="1 Corinthians 15:14–19" /> Behind such reasoning stands a doctrine. All things were made through the Word,<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="John 1:1–3" /> and God made human minds to understand the world he made.

Reformed theology draws from this a conclusion Scripture implies but does not spell out: rational order is grounded in God's eternal wisdom. It is not an arbitrary decree of God's will, as though God might have made contradictions true, nor an independent order standing above him. God implanted the rules of sound reasoning in rational creatures,<Cite source="turretin-institutio-1847" locator="I.10.1–3" note="Latin; paraphrased" /> and the one Logos made both reality and the laws of our thinking, so that the two correspond.<Cite source="bavinck-reformed-dogmatics-monergism-1" locator="Vol. 1, §7, PDF p. 217" />

## What this explains well

The account explains why logic is necessary and universal, why it applies to the world and not only to our heads, and why finite minds can grasp it. It also explains why the Westminster Confession can accept what follows from Scripture "by good and necessary consequence."<Cite source="wcf" locator="1.6" />

## Strongest objection

The objection takes the form of a dilemma. If logic depends on God's will, God could have made contradictions true, and logic would be arbitrary. If God's thinking conforms to logic, logic is a standard independent of God, and God adds nothing to explain it. Naturalists add that logic may be grounded in the structure of the world or of our own cognition, or may simply be self-evident and basic, with no need for God.<Cite source="sep-logical-truth" locator="§1.1" /><Cite source="russell-problems-philosophy" locator="ch. VII" />

## Christian reply

The Reformed answer rejects both horns. Logic is not an arbitrary decree: the laws of thought are eternal truths,<Cite source="bavinck-reformed-dogmatics-monergism-1" locator="Vol. 1, §7, PDF p. 208" /> and Augustine treats valid inference as eternal and as originating with God, not as something devised. Nor is logic a standard set over God, since God is "the alone fountain of all being"<Cite source="wcf" locator="2.2" /> and nothing stands outside him to measure him. Logic's necessity therefore lies in God's own eternal wisdom, the Word through whom all things were made: neither above God nor produced by his will. Critics reply that it only moves the dilemma back a step; the Christian answers that every account must stop at something necessary, and this one stops at a wise God rather than at an unexplained law. Turretin adds a useful distinction: mysteries such as the Trinity are *incomprehensible*, beyond our grasp, but not *incompossible*, self-contradictory.<Cite source="turretin-institutio-1847" locator="I.9.9" note="Latin; paraphrased" />

Against the naturalist the claim is comparative. A basic necessity that binds every mind and also governs the physical world is left unexplained; the Christian account explains why mind, world and logic fit together. That is a claim of better explanation, not a proof that no alternative is coherent.

## Deep dive

#### Reason after the Fall

The principles of natural reason, such as that the whole is greater than its part, survive the Fall, and faith uses them rather than overthrowing them.<Cite source="turretin-institutio-1847" locator="I.9.5" note="Latin; paraphrased" /> Bavinck adds that once the senses rouse the intellect, it grasps the laws of thought at once and of its own nature, before any proof, and that even the simplest person applies them without reflection.<Cite source="bavinck-reformed-dogmatics-monergism-1" locator="Vol. 1, §7, PDF p. 208" /> Sin distorts what people conclude about God, but it does not abolish the forms of valid reasoning.

#### One logic, two accounts of it

Van Til insisted that believers and unbelievers reason by the same formal logic: "I do not maintain that Christians operate according to new laws of thought any more than that they have new eyes or noses." Their disagreement is about logic's foundation and about how it is used.<Cite source="van-til-defense" locator="p. 296" /> He quotes his own earlier syllabus: "the law of contradiction, to operate at all, must have its foundation in God."<Cite source="van-til-defense" locator="p. 297" note="quoting Van Til's Introduction to Theology syllabus" /> Without creation and providence, he wrote, logic connects with facts no better than "a revolving door in a void."<Cite source="van-til-defense" locator="p. 265" />

#### Three claims, not one

It helps to separate three claims.
- **Biblical and Reformed doctrine:** God made the ordered world and rational creatures. Scripture teaches this directly.
- **Theological development:** human reasoning is dependent and creaturely, and rational order has its source in God's eternal wisdom. Augustine, Turretin and Bavinck develop this from Scripture's teaching about God and creation.
- **Van Til's apologetic claim:** only the Christian view can account for the preconditions of logic, so that on a non-Christian view even "the laws of logic which he employs are products of chance."<Cite source="van-til-defense" locator="p. 119" />

The third claim needs its own argument. A naturalist realist such as Russell does not think logic is a product of chance (see <QuestionLink question="logic-binding" worldview="naturalism">the naturalist answer</QuestionLink>). Van Til's point is better put as a challenge: can a view that makes matter or chance ultimate explain why logic is necessary and why it applies to the world? That challenge is serious, but it is a comparative argument, not a slogan that settles the matter.

#### Learning logic outside the church

Augustine thought valid forms of reasoning could be learned perfectly well "outside the pale of the Church," although the truth of the premises about God must be sought in Scripture.<Cite source="augustine-christian-doctrine" locator="II.31.49" /> The Christian account of logic therefore does not claim that only Christians reason well. It claims to explain why anyone can.
