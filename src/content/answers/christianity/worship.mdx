## The Christian view

Scripture treats worship as the creature's fitting response to God and idolatry as its corruption. The first commandments forbid other gods and images made for worship.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Exodus 20:3–6" /> Jesus answers the tempter that God alone is to be worshipped and served,<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Matthew 4:10" /> and tells the Samaritan woman that the Father seeks those who worship him in spirit and truth.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="John 4:23–24" /> Paul explains what happens instead: people who knew God did not honour him, and exchanged his glory for images and served the creature rather than the Creator.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 1:21–25" /> Even greed counts as idolatry.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Colossians 3:5" />

Calvin explains why worship is so persistent. A sense of deity is engraved on every human mind and cannot be erased; even those who deny God are haunted by it.<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="I.3.1–3" /> Because that sense is suppressed and distorted rather than lost, "the human mind is, so to speak, a perpetual forge of idols": the mind conceives a god and the hand gives it shape.<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="I.11.8" /> Bavinck calls this a seed of religion in every person, which sin does not uproot but corrupts into idolatry.<Cite source="bavinck-reformed-dogmatics-monergism-1" locator="Vol. 1, §8.5, PDF p. 231" />

The Heidelberg Catechism defines idolatry inwardly: "having or inventing something in which one trusts in place of or alongside of the only true God."<Cite source="heidelberg-catechism" locator="Q&A 95" /> Westminster adds that the light of nature shows God is to be feared, loved and served, but that "the acceptable way of worshiping the true God is instituted by himself."<Cite source="wcf" locator="21.1" />

## What this explains well

The account explains why religion appears in every culture and so often takes destructive forms: a real orientation toward God is misdirected. It treats secular devotions to nation, money or success as continuous with religious idolatry, since both concern ultimate trust. And it explains why worshippers become like what they worship.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Psalm 115:4–8" />

## Strongest objection

Naturalists answer that religious devotion is fully explained without God. Hume, though he professed belief in an intelligent author of nature, traced popular religion to hopes and fears about life's uncertain events, not to any primary instinct.<Cite source="hume-natural-history-religion" locator="N 0.1; 2.4–2.5" /> Cognitive science of religion treats belief in invisible agents as a byproduct or adaptation of ordinary cognition.<Cite source="sep-religion-science" locator="§1.4" /> On this view the "sense of deity" is a cognitive bias, and calling secular commitments idolatry is a rhetorical move.

## Christian reply

A natural explanation of how belief arises does not decide whether it is true; the scientific accounts are neutral on that point, as their own interpreters note.<Cite source="sep-religion-science" locator="§1.4" /> If God made humans to know him, natural faculties that generate belief in him are what one would expect. Bavinck argued that no naturalistic theory had satisfactorily explained religion's origin, and that it is what distinguishes humans from animals.<Cite source="bavinck-reformed-dogmatics-monergism-1" locator="Vol. 1, §8.16, PDF pp. 257–258" /> The charge of idolatry is not a claim that every commitment is religion. It applies where something is trusted as ultimate, in Heidelberg's sense. The inference to God rests on Scripture's account of the heart, not on cognitive science.

## Deep dive

#### Religion embraces the whole person

Bavinck rejects accounts that place religion in the intellect, the will or feeling alone. Religion embraces the whole person, head, heart and hand, and is the foundation of the true, the good and the beautiful.<Cite source="bavinck-reformed-dogmatics-monergism-1" locator="Vol. 1, §8.15, PDF p. 256" /> That is why the Larger Catechism's first-commandment duties include knowing, trusting, loving, delighting in and fearing God, and the sins it forbids include disordered affections toward created things.<Cite source="westminster-larger-catechism" locator="Q. 104–105" /> Idolatry begins in what the heart loves and trusts before it reaches what the hand makes. Augustine's "restless heart" names the same orientation from the inside.<Cite source="augustine-confessions" locator="I.1.1" />

#### Images, worship and the regulative principle

The Reformed churches read the second commandment strictly. God may not be represented by any image, though creatures may be portrayed, and images are not to be worshipped or used to serve God.<Cite source="heidelberg-catechism" locator="Q&A 97" /> Turretin draws the same line: images of creatures for history or ornament are lawful, but images of God and religious images in places of worship are not.<Cite source="turretin-institutio-1847" locator="XI.10.2–4" /> Westminster's principle that God is to be worshipped only as he has prescribed is specifically Reformed. Lutheran, Roman Catholic and Orthodox Christians hold different views of worship and images.<Cite source="wcf" locator="21.1" />

#### Whole-life worship and the mediator

Worship is not limited to services. Paul urges believers to present their bodies as a living sacrifice, their reasonable worship.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 12:1" /> Westminster also requires worship in families and in private, and teaches that since the Fall worship is offered through Christ as mediator.<Cite source="wcf" locator="21.2, 21.6" /> The anthropology behind this account is in <QuestionLink question="what-is-man" worldview="christianity">the human-nature anchor</QuestionLink>; the self-deceiving suppression of known truth that Romans 1 describes is treated in <QuestionLink question="self-deception" worldview="christianity">self-deception</QuestionLink>.
