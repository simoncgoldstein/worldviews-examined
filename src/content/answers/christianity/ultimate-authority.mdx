## The Christian view

When claims conflict, Reformed Christianity appeals finally to God himself, speaking in Scripture. God "is not man, that he should lie,"<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Numbers 23:19" /> and Jesus prays, "your word is truth."<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="John 17:17" /> Scripture is "breathed out by God,"<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="2 Timothy 3:16" /> written by people carried along by the Holy Spirit.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="2 Peter 1:21" /> So the Westminster Confession concludes that the supreme judge of every controversy "can be no other but the Holy Spirit speaking in the Scripture."<Cite source="wcf" locator="1.10" /> The Belgic Confession puts custom, majority, age, councils and official decisions all beneath "the truth of God."<Cite source="belgic-confession" locator="Art. 7" />

Behind the Christian view of Scripture stands Christ's own. Jesus received the Scriptures of Israel as the word of God. He came, he said, not to abolish the Law or the Prophets but to fulfill them, and "not an iota, not a dot" would pass from the Law until all was accomplished.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Matthew 5:17–18" /> In argument he could rest a case on a single word of a psalm, because "Scripture cannot be broken."<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="John 10:35" /> After his resurrection he rebuked his disciples for being slow to believe the prophets and, beginning with Moses, explained what all the Scriptures said about him: everything written about him in "the Law of Moses and the Prophets and the Psalms" had to be fulfilled.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Luke 24:25–27, 44" /> As Bavinck observes, Jesus and the apostles cite the Old Testament as God's own speech, accept its history as well as its teaching, and argue from its very words; they never stand over it as critics.<Cite source="bavinck-reformed-dogmatics-monergism-1" locator="Vol. 1, §11.10, PDF pp. 412–414" note="automated English translation; paraphrased" /> A disciple receives Scripture as his Lord received it. Christ also provided for the witness that became the New Testament: he promised the apostles he sent that the Spirit would teach them and remind them of all he had said.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="John 14:26" /> The church receives their writings as that Spirit-taught apostolic witness.

Why does Scripture need no higher court to certify it? Because there is none. Its authority rests "wholly upon God (who is truth itself) the author thereof," not on the testimony of any person or church.<Cite source="wcf" locator="1.4" /> It has real marks of divinity, such as the majesty of its teaching, the harmony of its parts and its power, but full persuasion comes "from the inward work of the Holy Spirit bearing witness by and with the Word in our hearts."<Cite source="wcf" locator="1.5" /> The Spirit's witness adds no new message: nothing is to be added to Scripture "by new revelations of the Spirit."<Cite source="wcf" locator="1.6" />

Reason and the church serve this authority. Reason is not rejected: Turretin gives it the work of judging whether statements cohere and whether conclusions follow, but "organically and ministerially, not despotically," using the rules of reasoning God implanted in us.<Cite source="turretin-institutio-1847" locator="I.10.1–3" note="Latin (organice et ministerialiter, non despotice); our translation" /> The church is the instrument through which Scripture reaches people, not the ground of its authority.<Cite source="turretin-institutio-1847" locator="II.6.6" note="Latin; paraphrased" /> Disputed passages are settled by clearer ones, because "the infallible rule of interpretation of Scripture is the Scripture itself."<Cite source="wcf" locator="1.9" />

## What this explains well

The account gives believers a standard above every majority, culture and church, including their own. It explains why a final authority must vouch for itself, since anything that certified it would be higher still. And it gives a public test, as the Bereans examined "the Scriptures daily to see if these things were so."<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Acts 17:11" />

## Strongest objection

The objection is circularity: "You believe the Bible because the Bible says so." A Muslim can say the same of the Qur'an; if self-attestation is allowed for one book, why not for all? The naturalist adds that holding every claim open to revision is the only honest policy, and that no belief should be beyond revision.<Cite source="sep-quine" locator="§3.3" note="Quine 1951, as quoted by SEP" /> And Protestants without an infallible interpreter still disagree about what Scripture means.

## Christian reply

The Christian accepts, deliberately, that the final authority attests itself. Every framework reaches commitments that no higher court certifies. Naturalism's revisable method is not certified by anything outside that method, as its own thesis of "no first philosophy" admits.<Cite source="sep-quine" locator="§4.4" /> Bavinck argues that every science rests on first principles that can be recognized but not demonstrated. Rome's answer, that the church proves Scripture, is no less circular, since the church's authority is itself proved from Scripture.<Cite source="bavinck-reformed-dogmatics-monergism-1" locator="Vol. 1, §12, PDF p. 462" /> The real question is whether a framework's ultimate commitments are coherent, consistent with themselves and adequate to the reality they claim to explain.

Self-attestation alone does not settle *which* book is God's word, and the tradition never said it did. Nor does the Christian believe the Bible merely because the Bible says so. He receives it because Christ, whom he trusts as Lord, received it as God's word; because the apostles Christ sent wrote and passed it on; because Scripture shows its own marks of divinity; and because the Spirit persuades him of what is there. This is not a neutral proof that first establishes Jesus' authority by historical argument and then certifies every verse by deduction. It is a cumulative case held from inside the faith, whose strands support one another. It does, though, move the question somewhere public: whether Jesus is who he claimed to be. Calvin treats the external evidences as real "secondary helps,"<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="I.8.1, 13" /> and the historical case is examined under <QuestionLink question="revelation" worldview="christianity">revelation</QuestionLink> and <QuestionLink question="jesus" worldview="christianity">Jesus</QuestionLink>. As for Protestant disagreement, the tradition claims a sufficient Scripture, not unanimous readers, and will not place a fallible human court above the text.

## Deep dive

#### Ground, cause and instrument

Turretin's distinctions keep the parts in order. Scripture's own marks are the *argument* for believing it, the Holy Spirit is the *efficient cause* who persuades, and the church is the *instrument* through which Scripture is presented.<Cite source="turretin-institutio-1847" locator="II.6.6" note="Latin; paraphrased" /> He argues that Scripture must be known through itself, as first principles are; otherwise each proof would need another, without end.<Cite source="turretin-institutio-1847" locator="II.6.11" note="Latin; paraphrased" /> Public decision about what is true of God belongs to God speaking in Scripture. Each believer's private judgment of discretion belongs to reason illumined by the Spirit.<Cite source="turretin-institutio-1847" locator="I.9.2" note="Latin; paraphrased" /> The Westminster Confession even says that demanding blind faith destroys liberty of conscience "and reason also."<Cite source="wcf" locator="20.2" /> Turretin's image for reason's place is biblical: reason is Hagar the servant, who must be subject to Scripture, not Sarah the mistress set over it.<Cite source="turretin-institutio-1847" locator="I.8.6" note="Latin; paraphrased" />

#### Two emphases within one doctrine

Calvin says that Scripture shows its truth as plainly "as white and black do of their colour,"<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="I.7.2" /> and he puts the weight on the Spirit's witness: "the testimony of the Spirit is superior to reason," and Scripture, "carrying its own evidence along with it, deigns not to submit to proofs and arguments."<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="I.7.4–5" /> Turretin gives more space to Scripture's marks as reasons for belief. Bavinck notes that with Turretin the testimony of the Spirit began to lose its place of honor, and he restores it: the Spirit's witness far surpasses the proofs, adds no new revelation, and makes faith certain knowledge.<Cite source="bavinck-reformed-dogmatics-monergism-1" locator="Vol. 1, §18, PDF pp. 611–612" /> The difference is one of emphasis inside a shared doctrine.

#### Van Til's apologetic use

Van Til turned the doctrine into an apologetic method. There is no neutral starting point from which a believer and an unbeliever could jointly assess Scripture, and "all reasoning is, in the nature of the case, circular reasoning" where ultimate commitments are concerned.<Cite source="van-til-defense" locator="p. 118" /> He denied that the natural man has the right to judge by his own reason what is possible or impossible for God.<Cite source="van-til-defense" locator="pp. 98–99" /> This sharpens the older doctrine for debate. It is not a different doctrine, and its claims about what unbelief cannot account for are examined under <QuestionLink question="logic-binding" worldview="christianity">logic</QuestionLink> and <QuestionLink question="induction" worldview="christianity">induction</QuestionLink>.

#### The confessional summary

The Westminster catechisms call the Scriptures "the only rule" of faith and obedience, and say that the Scriptures manifest themselves to be God's word while only the Spirit can fully persuade.<Cite source="westminster-larger-catechism" locator="Q. 3–4" /><Cite source="westminster-shorter-catechism" locator="Q. 2" /> The Belgic Confession receives the canonical books not chiefly because the church approves them, but "because the Holy Spirit testifies in our hearts that they are from God, and also because they prove themselves to be from God."<Cite source="belgic-confession" locator="Art. 5" />
