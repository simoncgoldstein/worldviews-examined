## The Christian view

The Bible tells history as one story with a beginning, a middle and an end. God creates the world; humanity falls; God promises a remedy and calls Abraham, through whom "all the families of the earth shall be blessed."<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Genesis 12:3" /> The story runs through Israel's covenant and kingdom to Christ: "when the fullness of time had come, God sent forth his Son."<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Galatians 4:4" /> It continues in the church's mission "to the end of the age,"<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Matthew 28:20" /> and ends in resurrection, judgement and a renewed creation: "Behold, I am making all things new."<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Revelation 21:5" /> God's purpose is "a plan for the fullness of time, to unite all things in Christ."<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Ephesians 1:9–10" />

Christ is the centre, and his work is unrepeatable: he "appeared once for all at the end of the ages."<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Hebrews 9:26" /> That is why Augustine rejected the ancient belief in endless cycles: "far be it, I say, from us to believe this. For once Christ died for our sins; and, rising from the dead, He dies no more."<Cite source="augustine-city-of-god" locator="XII.13" />

Augustine also explains why history does not look like steady progress. Two cities, formed by two loves,<Cite source="augustine-city-of-god" locator="XIV.28" /> run through "this whole time or world-age" from Cain and Abel onward.<Cite source="augustine-city-of-god" locator="XV.1" /> They are mixed together, using the same earthly goods and the same civil peace for different ends,<Cite source="augustine-city-of-god" locator="XIX.17" /> and they are separated only at the judgement. So the Christian expects neither an earthly utopia nor meaningless chaos. Bavinck adds that the kingdom's history is not an evolutionary process; the risen Christ himself directs world history.<Cite source="bavinck-reformed-dogmatics-monergism-3" locator="Vol. 3, §42, PDF p. 495" /> The final day is fixed but unknown, so that believers "shake off all carnal security."<Cite source="wcf" locator="33.3" />

## What this explains well

The view gives history meaning without pretending that it steadily improves, and it accounts for the stubborn mixture of good and evil in every age.

## Strongest objection

The pattern seems to be read into history rather than out of it. Historians find contingency, not a plot, and on the naturalist picture the world science presents is "purposeless" and "void of meaning."<Cite source="russell-free-mans-worship" locator="para. 5" /> The record of wars, famines and extinctions shows no visible direction toward the good. A providential reading also seems to make God answerable for that suffering.

## Christian reply

The Christian does not claim to read providence off the evening news. Augustine insists that the two cities stay mixed until the end, and that the length of the present age "cannot be measured."<Cite source="augustine-city-of-god" locator="XXII.30" /> The claim rests on revelation and above all on one public event, the resurrection (see <QuestionLink question="jesus" worldview="christianity">Jesus</QuestionLink>). Given that, the shape it proposes fits what we actually see: neither progress nor pure chaos, but goods and evils growing together. The moral question about suffering is real and is taken up under <QuestionLink question="suffering" worldview="christianity">suffering</QuestionLink> and <QuestionLink question="evil" worldview="christianity">evil</QuestionLink>.

## Deep dive

#### Seven ages and an eighth day

Augustine closes *The City of God* by dividing history into ages, like the days of creation. Five run from Adam to Christ; the sixth, the present one, has no fixed length. The seventh "shall be our Sabbath," ending not in evening but in "an eighth and eternal day, consecrated by the resurrection of Christ."<Cite source="augustine-city-of-god" locator="XXII.30" /> Against the philosophers' cycles he applies the psalm "The wicked walk in a circle," not because lives recur but because their false doctrine runs in circles.<Cite source="augustine-city-of-god" locator="XII.13" />

#### Two cities in one world

The two cities share the necessities of this life. The earthly city seeks earthly peace for its own sake. The heavenly city, living "like a captive and a stranger," uses that peace and obeys the laws that maintain it, so far as faith allows, until mortality passes away.<Cite source="augustine-city-of-god" locator="XIX.17" /> Christians may therefore work for justice and order without mistaking any political achievement for the kingdom of God.

#### One covenant, many administrations

The Westminster Confession sees one covenant of grace administered differently before and after Christ: "not therefore two covenants of grace, differing in substance, but one and the same, under various dispensations."<Cite source="wcf" locator="7.5–6" /> Reformed Christians differ over the millennium and over how far the gospel will spread before the end, but they share this single, Christ-centred line.

#### Renewal, not replacement

Bavinck argues that redemption is not a second, new creation but a re-creation. God does not discard the fallen world but raises it up.<Cite source="bavinck-reformed-dogmatics-monergism-4" locator="Vol. 4, §56, PDF p. 594" /> The fire of the last day, he says, cleanses and purifies the world rather than annihilating its substance.<Cite source="bavinck-reformed-dogmatics-monergism-4" locator="Vol. 4, §56, PDF pp. 626–627" /> The end of history is therefore the fulfilment of creation, not its abandonment (see <QuestionLink question="final-end" worldview="christianity">final end</QuestionLink>).
