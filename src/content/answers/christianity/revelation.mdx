## The Christian view

Christianity says God makes himself known in two ways. The first is the world itself: "The heavens declare the glory of God,"<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Psalm 19:1–4" /> and his power and divine nature are clearly seen in what he has made.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 1:19–20" /> That general revelation leaves people accountable, but it does not tell a sinner how to be reconciled to God.<Cite source="wcf" locator="1.1" />

The second is special revelation, given in history. God called Abraham, rescued Israel, sent prophets and finally came in person: "Long ago, at many times and in many ways, God spoke to our fathers by the prophets, but in these last days he has spoken to us by his Son."<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Hebrews 1:1–2" /> This is a connected history of acts and words, centred on Christ,<Cite source="bavinck-reformed-dogmatics-monergism-1" locator="Vol. 1, §10, PDF pp. 338–340, 376" /> and Scripture is its written record and interpretation.

How would anyone know it is genuine? The tradition gives several answers, which should be kept apart.

1. **Scripture's own voice.** The believer's certainty rests on God speaking in Scripture, made convincing by the Holy Spirit. That is the ground of faith, not an argument offered to outsiders (see <QuestionLink question="ultimate-authority" worldview="christianity">ultimate authority</QuestionLink>).
2. **Coherence.** Scripture holds together across many writers and centuries; Westminster counts "the consent of all the parts" among its marks.<Cite source="wcf" locator="1.5" /> Whether its account fits reality better than its rivals is the comparison this site makes.
3. **Public signs.** Revelation came in events done before witnesses: "in your midst, as you yourselves know," Peter told Jerusalem.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Acts 2:22" /> Luke wrote from eyewitness tradition so that his reader "may have certainty."<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Luke 1:1–4" />
4. **The resurrection.** Above all, the faith stakes itself on one public event. God "has given assurance to all by raising him from the dead,"<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Acts 17:30–31" /> and "if Christ has not been raised, your faith is futile."<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="1 Corinthians 15:17" /> The historical case is examined under <QuestionLink question="jesus" worldview="christianity">Jesus</QuestionLink>.

## What this explains well

The account explains why Christianity invites historical scrutiny, and why a faith resting on public events can still be held with personal certainty.

## Strongest objection

Many traditions claim revelation, and believers in each report the same inward certainty. Hume pressed the point: in religion "whatever is different is contrary," so each tradition's miracles count against the others'.<Cite source="hume-enquiry" locator="10.24" /> Conflicting religious experiences undermine one another, and natural causes may explain them.<Cite source="sep-religious-experience" locator="§3" /> The biblical events reach us only through believers' texts. And the appeal to the Spirit looks circular: the Bible is accepted because of a conviction the Bible says will come.

## Christian reply

The reply is cumulative. The Spirit's witness grounds the believer's certainty; it is not offered to the sceptic as evidence. Nor is it a private message: it persuades of a public text that anyone can examine, and adds nothing to it.<Cite source="wcf" locator="1.6" />

Rival claims are not all of one kind. Some rest on a national memory, some on a text's literary excellence, some on an eternal word with no author, some on insight confirmed in practice. Christianity rests its case on a datable public event, proclaimed where it happened while named witnesses were alive.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="1 Corinthians 15:3–8" /> That makes the claim testable, not proven, and diversity calls for comparison rather than dismissal.

Hume himself granted that testimony could in principle establish a violation of nature.<Cite source="hume-enquiry" locator="10.36" /> The real dispute is the prior probability: if God exists and intends to make himself known, revelation is not intrinsically absurd. All ancient history comes through texts; the question is how close and reliable these are. Calvin calls such evidences "secondary helps to our weakness," real but unable by themselves to produce faith.<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="I.8.13" /> The Christian places no neutral authority above God, but does not ask outsiders to accept a closed circle either.

## Deep dive

#### Revelation as history

Bavinck describes special revelation as theophany, prophecy and miracle forming one organic whole. It does not merely report history; it enters history and makes a history, and the incarnation is its centre. Because revelation is history, he argues, it is known the way all history is known, through testimony.<Cite source="bavinck-reformed-dogmatics-monergism-1" locator="Vol. 1, §10, PDF pp. 338–340, 376, 379" /> With Christ and the apostolic Scriptures, the earlier ways of revealing God's will have ceased.<Cite source="wcf" locator="1.1" />

#### Calvin's secondary helps

Calvin gathers a long list of evidences: the majesty and harmony of Scripture, the antiquity of Moses, fulfilled prophecy, the preservation of the books, the plain style of the Gospels, the consent of the church and the constancy of the martyrs.<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="I.8.1–13" /> Moses, he argues, published his miracles before the whole people: "How, then, could he possibly impose on the very eye-witnesses of what was done?"<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="I.8.5" /> Yet he concludes that it is "foolish to attempt to prove to infidels that the Scripture is the Word of God," since such proofs avail only after the Spirit has persuaded the heart.<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="I.8.13" />

Augustine framed a related argument from the origin of belief. He names "three incredibles": that Christ rose in the flesh, that the world believed it, and that "plain fishermen, and very few in number" persuaded the world of it.<Cite source="augustine-city-of-god" locator="XXII.5" /> Whether that spread is best explained by the truth of the message is argued under <QuestionLink question="jesus" worldview="christianity">Jesus</QuestionLink>.

#### Evidence and faith

Reformed theologians weigh these evidences differently. Calvin's catalogue gives them real force. Bavinck warns that historical-apologetic proof cannot come before faith and, used to produce faith, does not achieve it.<Cite source="bavinck-reformed-dogmatics-monergism-1" locator="Vol. 1, §14, PDF pp. 536–537" /> Van Til went further and denied that any evidence is neutral (see <QuestionLink question="ultimate-authority" worldview="christianity">ultimate authority</QuestionLink>). All three affirm both that the evidence is real and that the Spirit's work is decisive. The difference is one of emphasis.

#### Prophecy and a single standard

The Belgic Confession appeals to fulfilled prediction: "even the blind themselves are able to see that the things predicted in them do happen."<Cite source="belgic-confession" locator="Art. 5" /> Its force depends on when prophecies such as those in Daniel and Isaiah were written, which critical scholarship disputes. This page does not settle those dates, so it treats prophecy as an argument within the Christian reading of Scripture, not as public proof.

Calvin's argument from Moses' public miracles has the same form as the Jewish argument from Sinai (see <QuestionLink question="revelation" worldview="judaism">the Jewish answer</QuestionLink>), and it faces the same objection: we know those events only through a later text. One standard must apply to both. The difference the Christian points to is distance. Paul's letters were written within about twenty-five years of the events and name witnesses he had met.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Galatians 1:18–19" />
