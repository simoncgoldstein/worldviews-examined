## The Christian view

Guilt is more than an unpleasant feeling. Sin makes a person answerable to God, even when conscience is quiet; an accusing conscience can also need correction rather than unquestioning obedience. Paul locates justification in God's gift through Christ's redemption and presents the cross as the disclosure of divine justice in forgiving sinners.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 3:21–26" />

The ground of pardon is therefore Christ's obedience and satisfaction, not the sinner's remorse, renewed character or compensation. Faith receives his righteousness; it does not replace that righteousness with the worth of believing. Westminster distinguishes pardon and acceptance from the inward renewal that accompanies them.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 4:1–8" /><Cite source="wcf" locator="11.1–3" /> Paul's verdict has force precisely because the guilt was real: “There is therefore now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus.”<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 8:1" />

Repentance includes confession and turning from particular wrongs. It is necessary, yet supplies no satisfaction for sin. Injured people remain entitled to truthful acknowledgment and appropriate repair.<Cite source="wcf" locator="15.2–6" /><Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Luke 19:1–10" />

## What this explains well

This account distinguishes three things often confused: the wrong that needs pardon, the judgment that acquits, and the person who needs changing. Forgiveness can be secure while sanctification remains incomplete. Heidelberg speaks directly to a conscience that still accuses and to corruption that must still be resisted; its comfort rests on Christ's righteousness rather than a flawless new record.<Cite source="heidelberg-catechism" locator="Q&A 56, 60–61" />

## Strongest objection

If responsibility is personal, how can someone else's righteousness or suffering discharge it? Kant's demand that moral responsibility concern what a person makes of himself presses against transferable moral credit.<Cite source="kant-religion-greene-hudson" locator="Bk. I, General Observation" /> The objection is sharper than denying that guilty people need mercy: it questions whether substitution is justice, and whether acquittal leaves the victim's loss unaddressed.

## Christian reply

The Reformed reply concerns a willing mediator who represents his people, not a third party punished by accident. Hebrews depicts Christ offering himself and cleansing conscience for service.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Hebrews 9:11–15" /> Witsius connects Adam’s and Christ’s representative roles rather than treating obedience as private credit moving between unrelated individuals.<Cite source="witsius-economy-covenants" locator="I.8.31" /> His satisfaction answers guilt before God; it does not retrospectively make the injury harmless. Restitution and reconciliation remain fruits of repentance. This defense depends on the Christian account of Christ and representation, which the critic may still dispute.

## Deep dive

#### Pardon and a changed person

Justification and sanctification answer different aspects of sin. Acceptance before God cannot rest on the believer's degree of inward holiness, because that holiness remains incomplete. Yet saving faith does not leave the person morally untouched: Westminster describes faith as accompanied by other saving graces and active in love.<Cite source="wcf" locator="11.2; 13.1–3" /> The distinction prevents both despair over imperfect renewal and the claim that forgiven wrongdoing no longer matters.

Repentance likewise must not become a competing payment. Its grief concerns the wrong's character as well as its danger; it turns toward God's mercy in Christ. Confession names particular sins rather than offering a vague acknowledgment that everyone is imperfect.<Cite source="wcf" locator="15.2–5" /> The point is truthful return, not the production of an emotion intense enough to purchase acquittal. A person can regret consequences without hating the wrong, or struggle to feel relief while actually trusting the promise.

#### What forgiveness leaves to do

Zacchaeus's promised restitution belongs within a narrative of salvation, not outside it.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Luke 19:1–10" /> The relation matters: compensation should express a changed response to the neighbor, without becoming a substitute for Christ's work. Some injuries cannot be fully repaired by the wrongdoer. Christian forgiveness does not manufacture that missing repair by declaring the original damage imaginary.

Westminster also distinguishes continuing forgiveness from the believer's experience of God's fatherly displeasure. Serious sin can wound conscience and interrupt comfort without making Christ's righteousness fluctuate.<Cite source="wcf" locator="11.5; 17.3; 18.4" /> Assurance therefore deserves neither automatic presumption nor a rule that every anxious believer is condemned.
