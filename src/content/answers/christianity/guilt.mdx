## The Christian view

Guilt is more than a painful feeling. Sin makes a person answerable to God whether or not conscience is troubled, and a wrong done to a neighbor is also a wrong before God.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Psalm 51:4; Romans 3:19–23" />

The question is therefore not only whether God is willing to forgive, but how he can forgive and remain just. A judge who simply waved real wrongs away would not be good. Having shown that all have sinned, Paul says that God justifies sinners freely "through the redemption that is in Christ Jesus, whom God put forward as a propitiation by his blood." God had long "passed over former sins," and the cross shows that this patience was never indifference. It shows his righteousness, "so that he might be just and the justifier of the one who has faith in Jesus."<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 3:21–26" /> At the cross, forgiveness does not suspend justice: justice is done, and mercy is given.

God himself provides the remedy, in love: he "sent his Son to be the propitiation for our sins."<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="1 John 4:10" /> The Son comes willingly, takes our nature and stands as the head of his people. He bears what they owe and gives what they lack. Isaiah's servant is pierced for others' transgressions and bears their iniquities; his soul makes "an offering for guilt," and he makes many to be accounted righteous.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Isaiah 53:4–6, 10–11" /> The apostles read the cross the same way. Christ bore our sins in his body and became a curse for us, suffering "the righteous for the unrighteous, that he might bring us to God."<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="1 Peter 2:24; 3:18; Galatians 3:13" /> Paul compresses it into one sentence: "For our sake he made him to be sin who knew no sin, so that in him we might become the righteousness of God."<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="2 Corinthians 5:21" /> Christ did not become sinful; the sinless one bore sin that was not his own. Calvin connects Paul's phrase with the Old Testament sacrifices for sin, which were themselves called by the name of sin.<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="II.16.6" />

This is more than suffering. Christ's death is his obedient life freely offered: he "offered himself without blemish to God" once for all, as no repeated sacrifice could, and so cleanses the conscience.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Hebrews 9:11–14; 10:10–14" /> God's justice is not bypassed but answered; Reformed theology calls that answer **satisfaction**. The Westminster Confession says that Christ, "by his perfect obedience, and sacrifice of himself," "fully satisfied the justice of his Father," and purchased reconciliation.<Cite source="wcf" locator="8.5" /> **Propitiation** adds that God's wrath, his righteous and settled opposition to sin, is turned away, not because sinners pacify him, but because he provides the sacrifice himself.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 1:18; 5:9" /> In Calvin's words, "it is because he first loves us, that he afterwards reconciles us to himself."<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="II.16.3–4" />

These benefits become a sinner's own through union with Christ. While we are "separated from him," Calvin writes, nothing he suffered and did "is of the least benefit to us"; the Holy Spirit binds us to him, and faith receives him.<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="III.1.1" /> Joined to him, believers are justified: God pardons them and accepts them as righteous by imputing Christ's obedience and satisfaction to them, not for anything in them, nor by counting their faith as righteousness. Faith receives; it pays nothing.<Cite source="wcf" locator="11.1–2" /> "There is therefore now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus."<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 8:1" />

Repentance turns from particular wrongs. It is necessary, yet it supplies no satisfaction for sin, and injured people remain entitled to truthful acknowledgment and repair.<Cite source="wcf" locator="15.2–6" /><Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Luke 19:1–10" />

## What this explains well

This account keeps apart the wrong that needs pardon, the verdict that acquits and the person who still needs changing, so forgiveness can be secure while sanctification is unfinished. It shows how pardon can be free without being unjust, glorifying "both the exact justice and rich grace of God."<Cite source="wcf" locator="11.3" /> And it comforts a conscience that still accuses with Christ's righteousness, not a flawless new record.<Cite source="heidelberg-catechism" locator="Q&A 56, 60–61" />

## Strongest objection

If responsibility is personal, how can someone else's suffering or righteousness count for me? Kant argues that the debt of sin cannot be discharged by another, "so far as we can judge according to the justice of our human reason." It is "no transmissible liability which can be made over to another like a financial indebtedness," but "the most personal of all debts."<Cite source="kant-religion-greene-hudson" locator="Bk. II, Sec. One, C (third difficulty)" /> Punishing the innocent for the guilty looks unjust, and crediting one person with another's obedience looks like a legal fiction. Acquittal before God may also seem to leave the victim's loss unaddressed.

## Christian reply

The objection is strongest against a picture the Reformed doctrine rejects: God punishing an unrelated innocent so that an unchanged offender may go free. Turretin grants that substituting the innocent for the guilty seems at first sight unjust, and asks what would make it just. The substitute must share the nature that sinned, consent freely, have authority over his own life, be able to bear the whole penalty without being held by it, and be without sin. Christ meets every condition, and no one is wronged: not Christ, who chose it, nor the law, which he fulfills.<Cite source="turretin-institutio-1847" locator="XIV.10.13–15 (vol. 2, p. 371)" note="Latin; paraphrased" /> For the same reason the Heidelberg Catechism insists that the mediator be truly human as well as truly God.<Cite source="heidelberg-catechism" locator="Q&A 14–17" />

The deeper answer is union. Turretin grounds the exchange in Christ's twofold union with his people: natural, because he shares their nature, and forensic or mystical, because he and they are made one. Being one with them, he could take their sins on himself and pass his righteousness to them.<Cite source="turretin-institutio-1847" locator="XIV.10.15 (vol. 2, p. 371)" note="Latin; paraphrased" /> Scripture's pattern is Adam and Christ, two heads whose acts count for those they represent.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 5:12–19" /> Witsius reads Romans 5 as a covenant parallel: as Adam's sin is charged to those included with him in the covenant of works, so Christ's obedience is charged to his covenant people in the covenant of grace. His obedience is not credit passed between strangers.<Cite source="witsius-economy-covenants" locator="I.8.31" /> The union is also spiritual and real: believers do not view Christ "as at a distance and without us," Calvin says, but have "put him on" and been "ingrafted into his body."<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="III.11.10" /> Union does not replace imputation. The righteousness counted to believers remains Christ's; union is why it can justly be counted to those joined to him.

Two limits remain. Christ's satisfaction answers guilt before God; it does not make the injury harmless, and restitution remains a fruit of repentance. And the reply depends on the biblical account of Adam, Christ, covenant headship and union. A critic who rejects those premises, or whose theory of personal identity leaves no room for such union, will remain unpersuaded. The claim is that substitution is just within that account, not on every theory.

## Deep dive

#### One work, several images

Scripture describes Christ's work as sacrifice, ransom, reconciliation, the bearing of a curse and victory over hostile powers. These are not rival theories. The death that pays the debt also disarms the accuser: God canceled "the record of debt that stood against us," nailed it to the cross and triumphed over the rulers and authorities.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Colossians 2:13–15" /> In Reformed theology, substitution is the center that holds these images together, not a replacement for them. Christ's person and his offices of prophet, priest and king belong to <QuestionLink question="jesus" worldview="christianity">who Jesus is</QuestionLink>.

#### Satisfaction: the word and the thing

Owen observed that "satisfaction" is a term borrowed from the law, not a word the Bible uses for Christ's death, though the thing it names appears throughout: in Scripture's idiom, to bear iniquity is to undergo the punishment due to it (Leviticus 5:1; Isaiah 53:11–12).<Cite source="owen-works-goold-10" locator="Death of Death III.7, pp. 265–266" /> Christ, he argued, took on himself the whole punishment due to sin and, in offering himself, did what pleased God more than all the sins he answered for had offended him.<Cite source="owen-works-goold-10" locator="Death of Death III.7, p. 267" /> Turretin likewise counts Christ's lifelong obedience to the law, not only his sufferings, within the satisfaction credited to believers, as the received Reformed view.<Cite source="turretin-institutio-1847" locator="XIV.13.1–2 (vol. 2, p. 391)" note="Latin; paraphrased" />

The Westminster Confession keeps accomplishment and application apart. Chapter 8 describes what Christ did; chapter 11 says that sinners are not justified until the Holy Spirit "doth, in due time, actually apply Christ unto them."<Cite source="wcf" locator="8.4–5; 11.4" /> How that application happens (calling, regeneration, justification, sanctification and perseverance) belongs to <QuestionLink question="self-salvation" worldview="christianity">whether man can save himself</QuestionLink>.

#### Owen and definite atonement

Owen pressed the doctrine into an argument about the atonement's design. If satisfaction has truly been made for someone's sins, he reasoned, justice requires that person's release, so Christ's death secures the salvation of those for whom it was offered. Christ bore the punishment for all the sins of all people, all the sins of some, or some sins of all: if the last, no one could be saved; if the first, why are not all freed?<Cite source="owen-works-goold-10" locator="Death of Death I.3, pp. 173–174; III.7, p. 267" /> The confessions teach the particular design without resting on that argument. The Canons of Dort hold that Christ's death is "more than sufficient to atone for the sins of the whole world," that the gospel is to be announced to all "without differentiation or discrimination," and that it was God's will that Christ "should effectively redeem... all those and only those who were chosen from eternity to salvation."<Cite source="canons-of-dort" locator="II.3, 5, 8" /> The Westminster Confession likewise says that Christ purchased reconciliation "for all those whom the Father hath given unto him," and that he certainly applies redemption to all for whom he purchased it.<Cite source="wcf" locator="8.5, 8.8" /> Owen's dilemma and his reasoning from payment to release are his own defense of that confessional teaching, and not every Reformed theologian argued as he did. His editor notes that some, while holding that Christ's death infallibly secures the salvation of the elect, also gave it a general reference to all.<Cite source="owen-works-goold-10" locator="Goold, prefatory note to Death of Death, p. 141" />

#### Kant's own substitute

Kant does not finally dispense with substitution. In his own solution, the converted person is "morally another" than the old self, and the good disposition, which he personifies as the Son of God, "bears as vicarious substitute the guilt of sin."<Cite source="kant-religion-greene-hudson" locator="Bk. II, Sec. One, C (solution of the third difficulty)" /> He moves the representative inside the person rather than abolishing representation. The disagreement is over who the representative is, and what joins him to the guilty.

#### Pardon, renewal and repair

Union with Christ joins justification and sanctification without merging them. Calvin rejected Osiander's view that believers are righteous by Christ's essential righteousness poured into them.<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="III.11.10" /> The righteousness by which they are accepted remains Christ's, counted as theirs, while the faith that receives it is never alone but works by love.<Cite source="wcf" locator="11.2; 13.1–3" /> Repentance names particular sins rather than conceding a vague imperfection, and turns toward God's mercy in Christ.<Cite source="wcf" locator="15.2–5" />

Zacchaeus's promised restitution belongs within a narrative of salvation, not outside it.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Luke 19:1–10" /> Compensation expresses a changed response to the neighbor without substituting for Christ's work. Some injuries cannot be repaired by the wrongdoer, and forgiveness does not declare the damage imaginary. Serious sin can also wound conscience and interrupt comfort without making Christ's righteousness fluctuate.<Cite source="wcf" locator="11.5; 17.3; 18.4" /> Judgment's relation to unresolved injuries belongs to <QuestionLink question="after-death" worldview="christianity">after death</QuestionLink>; inherited guilt to <QuestionLink question="great-and-terrible" worldview="christianity">human greatness and misery</QuestionLink>.
