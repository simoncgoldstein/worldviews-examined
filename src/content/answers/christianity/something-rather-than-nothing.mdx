## The Christian view

There is something rather than nothing because God willed it. Scripture's first sentence is about this: "In the beginning, God created the heavens and the earth."<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Genesis 1:1" /> God "calls into existence the things that do not exist,"<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 4:17" /> and the heavenly worship in Revelation gives the reason: "by your will they existed and were created."<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Revelation 4:11" />

God did not have to create; he lacked nothing, and the world adds nothing to him. Augustine reads Genesis's "it was good" as showing that God made the world "not from any necessity, nor for the sake of supplying any want, but solely from His own goodness."<Cite source="augustine-city-of-god" locator="XI.24" /> The world is a free gift, not an overflow God could not hold back.

Creation is also "from nothing": not shaped from a pre-existing material, and not a portion of God's own being.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Hebrews 11:3" /> So "why is there something?" has a personal answer, God's free will, while "why is there God?" has a different kind of answer. God exists from himself, as <QuestionLink question="ultimate-reality" worldview="christianity">the anchor question</QuestionLink> explains.

## What this explains well

The account explains how the world can be fully real and yet need not have existed, and why it is good and intended rather than a by-product. It ends explanation in a person's free choice, which is accounted for by reasons rather than by a prior cause.

## Strongest objection

A free choice looks like a brute fact with a halo. If God might not have created, his choice to create is itself unexplained. Carroll argues that invoking a creator does not escape the need to posit something that simply is, so one might as well stop at the universe.<Cite source="carroll-something-rather-than-nothing" locator="§5" /> The Buddhist philosopher Vasubandhu pressed the point from another side. If God creates things in succession because of successive wishes, those wishes need causes. Then God is not the sole cause, and we are back with an endless series of conditions (our paraphrase of the Sanskrit).<Cite source="vasubandhu-akbh-gretil" locator="II.64d, Pradhan pp. 101–102" />

## Christian reply

Reformed theology does not picture God forming new wishes one after another. Turretin distinguishes God's will toward himself, which is necessary, from his will toward creatures, which is free. The world is necessary only given the decree, not in itself.<Cite source="turretin-institutio-1847" locator="III.14.5–6" /> That single eternal decree orders all succession without itself being one more link in the series.<Cite source="wcf" locator="3.1" /> Freedom is not arbitrariness either. Bavinck stresses that creation flows from God's will, yet that will is never without God's wisdom and goodness.<Cite source="bavinck-reformed-dogmatics-monergism-2" locator="Vol. 2, §29.9, PDF pp. 511–512" /> A good person's free gift is explained by reasons, even when it was not compelled. The Christian concedes that God's counsel finally exceeds our tracing,<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 11:33–36" /> and claims a better-grounded stopping point, not an explanation of everything.

## Deep dive

#### What "from nothing" means

"From nothing" does not mean that nothing is a kind of stuff God worked on. Turretin explains that the phrase marks the starting point, not a material. The world exists *after* nothing, not *out of* something called nothing. God acts outwardly not by necessity of nature but by the liberty of his will, and that is what distinguishes creation from the eternal generation of the Son (our paraphrase of the Latin).<Cite source="turretin-institutio-1847" locator="V.1.1–5, 10" /> Bavinck makes the same point: non-being is not a source of being.<Cite source="bavinck-reformed-dogmatics-monergism-2" locator="Vol. 2, §29.5, PDF p. 493" /> Augustine adds the reason the world cannot be made from God's own substance: then it would be equal to the Son.<Cite source="augustine-confessions" locator="XII.7.7" />

#### Creation with time, not in time

Asked what God was doing before he made the world, Augustine answers that there was no "before." Time itself is a creature, and God precedes all times in eternity, not in a longer stretch of time.<Cite source="augustine-confessions" locator="XI.13.15–16" /> The world was made "not in time, but simultaneously with time."<Cite source="augustine-city-of-god" locator="XI.6" /> This removes the objection that God must have waited an infinite time before acting.

#### Could the world have been eternal?

Turretin argues that the world neither was nor could have been eternal. This puts him against Aristotle on the fact, and on the bare possibility also against Thomas Aquinas and other theologians.<Cite source="turretin-institutio-1847" locator="V.3.1–5" /> Christian theologians have differed on the possibility. On the fact they agree.

#### Revelation and argument

Bavinck says that creation is finally known by faith, as Hebrews 11:3 says, and that the origin of things lies beyond what science can establish, because science presupposes being.<Cite source="bavinck-reformed-dogmatics-monergism-2" locator="Vol. 2, §29.2–3, PDF pp. 479, 485" /> Turretin still offers arguments from the dependence of causes.<Cite source="turretin-institutio-1847" locator="III.1.6–9" /> The two emphases are compatible: Scripture first, arguments as support. The confessions state the doctrine without the debates: God created all things "of nothing."<Cite source="wcf" locator="4.1" /><Cite source="belgic-confession" locator="Art. 12" />
