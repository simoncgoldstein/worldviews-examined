## The Christian view

At its root, evil is sin: any failure to conform to God's law, or any transgression of it.<Cite source="westminster-larger-catechism" locator="Q. 24" /> It is measured by God's law, not by the presence of pain or by anyone's preference. Reformed theology distinguishes this moral evil, the creature's sin, from the harms and miseries of the fallen condition that followed it: death entered human history through Adam's sin, and creation itself was subjected to futility and groans for its release.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 5:12; 8:19–22" />

Evil is not a substance competing with good. As Augustine argued, it is the privation or corruption of a good nature.<Cite source="augustine-enchiridion" locator="11, 13" /> A bad will remains a will; cruelty exploits capacities that were created good. Iniquity is the will's perversion, not a second evil nature.<Cite source="augustine-confessions" locator="VII.12.18; VII.16.22" /> Privation is not unreality: the victims' injuries are real and the perpetrators are culpable.

The Reformed difficulty is sharper than a contrast between good creation and free wrongdoing. God ordains everything that occurs, yet the Westminster Confession denies that he authors sin or coerces creaturely wills. Providence reaches sinful actions beyond bare permission, but their sinfulness belongs to the creature.<Cite source="wcf" locator="3.1; 5.4" /> Divine government and creaturely guilt are asserted together, not divided into two independent domains.

Evil is also active. People suppress the truth they know about God, so wrongdoing cannot be reduced to a lack of information.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 1:18–21" /> The norm, the agent's direction and the fallen condition belong together in the biblical account.

## What this explains well

The account explains why great capacities can serve terrible ends: intellect, desire and social power can be corrupted without ceasing to be real gifts. It preserves an objective standard for judging their direction, rather than identifying good with whatever happens to exist. It also refuses an equal contest between ultimate good and ultimate evil. No independent evil principle stands beyond God's government. These strengths depend on Christian premises about God, creation and law; the phenomena of cruelty alone do not prove them.

## Strongest objection

Hume's Philo asks why a world attributed to perfect goodness contains such pervasive suffering. He identifies pain as a motive, government by general laws, creatures' limited endowments and apparent defects in their constitution as sources of misery.<Cite source="hume-dialogues" locator="11.6–11.11" /> His strongest point is evidential: even if evil is logically compatible with a good God, does the world's observed character justify inferring one?<Cite source="hume-dialogues" locator="11.4–11.5; 11.13–11.15" /> The Reformed decree intensifies the question: why would comprehensive divine government include these harms?

## Christian reply

Christianity's answer runs through its central event. At the cross human beings did their worst: an innocent man was betrayed, condemned and executed. Yet Peter says that Jesus was "delivered up according to the definite plan and foreknowledge of God," and that his hearers had used "lawless men" to kill him. The church's prayer adds that Herod, Pilate, the Gentiles and the peoples of Israel gathered "to do whatever your hand and your plan had predestined to take place."<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Acts 2:23; 4:27–28" /> God's purpose was sovereign and the human guilt was real, and neither canceled the other. "If Christ was not crucified by the will of God," Calvin asks, "where is our redemption?" Yet those through whom God carried out his decree are not excused, because they were not obeying his command but violating it.<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="I.18.3–4" /> Joseph's brothers fit the same pattern: they meant harm, and God meant the same event for good.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Genesis 50:20" />

The cross does not explain every evil. It shows that the Christian claim about providence is not an abstract formula, since divine government and human wickedness meet at the center of the faith, and the outcome is redemption rather than mere tragedy. It does not disclose God's sufficient reason for any particular atrocity. The Belgic Confession declines to inquire "with undue curiosity" into what surpasses human understanding and adores God's hidden judgments, and the Westminster Confession handles the decree "with special prudence and care."<Cite source="belgic-confession" locator="Art. 13" /><Cite source="wcf" locator="3.8" />

Nor does evil have the last word. By the cross Christ disarmed the hostile powers and put them to open shame, and Scripture promises a world in which God wipes away every tear and death is no more.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Colossians 2:15; Revelation 21:4" /> Christian confidence that God is good, despite harms it cannot explain, rests on that revealed character and promised end, not on claiming that each loss looks beneficial. Philo's challenge remains a serious dispute about the warrant for that confidence, since the cross and the promise are known by revelation rather than read off the world.

## Deep dive

#### What privation does and does not explain

Augustine's privation account first answers what evil is. Anything that can be damaged has some good whose integrity can be impaired; corruption presupposes a subject rather than an independent evil substance.<Cite source="augustine-enchiridion" locator="11, 13" /> Privation is not a measure of size: a grave deprivation can be devastating precisely because the good destroyed was great. Nor is every painful experience a sin. A victim's injury and an aggressor's culpable will are connected but not identical.

When Augustine considers the origin of an evil will, he calls its cause deficient rather than efficient.<Cite source="augustine-city-of-god" locator="XII.7" /> The phrase protects the distinction between a created capacity and its failure of proper direction. It does not reconstruct the psychology of the first wrongdoing, and by itself it does not settle how that wrongdoing relates to God's decree. <QuestionLink question="fail-the-good" worldview="christianity">Failure to do the good</QuestionLink> treats voluntary disobedience and moral inability; <QuestionLink question="self-deception" worldview="christianity">self-deception</QuestionLink> concerns the distortion of judgment that can accompany it.

#### Government, guilt and unexplained harms

The Westminster Confession's refusal to identify decree with coercion gives the doctrine definite content. God governs events, while the sinner acts through his own will, and the sinfulness proceeds from the creature, which locates the blame.<Cite source="wcf" locator="3.1; 5.4" /> A critic can grant these distinctions and still ask whether they establish God's blamelessness; that is why the reply above answers through the cross rather than by restating them. The fuller dispute about responsibility appears in <QuestionLink question="great-and-terrible" worldview="christianity">the flagship</QuestionLink>.

Bavinck relates the miseries of nature to humanity's fallen condition, yet warns that particular calamities' purposes are rarely discernible.<Cite source="bavinck-reformed-dogmatics-monergism-3" locator="Vol. 3, §39.5, PDF pp. 208–209, 213–215" /> His historical scientific examples do not settle contemporary questions about nature before the Fall. The theological connection stands without a settled chronology of animal suffering, and no particular disaster is identified as a punishment.

#### Why the evidential objection remains

Philo grants a possible compatibility between the world and perfect goodness under assumptions not supplied by observation. He disputes whether observation itself supports that conclusion.<Cite source="hume-dialogues" locator="11.4–11.5" /> Saying that unknown reasons could exist does not meet that challenge. The Reformed answer brings Scripture and the confessed character of God to the interpretation of the world, and a reader who rejects that authority will reasonably ask what warrants it.

Mystery therefore has a limited role. It covers what the confession does not claim to unfold, God's reasons for particular harms. It does not shield the confession's positive distinctions, such as the difference between governing sin and authoring it, which remain open to scrutiny and have to be argued.
