## The Christian view

Every inference from past to future assumes that nature will go on as it has. Scripture grounds that expectation in God's faithfulness. After the flood God promises that "seedtime and harvest, cold and heat, summer and winter, day and night, shall not cease."<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Genesis 8:22" /> He speaks of "my covenant with day and night and the fixed order of heaven and earth."<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Jeremiah 33:25" /> How God governs through real created causes is explained under <QuestionLink question="order" worldview="christianity">order</QuestionLink>.

Augustine noticed that "experience teaches us to infer the future from the past."<Cite source="augustine-christian-doctrine" locator="II.30.47" /> The Christian explanation of why that works is providence. God ordinarily works through means, ordering events "according to the nature of second causes." Yet he "is free to work without, above, and against them, at his pleasure."<Cite source="wcf" locator="5.2–3" /> So the Christian expects *general* regularity, not exceptionless prediction.

Two claims must be kept apart. Scripture directly supports a theological reason to expect regularity: the world is governed by a God whose purposes do not change.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Hebrews 6:17–18" /> It does not claim a formally non-circular proof of induction, and the classical Reformed theologians never addressed Hume's problem as such. That stronger claim belongs to a later apologetic argument (see the Deep dive).

## What this explains well

The account explains why nature is regular and why our expectation of regularity fits it, which Hume himself called "a kind of pre-established harmony."<Cite source="hume-enquiry" locator="5.21" /> It explains both the reliability of science and the possibility of miracle.

## Strongest objection

The theistic answer seems only to move Hume's problem, which is that any argument from experience for the future's resembling the past "must be evidently going in a circle."<Cite source="hume-enquiry" locator="4.19" /> Why expect God's faithfulness to continue tomorrow, unless the future resembles the past? And the Christian learns of God's promises through testimony and memory, which already rely on induction. Naturalists meanwhile have several responses of their own, none of which needs God (see <QuestionLink question="induction" worldview="naturalism">the naturalist answer</QuestionLink>).

## Christian reply

The objection is partly right, and the Christian should grant it. Belief in God's faithfulness does not escape the circle in Hume's sense. It is not, however, an inductive generalization from God's past acts. It is a conviction about what kind of being God is, received from revelation and held as a basic commitment of the Christian framework, as every framework has basic commitments (see <QuestionLink question="ultimate-authority" worldview="christianity">the ultimate authority for truth</QuestionLink>). What theism then offers is an *explanation*: why nature is regular, and why minds like ours are fitted to expect it. Several naturalist answers also go beyond experience, by positing laws or natural necessities. The Christian holds that a faithful personal Creator explains regularity better than a necessity that has no explanation. That is a claim of comparative explanatory adequacy, not a proof that no alternative works.

## Deep dive

#### Van Til's stronger claim

Van Til quoted A. E. Taylor: science cannot demonstrate the uniformity of nature, since "all attempts to produce 'evidence' for the 'uniformity of nature' themselves presuppose the very principle they are intended to prove." Van Til agreed and went further. God's counsel, he argued, "is the only presupposition which can account for the uniformity of nature which the scientist needs." Just as one cannot see the beams under a floor yet knows a floor needs them, God's existence is "required for the uniformity of nature."<Cite source="van-til-defense" locator="p. 120" />

That is a transcendental argument. Its conclusion, that *only* Christian theism can account for uniformity, is stronger than the doctrine of providence. To establish it one would have to show that every rival account fails, including the naturalist responses to Hume. This answer claims less: that providence explains regularity, and that the leading alternatives either accept a circle, add a non-empirical premise or redefine justification, as theism also must.

#### Augustine and the psalmist

Augustine observed that every practical art depends on "connecting the memory of the past with the expectation of the future."<Cite source="augustine-christian-doctrine" locator="II.30.47" /> The psalmist ties the earth's stability to God's faithfulness: "Your faithfulness endures to all generations; you have established the earth, and it stands fast," "for all things are your servants."<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Psalm 119:89–91" />

#### Not a machine, not occasionalism

Reformed theology does not make nature a self-running machine, and it does not deny real created causes. Calvin rejected the idea of a "momentary Creator" who set the world going and left it.<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="I.16.1" /> Turretin held that second causes really act, while depending on God for their being and acting.<Cite source="turretin-institutio-1847" locator="VI.1.5–6" note="Latin; paraphrased" /> Regularity is therefore both creaturely and divine: things act according to the natures God gave them, and God keeps those natures in being.

#### Miracles and regularity

Because God is free to work "above" his ordinary means, the Christian does not expect nature to be exceptionless. But miracles presuppose a stable order against which they stand out as signs. When Moses' staff becomes a serpent and returns to a staff, the sign works only because staffs do not ordinarily do that.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Exodus 4:1–5" /> The doctrine supports the practice of science, which relies on general regularity, without promising that nothing new can ever happen.
