## The Christian view

Knowing the good is not the same as wanting it most. Conscience still accuses and defends our conduct, yet people suppress the truth they know.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 2:14–15; Romans 1:18–21" /> Sin reaches our desires: the mind set on the flesh resists God's law.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 8:7" /> Actual sins flow from a corrupted nature, and the fallen will cannot choose the spiritual good that belongs to salvation.<Cite source="wcf" locator="6.4; 9.3" /> Conscience survives, and not every choice is equally wicked; what is lost is the heart's love for God.

## What this explains well

It distinguishes moral recognition from a heart's governing love. It also accounts for the believer's continuing struggle: in the Reformed reading of Romans 7, a person who delights in God's law still does what he hates.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 7:15–25" /> That reading describes the renewed person's conflict, not the condition of every unbeliever.

## Strongest objection

If our governing desires are corrupted before we choose, how can wrongdoing be our fault? Kant insists that a person's moral character must be his own doing if he is to be responsible.<Cite source="kant-religion-greene-hudson" locator="Bk. I, General Observation" /> Calling a choice voluntary may seem insufficient if one cannot will otherwise.

## Christian reply

Edwards distinguishes lacking the capacity to act from being unwilling because of one's own inclinations. The first can excuse; the second need not, since commands address what we love and choose.<Cite source="edwards-works-hickman-1" locator="Freedom of the Will I.4; III.4" /> Someone who cannot walk because his legs are broken is not to blame; someone who cannot love God because he does not want to is in a different position, because the inability lies in his own will. That is the Reformed sense of moral inability: it is neither bodily incapacity nor coercion. Kant's objection assumes that responsibility requires a power to will against one's own strongest inclination. The Reformed account denies that premise, and the disagreement turns on it.

## Deep dive

#### What inability leaves intact

The inability the Westminster Confession teaches concerns the spiritual good accompanying salvation, not an inability to recognize any duty or perform any useful act. The Confession explicitly allows that unregenerate people can do things God commands which benefit themselves and others, while denying that these works arise from faith or have God's glory as their governing end.<Cite source="wcf" locator="9.3; 16.7" /> Conscience and the remaining image are therefore real, not appearances. Calvin describes reason and will as damaged rather than destroyed, and distinguishes mistaking a particular case from knowingly choosing wrongdoing.<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="II.2.12; II.2.22–23" />

Nor does regeneration remove every contrary desire at once. The Westminster Confession describes a continuing conflict between renewed life and remaining corruption, in which serious lapses are possible.<Cite source="wcf" locator="13.2; 17.3" /> Romans 7, on this reading, shows that conflict in believers: approval of the good and resistance to it can coexist within one person.
