## The Christian view

Knowing the good does not mean wanting it most. Paul describes conscience accusing or defending our conduct, yet also people suppressing truth they know.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 2:14–15; Romans 1:18–21" /> Sin reaches our desires: the mind directed by the flesh resists God's law.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 8:7" /> Westminster summarizes this as a corrupted nature producing sinful acts, with the fallen will unable to choose the spiritual good belonging to salvation.<Cite source="wcf" locator="6.4; 9.3" /> This does not erase conscience or make every choice equally wicked.

## What this explains well

It distinguishes moral recognition from a heart's governing love. It also accounts for the believer's continuing struggle: in the Reformed reading of Romans 7, someone who delights in God's law still does what he hates.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 7:15–25" /> That passage is not simply generalized to every unbeliever.

## Strongest objection

If our governing desires are corrupted before we choose, how can wrongdoing be our fault? Kant insists that a person's moral character must be his own doing if he is to be responsible.<Cite source="kant-religion-greene-hudson" locator="Bk. I, General Observation" /> Calling a choice voluntary may seem insufficient if one cannot will otherwise.

## Christian reply

Edwards distinguishes lacking the capacity to act from being unwilling because of one's own inclinations. The first can excuse; the second need not, since commands address what we love and choose.<Cite source="edwards-works-hickman-1" locator="Freedom of the Will I.4; III.4" /> This explains the Reformed sense of moral inability without equating it with bodily incapacity or coercion. Whether that suffices for responsibility remains the objection's disputed premise.

## Deep dive

#### Inability does not mean no good actions

The confessional claim needs its qualification. Westminster's inability concerns spiritual good accompanying salvation, not an inability to recognize every duty or perform any useful act. It explicitly allows that unregenerate people can do things God commands which benefit themselves and others, while denying that these works arise from faith or have God's glory as their governing end.<Cite source="wcf" locator="9.3; 16.7" /> Conscience and the remaining image are therefore real, not appearances. Calvin describes reason and will as damaged rather than destroyed, and distinguishes mistaking a particular case from knowingly choosing wrongdoing.<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="II.2.12; II.2.22–23" />

Nor does regeneration remove every contrary desire at once. Westminster describes a continuing conflict between renewed life and remaining corruption; serious lapses are possible.<Cite source="wcf" locator="13.2; 17.3" /> On this reading, Romans 7 concerns that conflict in believers, rather than proving that all moral agents have identical motives. Its point here is that approval of the good and resistance to it can coexist within a person.
