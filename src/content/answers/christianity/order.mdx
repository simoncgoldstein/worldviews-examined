## The Christian view

The world is not chaos because it was made by wisdom: "The Lord by wisdom founded the earth."<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Proverbs 3:19" /> The psalmist marvels at how manifold God's works are, all made in wisdom.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Psalm 104:24" /> After the flood God promises that seedtime and harvest, cold and heat, day and night "shall not cease."<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Genesis 8:22" /> Through Jeremiah he even compares his faithfulness to Israel with "my covenant with day and night and the fixed order of heaven and earth."<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Jeremiah 33:25" />

The regularity of nature is God's faithfulness made visible. The same God upholds all things by his word,<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Hebrews 1:3" /> and his care reaches the smallest event: not one sparrow falls "apart from your Father."<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Matthew 10:29" />

This does not mean that God does everything directly and creatures do nothing. The Westminster Confession says that God orders events "according to the nature of second causes, either necessarily, freely, or contingently." In his ordinary providence God makes use of means, yet he is free to work without, above or against them.<Cite source="wcf" locator="5.2–3" /> Fire really burns and seeds really grow, by powers God gave them and sustains. Augustine sees God's hand in the order of the smallest things: from God come every mode, species and order, "measure, number, weight," down to the leaf of a tree.<Cite source="augustine-city-of-god" locator="V.11" />

## What this explains well

The account explains why nature is both regular and intelligible: its order comes from a mind, and the human mind was made to understand it. The regularity can be relied on without being self-sufficient, which fits the practice of science without making laws the ultimate reality.

## Strongest objection

Hume argued that we never observe any necessary connection between cause and effect. Events "seem conjoined, but never connected."<Cite source="hume-enquiry" locator="7.26" /> Regularity is simply what we find, and the hidden springs behind it are closed to us.<Cite source="hume-enquiry" locator="4.12" /> Hume's Philo adds that matter in motion could settle into a self-maintaining order without design.<Cite source="hume-dialogues" locator="8.6" /> Sean Carroll warns against inferring "a legislative body or a law-enforcement agency" from the reliability of laws.<Cite source="carroll-something-rather-than-nothing" locator="§5" /> On this view order needs no lawgiver.

## Christian reply

The Christian does not argue that laws need enforcement the way a city needs police, but that the world's order expresses God's faithfulness. Hume's own point supports the need for such a ground: if we never see why the regularity holds, then its continuation is, for the Humean, simply a fact with no explanation. Calvin rejects the picture of a "momentary Creator" who set nature going and left it. God governs every part by a special providence, not by a general motion or "a perpetual law of nature" running on its own.<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="I.16.1–3" /> Turretin adds that second causes depend on God both for their being and for their acting.<Cite source="turretin-institutio-1847" locator="VI.1.5–6" /> The Christian offers an explanation where the Humean offers none. That is a comparative claim, not a proof.

## Deep dive

#### Calvin and Bavinck: two emphases

Calvin stresses that God directs every event by a "special providence," down to the sparrow, against the idea that God merely keeps a machine running.<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="I.16.1–3" /> Bavinck stresses the other side. Because there is one Creator, there is a unity of world-plan and of the order of the whole of nature. Scripture recognizes ordinances and laws for created things, and miracles presuppose that fixed order rather than abolish it.<Cite source="bavinck-reformed-dogmatics-monergism-3" locator="Vol. 3, §35.5, PDF p. 37" /> God governs things according to the powers and laws he has implanted in them.<Cite source="bavinck-reformed-dogmatics-monergism-2" locator="Vol. 2, §29.12, PDF pp. 519–520" /> The Westminster Confession holds both: God governs everything, and he does it ordinarily through means.<Cite source="wcf" locator="5.1–3" />

#### Not occasionalism

Reformed theology is not occasionalist. It does not hold that created things lack causal power and that God alone produces every effect. Turretin describes providence as God's knowledge, decree and execution. Second causes really act, but they depend on God in their being and acting, and contingency is not removed.<Cite source="turretin-institutio-1847" locator="VI.1.2, 5–6, 9" /> What looks disordered to us, he adds, is not confused in itself.<Cite source="turretin-institutio-1847" locator="VI.1.11" />

#### Order as an argument

Turretin also argues from order to God: order requires wisdom, and the old idea that atoms fell together into a world by chance cannot account for it.<Cite source="turretin-institutio-1847" locator="III.1.10–11" /> Augustine says that the well-ordered changes and movements of the world testify that it was made.<Cite source="augustine-city-of-god" locator="XI.4" /> The confessions keep this pastoral. The Belgic Confession says God did not abandon creatures to "chance or fortune,"<Cite source="belgic-confession" locator="Art. 13" /> and the Heidelberg Catechism says that all things come to us "not by chance but by his fatherly hand."<Cite source="heidelberg-catechism" locator="Q&A 27" />
