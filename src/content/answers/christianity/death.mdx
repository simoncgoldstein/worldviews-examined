## The Christian view

Death entered the human world through Adam's sin. It is the wages of sin, set against God's gift of eternal life.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 5:12; 6:23" /> The return to dust was part of the judgment on disobedience, and death is an enemy, not merely a stage of biological development.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Genesis 3:19; 1 Corinthians 15:26" /> Mortality belongs to the misery of fallen humanity.<Cite source="wcf" locator="6.6" />

This is a claim about the human condition, not a verdict that an early death reveals a person's exceptional wickedness. Nor does Christian comfort make the loss of embodied life harmless. Augustine distinguishes death's evil from the good use to which it may be turned: the faithful can die well although death itself remains a punishment within the fallen order.<Cite source="augustine-city-of-god" locator="XIII.1, 4–6" />

Christianity does not merely interpret death. It claims that death has been invaded and defeated. The Son shared our flesh and blood so that "through death he might destroy the one who has the power of death," and set free those enslaved all their lives by the fear of it.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Hebrews 2:14–15" /> Christ Jesus "abolished death and brought life and immortality to light."<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="2 Timothy 1:10" /> Death's sting is sin, and the law gives sin its power. Because Christ bore sin and answered the law's judgment (see <QuestionLink question="guilt" worldview="christianity">guilt</QuestionLink>), Paul can taunt the enemy: "O death, where is your victory? O death, where is your sting?"<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="1 Corinthians 15:54–57" /> At Lazarus's grave Jesus promised that whoever believes in him will live, even though he dies.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="John 11:25–26" /> The enemy is defeated but not yet destroyed. Believers still die, and death, the last enemy, is destroyed only when the dead are raised.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="1 Corinthians 15:26, 54" />

## What this explains well

This explains why death can appear both ordinary and profoundly wrong. A phenomenon's regular occurrence does not establish its moral or ultimate fitness. Bavinck connects mortality with human materiality while resisting the inference that being formed from dust makes death simply the creature's proper destiny.<Cite source="bavinck-reformed-dogmatics-monergism-3" locator="Vol. 3, §39.6, PDF pp. 218–221" /> The account can acknowledge bodily vulnerability and grief while placing them within a larger judgment on humanity.

## Strongest objection

Evolutionary accounts explain age-related decline through the changing strength of natural selection. They do not need an ancestral moral transgression to explain senescence.<Cite source="flatt-partridge-evolution-aging" locator="Why does aging evolve?; Key lessons from the evolution of aging" /> Why add a theological sentence to a biological process? And why should forgiven people still die if death is sin's penalty?

## Christian reply

The Christian distinguishes a biological mechanism from the theological status of human death. Biology's account of aging does not independently decide whether mortality is ultimately fitting. Why, then, do the forgiven still die? The Heidelberg Catechism answers that their death no longer pays for sin; it ends their sinning and is their entrance into eternal life.<Cite source="heidelberg-catechism" locator="Q&A 42" /> That changed meaning rests on Christ's own death and resurrection: the believer's death is not a payment because Christ has paid, and it leads to life because he has risen as "the firstfruits."<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="1 Corinthians 15:20" /> This is an expressly Christian claim, grounded in what Christianity says happened at Easter, not a conclusion drawn from senescence. The historical case for the resurrection is weighed in <QuestionLink question="jesus" worldview="christianity">who Jesus is</QuestionLink>.

## Deep dive

#### Cause, character and comfort

Three questions belong together without becoming identical. What processes bring a particular organism's life to an end? What is death's place in the human condition? What should the dying person's hope be? The Reformed answer to the second and third does not replace investigations of the first. Bavinck's dated biological examples are not scientific evidence; what endures is his distinction between material existence and the necessity or fittingness of death.

Augustine considers why the forgiven still undergo bodily death, and separates the endurance of that event from the removal of guilt.<Cite source="augustine-city-of-god" locator="XIII.4–6" /> The Heidelberg Catechism makes the altered meaning explicit. Christian hope neither spares the faithful bereavement nor treats fear at the approach of death as unbelief. Calvin distinguishes nature's dread from faith's desire for the promised life.<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="III.9.4–5" /> Those perspectives can explain the believer's mixed response without celebrating death's destructive power.

The Westminster Confession also keeps the body within the account: bodies return to dust, and they will be raised.<Cite source="wcf" locator="32.1–2" /> In the new creation "death shall be no more."<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Revelation 21:4" /> The hope is therefore more than the escape of an immaterial part from an otherwise irrelevant shell. The state after death is treated in <QuestionLink question="after-death" worldview="christianity">the after-death question</QuestionLink>.

The difficult relation between divine government and the world's harms is treated in <QuestionLink question="evil" worldview="christianity">the evil anchor</QuestionLink>. How adversity may be borne belongs to <QuestionLink question="suffering" worldview="christianity">suffering</QuestionLink>. Neither answer licenses identifying the divine purpose of a particular bereavement.
