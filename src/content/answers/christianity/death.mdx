## The Christian view

Romans connects death's entry into the human world with Adam's sin, and contrasts sin's wages with God's gift.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 5:12; 6:23" /> Genesis depicts the return to dust within the judgment on disobedience; Paul nevertheless calls death an enemy, not merely an inconvenient stage of biological development.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Genesis 3:19; 1 Corinthians 15:26" /> The Reformed confessions accordingly treat mortality as part of fallen humanity's misery.<Cite source="wcf" locator="6.6" />

That claim concerns the human condition. It does not say that every early death reveals a particular person's exceptional wickedness. Nor does Christian comfort require treating the loss of embodied life as harmless. Augustine distinguishes death's evil from the good use to which it may be turned: the faithful can die well although death itself remains a punishment within the fallen order.<Cite source="augustine-city-of-god" locator="XIII.1, 4–6" />

Christianity does not merely interpret death. It claims that death has been invaded and defeated. The Son shared our flesh and blood so that "through death he might destroy the one who has the power of death," and set free those enslaved all their lives by the fear of it.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Hebrews 2:14–15" /> Christ Jesus "abolished death and brought life and immortality to light."<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="2 Timothy 1:10" /> Death's sting is sin, and the law gives sin its power. Because Christ bore sin and answered the law's judgment (see <QuestionLink question="guilt" worldview="christianity">guilt</QuestionLink>), Paul can taunt the enemy: "O death, where is your victory? O death, where is your sting?"<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="1 Corinthians 15:54–57" /> At Lazarus's grave Jesus promised that whoever believes in him will live, even though he dies.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="John 11:25–26" /> The enemy is defeated but not yet destroyed. Believers still die, and death, the last enemy, is destroyed only when the dead are raised.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="1 Corinthians 15:26, 54" />

## What this explains well

This explains why death can appear both ordinary and profoundly wrong. A phenomenon's regular occurrence does not establish its moral or ultimate fitness. Bavinck connects mortality with human materiality while resisting the inference that being formed from dust makes death simply the creature's proper destiny.<Cite source="bavinck-reformed-dogmatics-monergism-3" locator="Vol. 3, §39.6, PDF pp. 218–221" /> The account can acknowledge bodily vulnerability and grief while placing them within a larger judgment on humanity.

## Strongest objection

Evolutionary accounts explain age-related decline through the changing strength of natural selection. They do not need an ancestral moral transgression to explain senescence.<Cite source="flatt-partridge-evolution-aging" locator="Why does aging evolve?; Key lessons from the evolution of aging" /> Why add a theological sentence to a biological process? And why should forgiven people still die if death is sin's penalty?

## Christian reply

The Christian distinguishes a biological mechanism from the theological status of human death. Biology's account of aging does not independently decide whether mortality is ultimately fitting. Why, then, do the forgiven still die? The Heidelberg Catechism answers that their death no longer pays for sin; it ends their sinning and is their entrance into eternal life.<Cite source="heidelberg-catechism" locator="Q&A 42" /> That changed meaning rests on Christ's own death and resurrection: the believer's death is not a payment because Christ has paid, and it leads to life because he has risen as "the firstfruits."<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="1 Corinthians 15:20" /> This is an expressly Christian claim, grounded in what Christianity says happened at Easter, not a conclusion drawn from senescence. The historical case for the resurrection is weighed in <QuestionLink question="jesus" worldview="christianity">who Jesus is</QuestionLink>.

## Deep dive

#### Cause, character and comfort

Three questions belong together without becoming identical. What processes bring a particular organism's life to an end? What is death's place in the human condition? What should the dying person's hope be? The Reformed answer to the second and third does not replace investigations of the first. Bavinck's historical biological examples should therefore not become current scientific evidence; the enduring claim here is his distinction between material existence and the necessity or fittingness of death.

Augustine considers why the forgiven still undergo bodily death, and separates the endurance of that event from the removal of guilt.<Cite source="augustine-city-of-god" locator="XIII.4–6" /> Heidelberg makes the altered meaning explicit. Christian hope does not promise that faithful people avoid bereavement or that fear at the approach of death proves unbelief. Calvin distinguishes nature's dread from faith's desire for the promised life.<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="III.9.4–5" /> Those perspectives can explain the believer's mixed response without celebrating death's destructive power.

The Westminster Confession also keeps the body within the account: bodies return to dust, and they will be raised.<Cite source="wcf" locator="32.1–2" /> In the new creation "death shall be no more."<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Revelation 21:4" /> The hope is therefore more than the assertion that some immaterial part escapes an otherwise irrelevant shell. Further claims about the state after death belong to <QuestionLink question="after-death" worldview="christianity">the after-death question</QuestionLink>; the present answer explains why death occurs and why it remains an enemy.

The difficult relation between divine government and the world's harms is treated in <QuestionLink question="evil" worldview="christianity">the evil anchor</QuestionLink>. Neither answer licenses identifying the divine purpose of a particular bereavement.
