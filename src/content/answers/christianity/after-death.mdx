## The Christian view

Christian hope distinguishes what follows death immediately from what happens at the last day. Paul desires to depart and be with Christ, and speaks of being away from the body and at home with the Lord.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Philippians 1:21–23; 2 Corinthians 5:1–10" /> Reformed theology therefore affirms conscious presence with Christ for believers while their bodies await resurrection. Death does not create a new person who merely resembles the one who lived.

Bodily resurrection completes that hope. Paul describes continuity and transformation: the perishable body is raised imperishable, with glory and power replacing weakness. A spiritual body is still a body, transformed through God's action rather than discarded as an irrelevant shell.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="1 Corinthians 15:35–58" /> Westminster calls it the same body with different qualities, reunited with the soul.<Cite source="wcf" locator="32.1–3" />

Judgment belongs to the account as well. Jesus speaks of resurrection to life and resurrection to judgment; Westminster distinguishes the blessedness of the righteous from punishment of the wicked.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="John 5:28–29" /><Cite source="wcf" locator="33.1–2" /> The confession rejects both unconscious soul-sleep and a further purgatorial destination for separated souls. Its claim is a Christian promise and judgment, not an inference from ordinary observation of dying bodies.

## What this explains well

This keeps the person's embodied life within the promise while offering comfort before its final completion. Present fellowship with Christ does not make resurrection redundant. Nor does final transformation require treating the body as a prison from which the real person ought to escape. Heidelberg makes the sequence explicit: immediate reception by Christ and the future raising of one's own flesh.<Cite source="heidelberg-catechism" locator="Q&A 57" />

## Strongest objection

Russell's mortalism interprets death as the end of the person rather than an entrance into another state.<Cite source="russell-what-i-believe" locator="ch. 1" /> A critic can therefore ask what warrants conscious survival or resurrection when the familiar embodied life has ended. Declaring the same person raised states the identity claim, but does not yet demonstrate it. The further moral difficulty is why survival should include everlasting punishment.

## Christian reply

The Christian rests the promise on Christ rather than bare speculation about an indestructible soul. Paul makes future resurrection depend on Christ's resurrection, and acknowledges that Christian hope fails if that central claim is false.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="1 Corinthians 15:12–23" /> This identifies the account's evidential burden. It does not settle it by wishful thinking, or supply detailed knowledge of every feature of separated existence.

## Deep dive

#### An intermediate state is not the final good

Westminster describes the righteous as perfected in holiness and received into heaven, yet still awaiting the full redemption of their bodies.<Cite source="wcf" locator="32.1" /> The two affirmations belong together. The intermediate state is blessed because of fellowship with Christ, but the human creature's ultimate completion is embodied. Paul himself describes longing for further clothing rather than merely being unclothed.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="2 Corinthians 5:1–5" />

Bavinck warns how easily speculation enters discussions of separated souls. Human activity is intimately connected with the body; that makes the manner of disembodied life hard to conceive. His account gives priority to life in communion with God over mere continued existence.<Cite source="bavinck-reformed-dogmatics-monergism-4" locator="Vol. 4, §54.7–8, PDF pp. 473–476" /> The distinction supports modesty about what the state is like without surrendering the scriptural claim that the believer is with Christ.

#### Identity, transformation and accountability

Resurrection is not simply the recovery of the organism in its present frailty. Paul's seed comparison joins continuity to a divinely given transformed condition; the same life is neither left untouched nor replaced by another individual.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="1 Corinthians 15:35–49" /> Westminster's formulation summarizes that commitment. It does not specify a material reconstruction procedure or require every particle present during earthly life to be recovered.

Judgment also concerns this person's life, rather than a successor paying for someone else's deeds.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="2 Corinthians 5:10" /> The Christian must face the severity of that claim, including its account of punishment. The separate questions are whether resurrection is warranted, whether Christ's judgment is righteous, and what final blessedness consists in. They should not be answered by describing heaven as a vague continuation of everything one currently desires.
