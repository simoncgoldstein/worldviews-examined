## The Christian view

The human problem is both guilt and corruption. People have sinned against God and need a righteous standing they cannot create by improving their record; fallen desire also resists the very good it ought to seek. Paul joins universal sin to justification through Christ, and describes sinners as spiritually dead, made alive by God's mercy.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 3:21–26; Ephesians 2:1–10" /> Adam's disobedience and Christ's obedience also stand in a representative relation.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 5:12–19" /> This diagnosis does not mean every person is as vicious as possible, or that unbelievers never benefit their neighbors.

The remedy begins with Christ's work, not with a technique for escaping guilt. His obedience, death and resurrection supply reconciliation; Hebrews depicts him offering himself and securing redemption. Believers receive his righteousness rather than making repentance or improved conduct the price of acceptance.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Hebrews 9:11–15; Romans 4:1–8" /> Union with Christ also brings a new relation to sin: Paul connects sharing in his death and resurrection with walking in newness of life.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 6:1–14" />

The Spirit applies that remedy through calling and regeneration. Christ speaks of the Father drawing people to him and of the new birth; Westminster describes the will renewed so that people come freely, made willing by grace.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="John 3:1–8; 6:37–44" /><Cite source="wcf" locator="10.1–2" /> Faith truly receives Christ and repentance truly turns from sin. The phrase “by grace you have been saved through faith” places dependence where it belongs; the same passage makes good works the result and purpose of God's new creation.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Ephesians 2:8–10" />

Justification and sanctification must remain distinct. The first is pardon and acceptance through Christ; the second is actual renewal, imperfect in this life. Perseverance depends on God's preservation, and assurance rests on promises, the Spirit's witness and the fruits of grace; it can be shaken without making faith unreal.<Cite source="wcf" locator="11.1–3; 13.1–3; 17.1–3; 18.1–4" /> The final state is glorification: embodied life conformed to Christ in God's renewed creation.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 8:18–30" />

## What this explains well

The remedy matches the twofold diagnosis. Pardon alone would leave corruption unchanged; moral improvement alone would not supply the ground of acceptance. Grace answers inability without making the person inactive: Paul commands believers to work while identifying God as the one working in their willing and action.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Philippians 2:12–13" /> A secure ground and an unfinished transformation can therefore coexist without being confused.

## Strongest objection

If moral responsibility requires that people can become better, why affirm a corruption from which they cannot free themselves? Kant's demand connects obligation with possible moral renewal.<Cite source="kant-religion-greene-hudson" locator="Bk. I, General Observation" /> The Reformed difficulty goes further: if effectual grace is necessary and not given equally, why does one person's unbelief warrant condemnation while another's faith is entirely a gift? The objection challenges both responsibility and the distribution of saving mercy, rather than pretending the doctrine denies all human action.

## Christian reply

The Reformed reply distinguishes moral unwillingness from external compulsion. Calvin and Edwards argue that acting voluntarily from a disordered love remains responsible even when spiritual renewal requires grace.<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="II.3.5" /><Cite source="edwards-works-hickman-1" locator="Freedom of the Will, I.4" /> Dort insists that renewal does not treat people as stones but restores their willing action.<Cite source="canons-of-dort" locator="III/IV.16" /> This addresses agency; it does not remove every moral question about election. The account rests on God's just judgment and free mercy, not on a proof that grace is owed equally.

## Deep dive

#### A remedy that changes standing and life

The Reformed diagnosis is not simply that people lack useful information. Romans describes resistance to God's law, while Ephesians speaks of life given to those dead in trespasses.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 8:7–8; Ephesians 2:1–5" /> Education and civil improvement can be real goods without constituting spiritual reconciliation. Westminster expressly recognizes works by the unregenerate that benefit themselves and others, while denying that they supply salvation's ground.<Cite source="wcf" locator="16.7" /> The doctrine should not be inflated into the claim that the unconverted cannot reason, love their children or perform useful acts.

Justification likewise does not rename an inwardly perfected person righteous. It rests on Christ's obedience and satisfaction, received by faith; faith is the instrument, not another meritorious achievement.<Cite source="wcf" locator="11.1–3" /> Sanctification concerns a different benefit, renewal of the actual person. The confessional distinction keeps the two benefits together without making the incomplete one undermine the security of the other.

#### How grace uses means

Monergistic regeneration means that God supplies spiritual life, not that the believer never acts. Dort describes the will as renewed and active, and rejects separating divine renewal from the gospel and the appointed means.<Cite source="canons-of-dort" locator="III/IV.12–14, 16–17" /> Westminster similarly connects saving faith with the Word and its strengthening through prayer and the sacraments.<Cite source="wcf" locator="14.1–2" /> A call to repent or believe is therefore not at odds with dependence on God.

Bavinck emphasizes the same continuing dependence: spiritual life is not only from God at its beginning but sustained in communion with Christ through the Spirit and Word.<Cite source="bavinck-reformed-dogmatics-monergism-3" locator="Vol. 3, §45.1, PDF pp. 619–620" /> Grace is not a one-time boost after which the autonomous individual completes the task. Nor is the Spirit's action a reason to abandon discipline, prayer or moral effort.

#### Preservation and the knowledge of it

Christ's promise that his sheep will not be snatched from his hand grounds confidence in preservation.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="John 10:27–30" /> That promise is distinct from every believer's uninterrupted feeling of confidence. Westminster allows long struggle before full assurance and describes negligence, sin and temptation as occasions on which assurance may diminish.<Cite source="wcf" locator="18.3–4" /> The Christian should neither make anxiety the proof of condemnation nor encourage certainty detached from repentance.

The strongest objection still matters: describing inability as moral does not independently establish that unequal effectual grace is righteous. The reply requires the broader Christian account of God and judgment. It should be offered as that account's defense, not as a neutral demonstration every critic must accept.

Assurance also concerns truth, not only confidence. Westminster distinguishes the false hopes of those who presume from the comfort available to those who truly trust Christ and seek to walk before him.<Cite source="wcf" locator="18.1–2" /> The same standard can affirm secure preservation and warn against self-deception. A reassuring religious identity or a past emotional experience does not replace the promise's object or the present reality of faith and repentance.
