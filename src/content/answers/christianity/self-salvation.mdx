## The Christian view

The human problem is both guilt and corruption. People have sinned against God and need a righteous standing they cannot create by improving their record; fallen desire also resists the very good it ought to seek. All have sinned, and they are justified freely through Christ; sinners are spiritually dead, and God in his mercy makes them alive.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 3:21–26; Ephesians 2:1–10" /> Adam's disobedience and Christ's obedience also stand in a representative relation.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 5:12–19" />

The remedy begins with what Christ has done, not with a technique for escaping guilt. By his obedient life and his death in the place of sinners he answered God's justice and reconciled his people to God; that work is finished, and <QuestionLink question="guilt" worldview="christianity">the Christian account of guilt</QuestionLink> explains why it is just.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 5:8–10; Hebrews 9:11–15" /> What Christ accomplished must also become ours. As long as we remain separated from him, Calvin says, nothing he suffered and did benefits us; the Holy Spirit is the bond that unites us to him.<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="III.1.1" /> In that union God gives a "twofold benefit." Reconciled by Christ's righteousness, believers find in God "instead of a judge, an indulgent Father"; sanctified by his Spirit, they begin to pursue a holy life.<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="III.11.1" /> The two come together without being confused. Believers share Christ's death and resurrection and so walk in newness of life, yet their acceptance rests on his righteousness, not on that newness.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 4:1–8; 6:1–14" />

The Spirit applies that remedy through calling and regeneration. Christ speaks of the Father drawing people to him and of the new birth.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="John 3:1–8; 6:37–44" /> God renews the will, so that people come to Christ "most freely, being made willing by his grace."<Cite source="wcf" locator="10.1–2" /> Faith truly receives Christ and repentance truly turns from sin. The phrase “by grace you have been saved through faith” places dependence where it belongs; the same passage makes good works the result and purpose of God's new creation.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Ephesians 2:8–10" />

Justification and sanctification must remain distinct. Justification is God's verdict: pardon and acceptance for Christ's sake, complete from the first.<Cite source="wcf" locator="11.1, 5" /> Sanctification is actual renewal of the whole person, real but imperfect in this life.<Cite source="wcf" locator="13.1–2" /> Calvin calls justification "the principal ground on which religion must be supported," because a person who does not know where he stands before God has no foundation for piety.<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="III.11.1" /> Those whom God accepts he also keeps. Perseverance rests on his preservation, not the believer's strength, and assurance can be shaken without faith becoming unreal.<Cite source="wcf" locator="17.2; 18.4" /> The final state is glorification: embodied life conformed to Christ in God's renewed creation.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 8:18–30" />

## What this explains well

The remedy matches the twofold diagnosis. Pardon alone would leave corruption unchanged; moral improvement alone would not supply the ground of acceptance. Grace answers inability without making the person inactive: Paul commands believers to work while identifying God as the one working in their willing and action.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Philippians 2:12–13" /> A secure ground and an unfinished transformation can therefore coexist without being confused.

## Strongest objection

If moral responsibility requires that people can become better, why affirm a corruption from which they cannot free themselves? Kant's demand connects obligation with possible moral renewal.<Cite source="kant-religion-greene-hudson" locator="Bk. I, General Observation" /> The Reformed difficulty goes further: if effectual grace is necessary and not given equally, why does one person's unbelief warrant condemnation while another's faith is entirely a gift? The objection presses both responsibility and the distribution of saving mercy.

## Christian reply

The Reformed reply distinguishes moral unwillingness from external compulsion. Calvin and Edwards argue that acting voluntarily from a disordered love remains responsible even when spiritual renewal requires grace.<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="II.3.5" /><Cite source="edwards-works-hickman-1" locator="Freedom of the Will, I.4" /> Grace does not work on people as on stones; as the Canons of Dort teach, it restores their willing action.<Cite source="canons-of-dort" locator="III/IV.16" /> The question of distribution also has a direct answer. No one is condemned for lacking grace. Those whom God passes by are ordained to wrath "for their sin, to the praise of his glorious justice,"<Cite source="wcf" locator="3.7" /> while those he saves receive mercy, which by its nature is not owed. No one therefore receives injustice, though some receive a mercy others do not. That answer rests on the Christian account of God's just judgment and free mercy. It does not remove the weight of the question, and it is offered as that account's defense, not as a neutral demonstration every critic must accept.

## Deep dive

#### A remedy that changes standing and life

The Reformed diagnosis is not simply that people lack useful information. The mind set on the flesh resists God's law, and those dead in trespasses must be given life.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 8:7–8; Ephesians 2:1–5" /> Education and civil improvement can be real goods without constituting spiritual reconciliation. The Westminster Confession recognizes that the unregenerate do works that benefit themselves and others, while denying that such works supply salvation's ground.<Cite source="wcf" locator="16.7" /> The doctrine does not claim that the unconverted cannot reason, love their children or perform useful acts.

Justification likewise does not rename an inwardly perfected person righteous. It rests on Christ's obedience and satisfaction, received by faith; faith is the instrument, not another meritorious achievement.<Cite source="wcf" locator="11.1–3" /> Sanctification concerns a different benefit, renewal of the actual person. The confessional distinction keeps the two benefits together without making the incomplete one undermine the security of the other.

#### How grace uses means

Monergistic regeneration means that God supplies spiritual life, not that the believer never acts. The renewed will is active, and God renews it through the gospel and the means he has appointed, not apart from them.<Cite source="canons-of-dort" locator="III/IV.12–14, 16–17" /> Saving faith is ordinarily worked by the Word and strengthened by the sacraments and prayer.<Cite source="wcf" locator="14.1–2" /> A call to repent or believe is therefore not at odds with dependence on God.

Bavinck emphasizes the same continuing dependence: spiritual life is not only from God at its beginning but sustained in communion with Christ through the Spirit and Word.<Cite source="bavinck-reformed-dogmatics-monergism-3" locator="Vol. 3, §45.1, PDF pp. 619–620" /> Grace is not a one-time boost after which the autonomous individual completes the task. Nor is the Spirit's action a reason to abandon discipline, prayer or moral effort.

#### Preservation and the knowledge of it

Christ's promise that his sheep will not be snatched from his hand grounds confidence in preservation.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="John 10:27–30" /> That promise is distinct from every believer's uninterrupted feeling of confidence. Full assurance may come only after long struggle, and negligence, sin and temptation can diminish it.<Cite source="wcf" locator="18.3–4" /> Anxiety is not proof of condemnation, and certainty detached from repentance is not assurance.

Assurance also concerns truth, not only confidence. The false hopes of those who presume differ from the comfort given to those who truly trust Christ and seek to walk before him.<Cite source="wcf" locator="18.1–2" /> The same standard can affirm secure preservation and warn against self-deception. A reassuring religious identity or a past emotional experience does not replace the promise's object or the present reality of faith and repentance.
