## The Christian view

Scripture first calls the human being a creature: formed from the dust and made alive by God's breath.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Genesis 2:7" /> Humanity shares earth and breath with the animals, yet is set apart by God's decision: he made humankind in his image, male and female, blessed them and gave them dominion.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Genesis 1:26–28" /> The same creature is small before the heavens yet crowned with glory,<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Psalm 8:3–8" /> and every nation, from one man, is God's offspring.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Acts 17:24–29" />

The person is one, yet not simple. At death the dust returns to the earth and the spirit to God,<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Ecclesiastes 12:7" /> and Jesus distinguishes those who can kill the body from the God who can destroy both soul and body.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Matthew 10:28" /> The Westminster Confession says God created humans "with reasonable and immortal souls," bearing his image in knowledge, righteousness and holiness.<Cite source="wcf" locator="4.2" /> Yet the soul is not a tenant lodged in the body. Body and soul together make one person, and death tears apart what God joined. Believers who die are with the Lord, yet their state is incomplete: they wait for "the full redemption of their bodies."<Cite source="wcf" locator="32.1" /> The hope is not escape from the body but its resurrection.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="1 Corinthians 15:42–49" />

To be made in God's image is to be a creature fitted to know God, to reflect his goodness, and to represent his rule on earth. The image is not a share in God's being, and it is not a likeness of bodily shape, since God has no body. It shows itself in the soul's understanding and will, in the original uprightness in which humanity was made, and in dominion over the creatures.<Cite source="turretin-institutio-1847" locator="V.10.2–6" note="Latin; paraphrased" /> The body is not left out. Calvin, who places the image's chief seat in the soul, insists that rays of the same glory shine in the body.<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="I.15.3" /> Bavinck draws the conclusion most sharply: man does not merely bear or contain God's image; he *is* that image, and the whole human being, soul and body, displays it.<Cite source="bavinck-reformed-dogmatics-monergism-2" locator="Vol. 2, §33.8, 33.10, PDF pp. 659, 669–671" />

## What this explains well

The account holds continuity and distinctiveness together: humans are dust, kin to the creatures they rule, yet answerable to God as no other creature is. It grounds equal worth in what God made rather than in measured ability, so dignity does not rise and fall with intelligence, health or age. After the Fall, murder and cursing are still forbidden because the victim is made in God's likeness.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Genesis 9:6; James 3:9" /> Embodiment and sexual difference belong to the design, not to the soul's housing. And the image can be both permanent and renewed: the creature remains, while its moral likeness to God is restored in Christ.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Ephesians 4:24; Colossians 3:10" />

## Strongest objection

The naturalist objection is that both soul and image are now explanatorily idle. Russell notes that thinking depends on the organization of the brain, that a chemical deficiency can wreck a clever person's thinking, and that the soul is no single persisting entity but a history of events; a separate soul he calls a metaphysical superstition. Because death destroys the brain's organization, he concludes, an individual's thinking cannot be supposed to survive it.<Cite source="russell-what-i-believe" locator="ch. 1" /> Darwin concluded that man, for all his noble qualities, still bears in his bodily frame the "indelible stamp of his lowly origin."<Cite source="darwin-descent" locator="ch. XXI, p. 619" /> If mind varies with the brain and human capacities differ from animal ones by degree, a soul adds nothing, and the "image" names a status that biology cannot find.

## Christian reply

The objection is strongest against a mind loosely lodged in a body, which Reformed theology does not teach. For Bavinck the whole human being is the image,<Cite source="bavinck-reformed-dogmatics-monergism-2" locator="Vol. 2, §33.10, PDF pp. 669–671" /> so an embodied creature whose thinking, in this life, depends on its brain is what the doctrine expects. Correlation between mind and brain is evidence of their union; whether it shows their identity is a philosophical judgment, not an experimental result. Russell's further step is harder to meet. The tradition holds that the soul lives on between death and resurrection,<Cite source="wcf" locator="32.1" /> and no study of the living brain can confirm that; the Christian holds it on the authority of revelation and the hope of resurrection, not as a conclusion from neuroscience. What the evidence of dependence does not show is that a person is nothing more than the organ of present thought.

Nor does the doctrine rest the image on a gap in capacities. Genesis affirms continuity with the earth in the same breath as distinctiveness, and grounds the difference in God's purpose, blessing and commission.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Genesis 1:26–28; 2:7" /> Faculties belong to the image, but what unifies it is a relation to God, not a trait biology must detect. The case rests on revelation, not on neutral premises. Adam's place as the first man and covenant head, and modern dissent from that confessional position, are discussed in <QuestionLink question="great-and-terrible" worldview="christianity">the flagship question</QuestionLink>.

## Deep dive

#### The broader and the narrower image

Scripture speaks of the image in two ways. Some texts assume an image that every human being still bears, so that murder and cursing remain assaults on it;<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Genesis 9:6; James 3:9" /> others speak of an image that must be renewed in Christ.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Ephesians 4:24; Colossians 3:10" /> Reformed theologians, Turretin among the most precise, name the difference with a distinction Scripture does not itself label. In the broader sense the image is the human nature itself, with reason, will and dominion; in the narrower sense it is the original knowledge, righteousness and holiness in which that nature was made. The first remains after the Fall; the second was lost.<Cite source="turretin-institutio-1847" locator="V.10.7" note="Latin; paraphrased" /> Calvin likewise calls the fallen image not wholly destroyed but deformed, and says its true nature is best known from its renewal in Christ, by Paul's words about knowledge, righteousness and holiness.<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="I.15.4" /> The Belgic Confession puts the same point confessionally: fallen humans lost their excellent gifts but kept traces of them, enough to leave them without excuse.<Cite source="belgic-confession" locator="Art. 14" /> The distinction explains how the dignity of every human can be undiminished while the moral likeness has to be restored.

#### Body, soul and identity

The Larger Catechism describes the man's body formed from the dust and both man and woman endued with living, reasonable and immortal souls.<Cite source="westminster-larger-catechism" locator="Q. 17" /> Two claims are being held together. The soul is not produced by the body, and it survives death; yet the person is not the soul alone. What persists through death and resurrection is the same embodied creature, raised with the same body, not a replacement.<Cite source="wcf" locator="32.1–2" /> Augustine sees God's work even in generation: God gives the rational soul and forms the body, and the arts and inventions of the race spring from that endowment even under the curse.<Cite source="augustine-city-of-god" locator="XXII.24" /> How the soul exists between death and resurrection belongs to <QuestionLink question="after-death" worldview="christianity">the after-death question</QuestionLink>.

#### Prophet, priest and king

Bavinck opens his treatment by saying that the essence of man is to be God's image, and he gathers the biblical texts around that claim.<Cite source="bavinck-reformed-dogmatics-monergism-2" locator="Vol. 2, §33.1, PDF p. 626" /> He describes humanity as a microcosm, joining the visible and the invisible worlds, and assigns to man the threefold office of prophet, priest and king.<Cite source="bavinck-reformed-dogmatics-monergism-2" locator="Vol. 2, §33.10, PDF pp. 669–671" /> The image therefore describes a vocation as well as a nature. Bavinck adds that it is fully unfolded only in humanity as a whole, one organism across its generations, not in any single individual.<Cite source="bavinck-reformed-dogmatics-monergism-2" locator="Vol. 2, §34.4–5, PDF pp. 690–693" />

#### Male and female

The image is given to humanity as male and female together.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Genesis 1:27" /> Bavinck treats marriage, household and the cultural task as grounded in creation, and he speaks of man as the first bearer of dominion within marriage.<Cite source="bavinck-reformed-dogmatics-monergism-2" locator="Vol. 2, §34.4–5, PDF pp. 690–693" /> That headship language is complementarian; Reformed churches disagree over how it applies, and it is reported here as Bavinck's view, not as a definition of the image itself. What the tradition shares is that sexual difference belongs to the created goodness of the body, as <QuestionLink question="offspring-family" worldview="christianity">the family question</QuestionLink> develops.

#### Where the Fall touches nature

Sin corrupts the faculties without abolishing them, and the Westminster Shorter Catechism still defines the creature by the image in knowledge, righteousness and holiness, with dominion over the creatures.<Cite source="westminster-shorter-catechism" locator="Q. 10" /> The present question concerns what the human being is; why that creature is now both great and terrible is the subject of <QuestionLink question="great-and-terrible" worldview="christianity">the flagship</QuestionLink>.
