## The Christian view

Human beings know right and wrong because they are made for God and answerable to him. Genesis presents humanity as created in God's image and given its task under his blessing and command:<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Genesis 1:26–28" /> from the start the human creature stands under God's word, not above it. God's claim comes before anyone's consent, and conscience is the creature's awareness of that claim. Paul says that Gentiles who never received the law of Moses still show that "the work of the law is written on their hearts," while their conscience bears witness, accusing or excusing them.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 2:14–15" /> The Westminster Confession says the first humans were made with "the law of God written in their hearts."<Cite source="wcf" locator="4.2" />

That knowledge survives the Fall, but damaged. Its first principles remain in everyone, together with the conclusions that follow directly from them: good is to be done and evil avoided, God is to be worshiped, parents honored, and no one harmed. The remoter conclusions, reached by longer reasoning, are where sin does its work, through native corruption, upbringing and custom, until whole societies can honor vices as virtues.<Cite source="turretin-institutio-1847" locator="XI.1.7, 11, 20" note="Latin; paraphrased" /> Paul describes people who know God's righteous decree yet suppress the truth.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 1:18–21, 32" /> The Canons of Dort teach that a light of nature remains, with some notion of the difference between moral and immoral conduct, yet fallen people do not use it rightly even in natural and civil matters.<Cite source="canons-of-dort" locator="III/IV.4" />

That is why God gave his law in writing. The law given at creation "continued to be a perfect rule of righteousness" after the Fall and was delivered at Sinai in the Ten Commandments.<Cite source="wcf" locator="19.1–2" /> Its content is the same as the law written on the heart; it differs in clarity and fullness. Turretin compares what remains of natural law in fallen hearts to an old, faded picture that God renews by his own voice and hand.<Cite source="turretin-institutio-1847" locator="XI.1.22–23" note="Latin; paraphrased" /> Scripture clarifies and corrects moral knowledge; it does not create it from nothing.

## What this explains well

The account holds together common moral knowledge, genuine disagreement and moral failure. It does not require saying that unbelievers never identify a true obligation, nor that every conscience judges every case reliably. Agreement is widest at the level of first principles and narrows as judgments become particular. Calvin observes that civil and social duties are better recognized than duties toward God, and that judgments are bent most where self-love has most to gain.<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="II.2.22–24" /> The account therefore predicts a definite pattern: shared first principles, divergent applications, and the sharpest distortion where self-interest and worship are at stake. A markedly different pattern would tell against it. A faculty can remain real while its exercise needs correction, and knowing the good can come apart from doing it.

## Strongest objection

An evolutionary account can explain why social animals with reflective capacities develop conscience.<Cite source="darwin-descent" locator="ch. IV, pp. 97–98" /> Naturalists who are moral realists add that moral facts need not be any less objective for that: moral assessment can consider everyone's interests from a social point of view and be corrected through experience.<Cite source="railton-moral-realism" locator="pp. 190–194" /> Why add God to that account? Conversely, if conscience is God-given, why is it so uneven and so vulnerable to cultural distortion? Appealing to corruption can seem to accommodate whatever pattern the evidence presents rather than yielding an independently testable account.

## Christian reply

The Christian need not deny the mechanisms. The objection runs together three questions. One is causal: how moral cognition arose in individuals and societies, which evolutionary and social history may largely explain. A second is epistemic: whether conscience gets anything right. The third concerns authority: why an obligation actually binds someone who dislikes it, whose society rejects it, or who would gain by breaking it. A history of moral feeling, however complete, answers the first question. By itself it does not answer the third. On the Christian account the three belong together: God may form conscience through natural and social means, what it registers is a real if damaged knowledge of his law, and his righteous claim is why that law binds.

The Christian answer to the third is that obligation has authority because creatures stand under a Creator who is himself righteous. God, Calvin argues, "as our Creator, is entitled to be regarded by us as a Father and Master"; we "are not our own," and since God "can only require what is right, we are necessarily under a natural obligation to obey."<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="II.8.2" /> Turretin sharpens the point. The natural law is not just because God commands it; God commands it because it is just, rooted in his own holiness and wisdom, so that once he made rational creatures he could not fail to prescribe it.<Cite source="turretin-institutio-1847" locator="XI.1.4, 9" note="Latin; paraphrased" /> The good is therefore neither an arbitrary decree nor a standard above God. It is the claim of his own righteous character on the creatures he made for himself, and it binds as the word of the "one lawgiver and judge" to whom every person must give account.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="James 4:12" />

This does not mean that morality without God is mere preference. Naturalist realists hold that moral facts are objective (see <QuestionLink question="know-the-good" worldview="naturalism">the naturalist answer</QuestionLink>), and the Christian's pressure on them is comparative. Railton grants that his realism does not make moral demands categorical: someone can truly be obligated by what is rational from an impartial point of view and yet have no reason of his own to comply. He argues that morality can live with this, since the obligation still holds and has general grounds, as logic's demands do.<Cite source="railton-moral-realism" locator="pp. 201–203" /> The Christian presses at that point: what gives the obligation authority over that person, rather than describing what an impartial standpoint would approve? The Christian answer is that obligation is personal at its source, the claim of the Creator to whom the person belongs, and that conscience was made by the same God whose character it registers. That is a claim of better explanation, not a demonstration that realism without God is incoherent.

## Deep dive

#### Natural law in Reformed theology

Turretin distinguishes two kinds of divine law. Positive law, such as the command about the tree in Eden or Israel's ceremonies, depends on God's free will alone; its precepts are good because God commands them. Natural law rests on God's own justice and holiness; its precepts are commanded because they are good.<Cite source="turretin-institutio-1847" locator="XI.1.4" note="Latin; paraphrased" /> It is called natural not because it arises from bare nature, since it depends on God the supreme Lawgiver, but because it is known from creation and implanted in the mind. Against Carneades and Hobbes, who made justice a matter of convention or utility, Turretin held that it binds all peoples before any human legislation.<Cite source="turretin-institutio-1847" locator="XI.1.5–7" note="Latin; paraphrased" /> He also argues from dependence: rational creatures depend on God for their moral life as much as for their being, so to deny that they are bound by his law would be to imagine a creature independent of its Creator.<Cite source="turretin-institutio-1847" locator="XI.1.9, 15" note="Latin; paraphrased" />

#### Conscience accuses and excuses

Romans 2 describes both functions; it does not certify every verdict an individual happens to feel. Calvin calls conscience "an inward witness and monitor," which "reminds us of what we owe to God" and "points out the distinction between good and evil," yet by it a fallen person is "scarcely able" to form any tolerable idea of the worship God accepts.<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="II.8.1" /> The law written on the heart can be suppressed in its exercise without being extinguished in its principle.<Cite source="turretin-institutio-1847" locator="XI.1.18" note="Latin; paraphrased" /> The Larger Catechism explains that the moral law declares the will of God, informs people "of the holy nature and will of God, and of their duty," convinces them of their inability, and, for the unregenerate, either drives them to Christ or leaves them "inexcusable."<Cite source="westminster-larger-catechism" locator="Q. 93, 95–96" />

#### Knowing duty and doing it

Knowing an obligation is not the same as having a heart renewed to love it, and natural moral knowledge is not saving knowledge: it leaves people accountable without supplying the knowledge of God necessary for salvation.<Cite source="wcf" locator="1.1" /> <QuestionLink question="fail-the-good" worldview="christianity">Moral failure</QuestionLink> explains acting against recognized duty, and <QuestionLink question="self-deception" worldview="christianity">self-deception</QuestionLink> considers how its recognition is distorted.
