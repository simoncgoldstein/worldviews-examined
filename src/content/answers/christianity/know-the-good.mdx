## The Christian view

Paul describes Gentiles without the Mosaic law recognizing its requirements, their conscience accusing or defending their conduct.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 2:14–15" /> The Christian explanation begins with humanity's creation in God's image, not with an otherwise morally empty creature receiving arbitrary rules.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Genesis 1:26–28" /> Westminster connects that creation to knowledge, righteousness, and God's law written on the heart.<Cite source="wcf" locator="4.2" />

This knowledge is real but not morally innocent or exhaustive. Paul also describes known truth suppressed, so awareness of obligation can coexist with resistance to its source.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 1:18–21" /> Dort affirms remaining notions of the difference between moral and immoral conduct while warning that people distort their use.<Cite source="canons-of-dort" locator="III/IV.4" /> Scripture's written moral law therefore clarifies rather than creates every instance of moral recognition. Westminster describes continuity between the law given at creation and its later declaration.<Cite source="wcf" locator="19.1–2" />

## What this explains well

The account holds together common moral knowledge, genuine disagreement, and moral failure. It does not require saying that unbelievers never identify a true obligation, nor that every person's conscience reliably judges every case. Calvin distinguishes the general awareness of justice from judgments distorted by self-love or particular circumstances.<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="II.2.22–24" /> A faculty can remain real while its exercise needs correction. Seeking the good and faithfully doing it are therefore distinguishable.

## Strongest objection

An evolutionary account can explain why social animals with reflective capacities develop conscience.<Cite source="darwin-descent" locator="ch. IV, pp. 97–98" /> Why add knowledge of God to that account? Conversely, if conscience is God-given, why is it so uneven and vulnerable to cultural distortion? Appealing to corruption can seem to accommodate whatever pattern the evidence presents rather than yielding an independently testable account.

## Christian reply

The Christian need not reject a history of moral learning or its natural mechanisms. His further claim is that the obligation recognized concerns a personal Creator whose law governs the creature. Its reality does not depend on flawless human recognition. The confession also limits what natural knowledge supplies: it leaves people accountable without providing all the knowledge necessary for salvation.<Cite source="wcf" locator="1.1" /> This is a theological interpretation of the evidence, not a proof derived from the mere existence of conscience. The critic can dispute that interpretation without thereby denying moral awareness.

## Deep dive

#### Four questions about conscience

The origin of a capacity is not identical with the truth of its judgments. A social or developmental history can describe how someone comes to recognize a duty. Whether the duty really binds, why it binds, and whether this particular recognition is reliable require further argument. The Reformed claim locates its authority in God's law while admitting that its human recognition is damaged.

This matters when conscience accuses and when it excuses. Romans 2 describes both functions; it does not certify every verdict an individual happens to feel. Nor does the doctrine make intense conviction a substitute for Scripture or attention to the actual case. The Larger Catechism presents the moral law as informing duty and disclosing inability, not as congratulating people on whatever their consciences approve.<Cite source="westminster-larger-catechism" locator="Q. 93, 95–96" />

Calvin also treats moral discernment as uneven: civil and social duties can be better recognized than duties toward God.<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="II.2.24" /> That supports a qualification within the account, not the claim that all shared ethical judgments are worthless. The dossier notes differences of emphasis within Reformed natural-law discussions; this page does not adjudicate the modern debate without its primary arguments.
