## The Christian view

At the bottom of everything Reformed Christianity places a living God, not a substance or a force. He has no beginning, no cause and no source outside himself, and he depends on nothing. Before the mountains were brought forth he is God from everlasting to everlasting;<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Psalm 90:2" /> he has life in himself;<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="John 5:26" /> and he is not served by human hands "as though he needed anything."<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Acts 17:24–25" /> Everything else, galaxies, angels, minds and time, exists because he made it,<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Genesis 1:1; Revelation 4:11" /> and it goes on existing because he holds it: "in him all things hold together."<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Colossians 1:16–17" />

The basic division in reality is therefore between Creator and creature, and the two are not equal principles. God is not the largest item in a universe bigger than himself, and the world is not a piece or overflow of God. Created things are fully real. Bavinck insists, against pantheism, that a creature has its own distinct being. It has that being only as something given.<Cite source="bavinck-reformed-dogmatics-monergism-2" locator="Vol. 2, §26.1, PDF pp. 151–155" />

This God is personal, and he is one God in three persons, Father, Son and Holy Spirit.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Deuteronomy 6:4; Matthew 28:19" /> The Westminster Confession gathers the biblical picture into one phrase: God is "the alone fountain of all being."<Cite source="wcf" locator="2.1–2" />

## What this explains well

The account explains why the world is both real and contingent: real enough to study, enjoy and take morally seriously, yet in need of explanation, because everything in it changes, depends and could have been otherwise. The view also gives a stopping point that is not arbitrary: a being whose existence is not borrowed from anything. And it makes persons native to reality rather than a late accident, as <QuestionLink question="what-is-man" worldview="christianity">the account of human nature</QuestionLink> develops.

## Strongest objection

Every worldview must stop somewhere. The naturalist asks why the stopping point should be God rather than the universe. In Hume's *Dialogues* even Cleanthes, the theist, asks why the material universe could not be the necessarily existent being.<Cite source="hume-dialogues" locator="9.7" /> Sean Carroll argues that every explanation bottoms out in brute facts. A creator, he says, simply adds one more brute fact, and there is no reason not to count the universe itself as the thing that just exists. If the theist answers that God exists necessarily, Carroll replies that the idea of a necessary being does not hold together.<Cite source="carroll-something-rather-than-nothing" locator="§5" /> On this view "self-existent God" is only a new label for the stopping point, bought at the price of an extra entity.

## Christian reply

Every account ends in something not explained by anything else; the question is which stopping point fits. Augustine points out that heaven and earth change, and so declare that they were made and did not make themselves. Compared with their Creator, he says, they do not even exist in the way he does.<Cite source="augustine-confessions" locator="XI.4.6" /> The universe as we meet it is composite, law-bound and changing: marks of something that depends.

The God of Scripture is a different kind of stopping point. He is not one more item with an unexplained existence. Turretin argues that God is simple, not assembled from parts or qualities, so nothing prior is needed to account for him.<Cite source="turretin-institutio-1847" locator="III.7.1–4" /> A naturalist who denies that any being can exist necessarily will not grant this, so the reply depends on the coherence of a self-existent God, which classical theism defends and Carroll disputes. That is a judgment of comparative explanatory adequacy, not a proof. The Christian also holds the doctrine because God has revealed it, not only because of arguments.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Hebrews 11:3" /> <QuestionLink question="something-rather-than-nothing" worldview="christianity">Why there is anything at all</QuestionLink> takes the argument further.

## Deep dive

#### Aseity and simplicity in plain terms

Theologians call God's existence from himself *aseity*. Bavinck makes it the first thing to say of God: God exists of himself and is the only source of all being, and everything else has being from him.<Cite source="bavinck-reformed-dogmatics-monergism-2" locator="Vol. 2, §26.1, PDF pp. 151–155" /> *Simplicity* follows from it. If God were made of parts, the parts would be prior to him, and something would have to unite them. Turretin argues simplicity from four directions: God's independence, his unity, his perfection, and his being pure act rather than a mix of potential and actual.<Cite source="turretin-institutio-1847" locator="III.7.1–4" /> Augustine had put the point briefly: God alone is what he has.<Cite source="augustine-city-of-god" locator="XI.10" /> Bavinck adds that simplicity does not mean emptiness. It means infinitely full being, and so it is the opposite of a pantheist abstraction.<Cite source="bavinck-reformed-dogmatics-monergism-2" locator="Vol. 2, §26.5, PDF p. 185" /> The Westminster and Belgic confessions both state the doctrine.<Cite source="wcf" locator="2.1" /><Cite source="belgic-confession" locator="Art. 1" /> Some recent Reformed writers question its strongest forms; the confessional position is followed here.

#### Not emanation, not a world-soul

Augustine explains why the world cannot be made out of God's substance: then it would be equal to the Son, who alone is from the Father's substance. Nor is it made from a pre-existing matter. It is made from nothing, by the Word.<Cite source="augustine-confessions" locator="XI.5.7; XII.7.7" /> Calvin rejects both the old idea that the visible world is itself God and the dualist idea of two eternal powers.<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="I.13.1" /> He calls the ancient theory of a world-soul, in which God is the inner life of the cosmos, absurd. Creation, he says, shows God's eternity by contrast with the world's beginning.<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="I.14.1" /> Bavinck's argument is that pantheism cannot distinguish God's will from the world's own process. Scripture, by contrast, gives creatures their own being because God willed it freely.<Cite source="bavinck-reformed-dogmatics-monergism-2" locator="Vol. 2, §26.16, PDF p. 262" />

#### Present everywhere without being everything

Heaven cannot contain God,<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="1 Kings 8:27" /> and there is nowhere to flee from his presence.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Psalm 139:7–10" /> He fills heaven and earth, yet is not a hidden spatial ingredient of them.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Jeremiah 23:23–24" /> This immensity is the positive side of the Creator–creature distinction. God is near to every creature because every creature depends on him, not because he and the creature share one substance.

#### Where the confessions place this

The Larger Catechism opens its doctrine of God with a spirit "in and of himself infinite in being."<Cite source="westminster-larger-catechism" locator="Q. 7" /> The Shorter Catechism gives the same doctrine in a sentence.<Cite source="westminster-shorter-catechism" locator="Q. 4" /> Both summarize Scripture's self-existent Creator; neither adds to it.
