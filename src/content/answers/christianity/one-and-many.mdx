## The Christian view

The world is plainly both one and many: one cosmos, countless distinct things. Christianity locates the source of both in God.

God is one: "the Lord our God, the Lord is one."<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Deuteronomy 6:4" /> Yet the one God is Father, Son and Holy Spirit, named together in a single name.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Matthew 28:19" /> The persons are really distinct but not divided; neither the unity nor the distinction comes first.<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="I.13.17" />

Creation is many things from one source: "from him and through him and to him are all things."<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 11:36" /> In God, unity in diversity and diversity in unity are complete. Creatures reflect this only faintly; in them, as Bavinck puts it, either the unity or the diversity never fully comes into its own.<Cite source="bavinck-reformed-dogmatics-monergism-2" locator="Vol. 2, §27.25, PDF pp. 385–387" note="automated English translation; paraphrased" /> Still, the world has a unity that preserves its variety rather than destroying it, because one wise God made, preserves and governs all things.<Cite source="bavinck-reformed-dogmatics-monergism-2" locator="Vol. 2, §29.12, PDF pp. 519–520" note="automated English translation; paraphrased" />

## What this explains well

The account lets neither unity nor plurality swallow the other: the many are real, and so is the one order, both grounded in a God who is neither a bare unity nor a crowd.

## Strongest objection

The objection is that this explains too little. Calling God "one and three" names the puzzle rather than solving it, and the problem concerns the world, not God's inner life. A naturalist can describe unity and plurality through physics and levels of organization without appeal to the Trinity.<Cite source="sep-physicalism" locator="§§2, 3.1" />

## Christian reply

The reply should not overclaim. The Trinity is revealed, not deduced, and it does not "solve" the problem by demonstration. Bavinck's claim is more modest. He argues that only a God with real distinction in himself lets theology hold the world both connected to God and distinct from him, avoiding both deism and pantheism.<Cite source="bavinck-reformed-dogmatics-monergism-2" locator="Vol. 2, §27.25, PDF pp. 385–387" note="automated English translation; paraphrased" /> That is theological reasoning about fit.

The naturalist is right that physics can describe how things fall under common laws without settling any question about God. The Christian's pressure lies elsewhere: physical description takes for granted that the world is both truly one and truly many. The Christian locates the ground of both in a God in whom neither comes first. That is a comparative claim, that one account explains the fit of unity and diversity while the other accepts it as a brute fact.

## Deep dive

#### Simple and triune

Augustine insists that the Trinity does not make God composite. God is called simple because he is what he has, and the persons are distinguished only by their relations to one another.<Cite source="augustine-city-of-god" locator="XI.10" /> The Westminster Confession states the doctrine without speculation: in the unity of the Godhead there are three persons of one substance, power and eternity.<Cite source="wcf" locator="2.3" />

#### One God, one Lord

Paul writes of "one God, the Father, from whom are all things," and "one Lord, Jesus Christ, through whom are all things."<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="1 Corinthians 8:6" /> The many gifts in the church come from the same Spirit, the same Lord and the same God.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="1 Corinthians 12:4–6" /> Scripture links the variety of creation and of the church to the one God in three persons, but it does not offer a metaphysical theory.

#### The world as an organism

Bavinck describes the world, in a figurative sense, as an organism. Its members are connected and act on one another, yet each keeps its own nature.<Cite source="bavinck-reformed-dogmatics-monergism-2" locator="Vol. 2, §29.12, PDF pp. 519–520" note="automated English translation; paraphrased" />

#### Van Til's stronger argument

Van Til made the one and the many central to his apologetic. In God, he argued, "the one and the many are equally ultimate": unity is no more basic than diversity, nor diversity than unity.<Cite source="van-til-defense" locator="p. 42" /> Non-Christian thought, he contended, is caught between particulars so unrelated that they could not be known and a unity so abstract that it strips particulars of their particularity. Only the triune God is a true "concrete universal," and created unity and diversity are "equally derived and equally dependent upon God."<Cite source="van-til-defense" locator="pp. 42–44" /> He insisted that he borrowed philosophers' vocabulary to set the biblical Trinity against theories built on human experience, not to make the Trinity an abstract principle.<Cite source="van-til-defense" locator="pp. 228, 240–241" />

These are three different claims. That God is one being in three persons, the source of the world's unity and variety, is confessional doctrine resting on Scripture.<Cite source="wcf" locator="2.3; 4.1" /> That creation's unity in diversity faintly reflects the triune life is Bavinck's theological exposition. That only the ontological Trinity can ground unity and diversity without reducing one to the other is Van Til's apologetic development; Scripture and the Westminster Standards do not state it, and not every Reformed theologian has argued it. Its force is real but limited. Van Til's verdict that there is "no answer to these problems from a non-Christian point of view"<Cite source="van-til-defense" locator="p. 43" /> rests on the failure of the philosophies he examined, which shows that those systems failed, not that every alternative must. Pressed fairly, it becomes the comparative claim made in the reply.
