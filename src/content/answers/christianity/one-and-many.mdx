## The Christian view

The world is plainly both one and many: one cosmos, countless distinct things. Christianity locates the source of both in God.

God is one: "the Lord our God, the Lord is one."<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Deuteronomy 6:4" /> Yet the one God is Father, Son and Holy Spirit, named together in a single name.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Matthew 28:19" /> The persons are really distinct but not divided; neither the unity nor the distinction comes first.<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="I.13.17" />

Creation is many things from one source: "from him and through him and to him are all things."<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 11:36" /> Bavinck argues that in God there is unity in diversity and diversity in unity in an absolute way. Creatures show only a weak analogy of it, since in them either the unity or the diversity never fully comes into its own.<Cite source="bavinck-reformed-dogmatics-monergism-2" locator="Vol. 2, §27.25, PDF pp. 385–387" /> The world has a unity that maintains its diversity rather than destroying it, because one wise God made, preserves and governs all things.<Cite source="bavinck-reformed-dogmatics-monergism-2" locator="Vol. 2, §29.12, PDF pp. 519–520" />

## What this explains well

The account lets neither unity nor plurality swallow the other: the many are real, and so is the one order, both grounded in a God who is neither a bare unity nor a crowd.

## Strongest objection

The objection is that this explains too little. Calling God "one and three" names the puzzle rather than solving it, and the problem concerns the world, not God's inner life. A naturalist can describe unity and plurality through physics and levels of organization without appeal to the Trinity.<Cite source="sep-physicalism" locator="§§2, 3.1" />

## Christian reply

The reply should not overclaim. The Trinity is revealed, not deduced, and does not "solve" the problem by demonstration. Bavinck's claim is more modest. Only a God with real distinction in himself can create a world connected to him yet distinct from him, avoiding both deism and pantheism.<Cite source="bavinck-reformed-dogmatics-monergism-2" locator="Vol. 2, §27.25, PDF pp. 385–387" /> That is theological reasoning about fit, presented as such.

## Deep dive

#### Simple and triune

Augustine insists that the Trinity does not make God composite. God is called simple because he is what he has, and the persons are distinguished only by their relations to one another.<Cite source="augustine-city-of-god" locator="XI.10" /> The Westminster Confession states the doctrine without speculation: in the unity of the Godhead there are three persons of one substance, power and eternity.<Cite source="wcf" locator="2.3" />

#### One God, one Lord

Paul writes of "one God, the Father, from whom are all things," and "one Lord, Jesus Christ, through whom are all things."<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="1 Corinthians 8:6" /> The many gifts in the church come from the same Spirit, the same Lord and the same God.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="1 Corinthians 12:4–6" /> Scripture links the variety of creation and of the church to the one God in three persons, but it does not offer a metaphysical theory.

#### How far the argument goes

Bavinck describes the world, in a figurative sense, as an organism. Its members are connected and act on one another, yet each keeps its own nature.<Cite source="bavinck-reformed-dogmatics-monergism-2" locator="Vol. 2, §29.12, PDF pp. 519–520" /> Some later Reformed apologists have pressed a stronger, transcendental version of the one-and-many argument. It is not presented here, because their texts could not be lawfully checked for this domain. Bavinck is read in an automated English translation and paraphrased.
