## The Christian view

The organizing principle is that love, beauty and making come from God and are rightly ordered when they return to him. Love is commanded first toward God and then toward neighbour,<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Matthew 22:37–40" /> and it answers God's prior love,<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="1 John 4:7–12" /> for "We love because he first loved us."<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="1 John 4:19" /> God saw that what he had made was very good,<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Genesis 1:31" /> the heavens declare his glory,<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Psalm 19:1–4" /> and the psalmist's one desire is "to gaze upon the beauty of the LORD."<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Psalm 27:4" /> God fills Bezalel with his Spirit for artistic design in every craft.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Exodus 31:1–6" />

Augustine gives the principle of order. Created beauty is a good, loved well when loved in its place and badly when preferred to God; virtue is, briefly and truly, "the order of love."<Cite source="augustine-city-of-god" locator="XV.22" /> Calvin insists that God made things not only for use but for delight: food for enjoyment, clothing for comeliness, flowers and trees for their beauty and scent.<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="III.10.2" /> The arts and sciences, even among unbelievers, are gifts of the Spirit.<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="II.2.15–16" /> Edwards adds that the beauty of order and harmony in the world is an image of the higher beauty of love itself.<Cite source="edwards-works-hickman-1" locator="True Virtue ch. 3" />

## What this explains well

The account explains why beauty seems to mean more than usefulness, why love feels both natural and commanded, and why humans make things that no survival need requires. It also explains how these goods become destructive when they are made ultimate.

## Strongest objection

Darwin traced the sense of beauty and the origins of music to sexual selection.<Cite source="darwin-descent" locator="ch. III, pp. 92–93; ch. XIX, p. 572" /> If evolution explains why we find things beautiful, the theological meaning seems superfluous.

## Christian reply

An account of how a capacity arose does not show that its objects are not really beautiful, or that beauty points to nothing. Calvin's point is that God made a world fitted to delight creatures able to perceive it. The Christian grants that this is a theological reading of the data, not a deduction from biology.

## Deep dive

#### Art, images and worship

The Reformed objection is to images of God and to worship through images, not to art. The Heidelberg Catechism says that God may not be visibly portrayed, although "creatures may be portrayed."<Cite source="heidelberg-catechism" locator="Q&A 97" /> Calvin calls sculpture and painting gifts of God and forbids only representing God's form.<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="I.11.12" /> Turretin allows images of creatures for history, ornament and delight.<Cite source="turretin-institutio-1847" locator="XI.10.2–3" /> Reformed churches differ over images in worship spaces.

#### Art is not religion

Bavinck holds that religion and art are essentially distinct. Art can lift the soul in imagination, but it cannot reconcile guilt or give communion with God.<Cite source="bavinck-reformed-dogmatics-monergism-1" locator="Vol. 1, §8.14, PDF p. 255" /> Augustine confesses that he rushed among "the things of beauty You made" while they kept him far from God, the Fairness "so ancient, and yet so new."<Cite source="augustine-confessions" locator="X.27.38" /> The image-bearing creator behind this account is described in <QuestionLink question="what-is-man" worldview="christianity">the human-nature anchor</QuestionLink>; how good gifts become rivals to God is treated in <QuestionLink question="worship" worldview="christianity">the worship question</QuestionLink>.
