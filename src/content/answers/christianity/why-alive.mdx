## The Christian view

Scripture gives human life a purpose that comes from its maker. God speaks of everyone called by his name as created for his glory.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Isaiah 43:7" /> All things are from him, through him and to him;<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 11:36" /> they were created through Christ and for him,<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Colossians 1:16" /> and exist by God's will.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Revelation 4:11" /> That purpose reaches ordinary life: whether one eats or drinks, all is to be done to God's glory.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="1 Corinthians 10:31" /> Having God, the psalmist desires nothing besides him on earth.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Psalm 73:25–26" />

Augustine turned this into a description of the human heart: "You have made us for Yourself, and our hearts are restless until they rest in You."<Cite source="augustine-confessions" locator="I.1.1" /> Jonathan Edwards argued that God created the world not from need but from an inclination to communicate his own fullness, and that God's glory includes the creature's knowledge of God, love for him and joy in him.<Cite source="edwards-works-hickman-1" locator="End of Creation I.2; II.7" /> The Westminster Standards compress this into one sentence: man's chief end is "to glorify God, and to enjoy him forever."<Cite source="westminster-shorter-catechism" locator="Q. 1" />

## What this explains well

The account explains why humans ask the question at all: a creature made for God looks for a purpose larger than itself and finds lesser goods unsatisfying when made ultimate. It gives ordinary work, meals and family life a meaning that does not depend on being extraordinary. And it holds together purpose and happiness: because the end is to enjoy God, fulfilment is not sacrificed to duty.

## Strongest objection

Some philosophers argue that a God-given purpose would diminish rather than secure meaning. A recurrent objection, traced to Sartre and first stated in analytic philosophy by Kurt Baier, is that a God who created us with a purpose in mind would thereby degrade us, as one makes an instrument; others argue that God would remove the meaning of being one's own master.<Cite source="sep-meaning-of-life" locator="§§2.1, 3.3" /> A God who makes his own glory the end of creation can also look self-seeking. Russell puts the humanist alternative directly: "It is we who create value," and the good life is for us to determine, "not even for Nature personified as God."<Cite source="russell-what-i-believe" locator="ch. 1" />

## Christian reply

The objection assumes that God's glory and human good compete. Edwards denies it: God's glory, rightly understood, includes the creature's own knowledge, love and joy, so that in seeking his glory God seeks the creature's happiness in him.<Cite source="edwards-works-hickman-1" locator="End of Creation II.7" /> A tool serves an end outside itself; communion with God is the creature's own good. God, needing nothing, gives life and breath to all.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Acts 17:24–28" /> The Christian concedes that this answer depends on God's goodness, which the objector doubts. How the purpose is completed is treated in <QuestionLink question="final-end" worldview="christianity">the final-end question</QuestionLink>.

## Deep dive

#### God's communicative fullness

Edwards's argument begins from God's self-sufficiency. Because God lacks nothing, he cannot create in order to gain something. Creation flows from a disposition in God to communicate his own fullness of knowledge, holiness and happiness.<Cite source="edwards-works-hickman-1" locator="End of Creation I.2" /> When God makes himself his end, he makes the creature's participation in that fullness his end too.<Cite source="edwards-works-hickman-1" locator="End of Creation II.7" /> This is Edwards's formulation, not a confessional formula, but it explains why Reformed theology refuses to set God's glory against human flourishing.

#### Ordinary life as calling

The Larger Catechism describes humanity's original condition as life in paradise, with work to do, dominion over the creatures, marriage for mutual help, communion with God and the Sabbath.<Cite source="westminster-larger-catechism" locator="Q. 20" /> Bavinck treats these as creation ordinances, grounded in creation and not in sin, and describes the human vocation as both gift and task: humanity is to unfold the image across its generations through family, work, science and art.<Cite source="bavinck-reformed-dogmatics-monergism-2" locator="Vol. 2, §34.4–5, PDF pp. 690–693" /> The Belgic Confession says the creatures serve humanity so that humanity may serve God.<Cite source="belgic-confession" locator="Art. 12" />

#### Purpose known and refused

Calvin holds that all people are born to know God, so that a life not directed to that knowledge misses the purpose of its creation.<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="I.3.3" /> The purpose does not disappear when people reject it. It reappears as restlessness, or as devotion transferred to something else, which <QuestionLink question="worship" worldview="christianity">the worship question</QuestionLink> treats. For the Reformed tradition, the restoration of that purpose depends on Christ's work, as <QuestionLink question="self-salvation" worldview="christianity">the salvation anchor</QuestionLink> explains. What humans are, the creature for whom this purpose is fitting, is set out in <QuestionLink question="what-is-man" worldview="christianity">the human-nature anchor</QuestionLink>.
