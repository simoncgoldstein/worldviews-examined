## The view

Muslim theologians expect regularity because God creates according to a constant custom. Al-Ghazali opened his famous discussion of causation with a sentence that anticipates Hume: "The connection between what is habitually believed to be a cause and what is habitually believed to be an effect is not necessary, according to us." Fire and burning are connected "due to the prior decision of God, who creates them side by side," not because the connection is necessary in itself.<Cite source="sep-al-ghazali" locator="§7.2" note="quoting the Tahāfut, Discussion 17" /><Cite source="averroes-tahafut-bouyges" locator="Phys. Disc. I, pp. 517–518" note="Arabic; paraphrased" /> He denies necessary connection, not every causal connection. In one of his approaches he even allows secondary causes, provided they work as God has arranged.<Cite source="sep-al-ghazali" locator="§§7.2, 7.4" />

Why, then, expect fire to burn tomorrow? Because God has chosen a fixed order and has told us he will keep it. In his *Revival*, al-Ghazali says that God creates things one after another in an orderly way, calls this order God's habit (*sunna*), and quotes the Qur'an: "You will not find any change in God's habit."<Cite source="sep-al-ghazali" locator="§7.4" note="on Iḥyāʾ, book 31" /> In their Qur'anic setting those verses speak of God's dealings with earlier peoples.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="33:62; 48:23" /> Al-Ghazali applies the language more broadly, to the order of nature.

Not all Sunni theologians were occasionalists. Maturidis and Ibn Taymiyya affirmed that God gives created things real powers that act as his instruments, as rain makes plants grow.<Cite source="sep-ibn-taymiyya" locator="§4.5" /> The philosopher Ibn Rushd held that causes act necessarily and called their denial sophistry.<Cite source="averroes-tahafut-bouyges" locator="Phys. Disc. I, p. 519" note="Arabic; paraphrased" /> These positions are described more fully under <QuestionLink question="order" worldview="islam">order</QuestionLink>.

No classical Muslim thinker addressed Hume. What these views provide is a theological ground for expecting regularity, together with sharply different theories of what a cause is.

## What this explains well

The account explains why regularity is reliable without making nature independent of God, and leaves room for miracles. Al-Ghazali saw that observation shows only that events occur together, not that one must produce the other.

## Christian response

Al-Ghazali's answer is structurally the closest to the Reformed one: our confidence in regularity rests on God's faithfulness, made known by revelation. The Christian has no quarrel with that. The difference concerns causation. Reformed theology affirms real second causes that act "according to the nature of second causes,"<Cite source="wcf" locator="5.2" /> closer to Ibn Taymiyya and the Maturidis than to al-Ghazali's occasionalist approach. Both lanes face the same limit: God's promise gives a reason to expect regularity, not a proof that escapes Hume's circle (see <QuestionLink question="induction" worldview="christianity">the Christian answer</QuestionLink>). This is a theological disagreement within shared ground, not a charge of incoherence.

## Pressure questions

- If created things have no causal power of their own, what does science discover when it finds a law of nature?
- If confidence in regularity rests on God's promise, how is that promise received without already relying on the regularity it guarantees?

## Deep dive

#### Two approaches in one discussion

Al-Ghazali offers two accounts in the same chapter. In the first, God creates every event directly, following a custom (*ʿāda*); fire does not really burn cotton, burning merely accompanies contact with fire. In the second, God works through secondary causes he has arranged. Miracles, on this account, are built into God's plan from the beginning rather than being interventions.<Cite source="sep-al-ghazali" locator="§§7.2, 7.4" /> His aim was not to deny that science works. It was to show, against Ibn Sina, that God's freedom is not bound by necessary causes.

#### The verses in context

The verse quoted in the *Revival* occurs twice in the Qur'an. In one place it follows a warning to hypocrites: "This has been God's practice with those who went before."<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="33:62" /> In the other it concerns a battle: "such was God's practice in the past."<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="48:23" /> A third verse, about earlier peoples who plotted evil, says the same.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="35:43" /> The verses concern God's consistent dealings with communities. Extending them to the order of nature is al-Ghazali's interpretive step, a reasonable one within his theology but not the verses' direct subject.

#### Ibn Taymiyya on induction

Ibn Taymiyya was an empiricist about general claims. To know with certainty that "all flames burn," one would have to examine every flame; because complete induction is so hard, knowledge of the external world is usually "merely probable, not certain."<Cite source="sep-ibn-taymiyya" locator="§3.2" /> He argued that a demonstrative syllogism with a universal premise about the world adds nothing to the induction on which that premise rests. Anyone who has checked every flame already knows each one burns.<Cite source="sep-ibn-taymiyya" locator="§3.2" /> He thus put induction, and its fallibility, at the base of natural knowledge.
