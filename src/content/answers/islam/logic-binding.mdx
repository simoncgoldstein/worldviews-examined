## The view

Muslim theologians did not doubt that logic binds. Its basic principles belong to what reason knows immediately, without proof. The creed of al-Nasafi gives an example: reason knows "of necessity... that everything is greater than its parts."<Cite source="macdonald-muslim-theology" locator="App. I, p. 308" /> The Ash'ari theologian al-Juwayni held that every sound thinker finds within herself "the knowledge about the possibility of what is possible, the necessity of what is necessary, and the impossibility of what is impossible." No one learns this from others; it is an immediate impulse of reason.<Cite source="sep-al-ghazali" locator="§7.3" note="al-Juwayni, as quoted by SEP" /> Ibn Taymiyya, of a different school, likewise counts the laws of non-contradiction and excluded middle as immediate, necessary knowledge, part of the natural constitution (*fiṭra*) with which God creates human beings.<Cite source="sep-ibn-taymiyya" locator="§3.1" />

So the binding force of logic is treated as given with the created mind. Classical Islamic thought did not develop a separate theory of what makes logical necessity hold; it counted these principles among the first things known.

The real disagreement concerned Aristotelian logic as a discipline. Al-Ghazali welcomed it. Logic, he wrote, "contains nothing for or against religion." It studies proofs, syllogisms and definitions, and its only danger is that readers impressed by its rigour may trust the philosophers' metaphysics too far.<Cite source="ghazali-confessions-field" locator="pp. 30–32" /> Ibn Taymiyya was more critical. He granted that a valid syllogism with certain premises yields certain knowledge, but rejected the logicians' claim that certain knowledge needs the syllogism at all.<Cite source="sep-ibn-taymiyya" locator="§3.2" note="quoting al-Radd ʿalā al-manṭiqiyyīn via SEP" /> Islamic thought was therefore neither uniformly Aristotelian nor uniformly anti-Aristotelian.

## What this explains well

The account explains why logic seems self-evident and universal without making it depend on any teacher, and it leaves room to criticize formal logic's pretensions while keeping valid reasoning.

## Christian response

The Christian largely agrees. Logic is given with the mind God made, and Ibn Taymiyya's placing of the rules of thought within the God-given *fiṭra* is the closest parallel to the Reformed view that God implanted the rules of sound reasoning in rational creatures.<Cite source="turretin-institutio-1847" locator="I.10.1–3" note="Latin; paraphrased" /> The difference is mainly one of explicitness. Reformed theology goes on to ask why logical necessity holds of things and not only in minds, and answers by grounding it in God's truthfulness (see <QuestionLink question="logic-binding" worldview="christianity">the Christian answer</QuestionLink>). Islamic theology largely treats the principles as basic. That is a difference in how far each tradition has theorized the question, not a contradiction in either.

## Pressure questions

- If the laws of thought are known by immediate impulse, what distinguishes a genuine rational necessity from a deeply held assumption?
- If God freely chooses which possibilities become actual, what makes the impossible impossible even for him?

## Deep dive

#### Possibility in Ash'ari theology

Ash'ari theologians developed an account of modality close to the modern idea of alternative possible states of affairs. God is the "particularizing agent" who makes one of several alternatives actual. The necessary is what holds in every alternative, and the impossible holds in none.<Cite source="sep-al-ghazali" locator="§7.3" /> On this view God's freedom ranges over the possible, while the impossible is excluded by reason's own judgment, not by a divine decision.

#### Ibn Taymiyya against the logicians

Of the valid syllogism Ibn Taymiyya wrote, "This is not disputed." He also rejected the logicians' theory of definition. He mocked the elaborate figures and conditions of the syllogism as "lean camel meat on a rugged mountain peak," too hard to reach and not worth carrying down. More seriously, he argued that people reach certainty in different ways, that some need a middle term and others do not, and that a sound inference need not have exactly two premises.<Cite source="sep-ibn-taymiyya" locator="§3.2" note="quoting al-Radd ʿalā al-manṭiqiyyīn via SEP" /> He also held that universal claims about the world rest on observation of particulars, a point taken up under <QuestionLink question="induction" worldview="islam">induction</QuestionLink>. His works are cited here through the Stanford Encyclopedia, which quotes them with page references; no checked edition was available.

#### Al-Ghazali's legacy

According to SEP, al-Ghazali's acceptance of demonstration led to a flowering of Aristotelian logic in later Muslim theology.<Cite source="sep-al-ghazali" locator="introduction" /> His warning concerned trust, not validity: a proof in mathematics or logic is not a reason to accept a philosopher's metaphysics.<Cite source="ghazali-confessions-field" locator="pp. 28–32" />
