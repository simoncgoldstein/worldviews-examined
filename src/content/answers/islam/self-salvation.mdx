## The view

Islamic sources begin with each soul's responsibility for its own deeds. There is no inherited guilt; fitra names an original disposition that can be corrupted. Guidance and return are needed because humans are vulnerable to desire and wrongdoing, not because sin is an independent substance.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="6:164; 91:9–10" /><Cite source="muslim-sahih-siddiqui" locator="2865a" /> Al-Ghazali's account of desire preceding mature discernment explains why repentance is universally necessary.<Cite source="ghazali-repentance-stern" locator="p. 43" />

The remedy is God's guidance and mercy received in faith, tawba and changed conduct. The Qur'an forbids despair of mercy and describes God turning toward people so that they turn back. Repentance is itself a response to divine help.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="39:53; 9:118" /> Al-Ghazali analyzes knowledge, remorse and a resolve expressed in action: learning a rule without a change of heart is not yet the complete remedy.<Cite source="ghazali-repentance-stern" locator="pp. 30–32" />

Deeds matter, but they do not independently purchase paradise. Bukhari's report denies that deeds alone save even the Prophet; mercy is essential.<Cite source="bukhari-sahih-khan" locator="6463" /> Purification and discipline are nevertheless real tasks. Al-Ghazali argues that counsel and training would be pointless if character could not change.<Cite source="ghazali-disciplining-soul" locator="p. 23" /> Grace and effort both belong to the account, though not in the Reformed order of regeneration and justification.

Guilt is answered through forgiveness and sincere return, with interpersonal claims remaining serious. Muslim's bankrupt-person report shows religious observance failing to cancel the wrongs done to others.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="4:110; 25:70" /><Cite source="muslim-sahih-siddiqui" locator="2581" /> A person's remedy must therefore reach his treatment of neighbors as well as his expressed piety.

Final judgment gives the project its horizon. Bodily resurrection brings responsibility before God; paradise, God's good pleasure and the Sunni promise of vision describe final blessedness.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="36:78–79; 21:47; 9:72" /><Cite source="muslim-sahih-siddiqui" locator="181a; Book 1, Hadith 354" /> Hope in mercy is not knowledge of any individual's verdict. Divine guidance and predestination also remain part of the Sunni account, as al-Ash'ari emphasizes; they are not replaced by unaided merit-making.<Cite source="ashari-ibana-klein" locator="pp. 51–52" />

## What this explains well

The account keeps repentance hopeful while making it concrete. The heart can change, mercy welcomes return, and the neighbor retains a claim against the wrongdoer. The Qur'an's account of God's prior turning and Bukhari's insistence on mercy also prevent reducing salvation to a contest of accumulated merits.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="9:118" /><Cite source="bukhari-sahih-khan" locator="6463" /> Genuine action remains meaningful within dependence on God.

## Christian response

The Christian shares the importance of mercy, repentance and inward renewal. His distinctive diagnosis includes guilt, answered by Christ bearing sin in the sinner's place (see <QuestionLink question="guilt" worldview="christianity">the Christian account of guilt</QuestionLink>), and corruption, answered by the Spirit's life-giving action.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 3:21–26; Ephesians 2:1–10" /> Islam instead claims forgiveness through God's merciful judgment and commanded return. The issue is whether Christ's mediation is required for just reconciliation, not whether Islam knows grace. The Christian argues that forgiveness by sovereign will alone leaves unanswered how God can be just in justifying sinners; the Muslim answers that God's justice is not compromised when he forgives whom he wills. Christian claims about that requirement carry their own theological and historical burden.

## Pressure questions

- What grounds hope of acceptance without making either deeds or felt remorse the price of mercy?
- How does divine guidance relate to responsibility for refusing the good one knows?

## Deep dive

#### A diagnosis that admits both responsibility and help

Personal responsibility does not make the person independent of God. The Qur'an's own presentation of divine turning precedes the penitents' return, and al-Ash'ari's creed assigns guidance and the ordering of outcomes to God.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="9:118" /><Cite source="ashari-ibana-klein" locator="pp. 51–52" /> Faith and deeds stand within that dependence; that is the Sunni center of gravity.

Conversely, divine action does not remove the commands to turn and purify. The Qur'an connects flourishing with purification and condemns corrupting the soul.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="91:9–10" /> The person is addressed as responsible. Classical explanations of God's action and human agency differ among the schools.

#### Repentance as an actual change

Al-Ghazali's knowledge-remorse-action sequence joins recognition of the wrong to a movement of the heart and a changed resolve.<Cite source="ghazali-repentance-stern" locator="pp. 30–32" /> It explains why merely repeating a rule is insufficient and why a sorrowful feeling without a new direction is incomplete. His argument that character can change supports the importance of discipline and counsel.<Cite source="ghazali-disciplining-soul" locator="p. 23" /> The remedy is more than either information or ritual display.

Divine forgiveness likewise does not erase interpersonal obligations. The bankrupt-person report exposes an offender who brings religious acts but has injured others.<Cite source="muslim-sahih-siddiqui" locator="2581" /> The point is morally specific: the victim's standing is not swallowed by the offender's self-description as religious. Sincere return should therefore be examined in relation to the harms it acknowledges and the conduct it changes.

#### Mercy, confidence and the final horizon

The hadith denying salvation by deeds alone is central to the comparison.<Cite source="bukhari-sahih-khan" locator="6463" /> It does not say good conduct is irrelevant; it denies that conduct independently establishes a debt under which God must admit its performer. Islam is therefore not a system of merit accounting; its central claim is about mercy.

Confidence nonetheless has limits. A promise of forgiveness to the returning person is not a disclosure of every named person's final outcome. Reports about permitted intercession and deliverance of some believers from fire add hope without making judgment negligible.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="2:255; 39:44" /><Cite source="bukhari-sahih-khan" locator="7439; Book 97, Hadith 65" /> Dependence and accountability both stand; neither the reassuring nor the threatening passages cancel the other.

The deeper dispute concerns the ground of mercy. The Christian claims that God shows himself both just and the justifier through Christ; the Sunni account appeals to God's sovereign, merciful and righteous judgment. Both appeal to divine sovereignty, so sovereignty cannot settle the question for one side and not the other. What separates them is whether forgiveness requires that justice be satisfied, which Christianity affirms and Islam denies.

The Qur'an's promise concerning changed deeds joins repentance, faith and righteous action.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="25:70" /> It does not divide an inward pardon from every outward manifestation of return. Yet the hadith on mercy prevents interpreting those manifestations as an independently sufficient payment. The combination provides a more exact test of the remedy than asking only whether Islam emphasizes grace or works: conduct matters within a relation that remains dependent on God's forgiveness.
