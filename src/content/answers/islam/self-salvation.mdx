## The view

Islamic sources do not begin with the Reformed diagnosis of inherited guilt. Each soul answers for its own deeds, while fitra names an original disposition that can be corrupted. Guidance and return are needed because humans are vulnerable to desire and wrongdoing, not because sin is an independent substance.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="6:164; 91:9–10" /><Cite source="muslim-sahih-siddiqui" locator="2865a" /> Al-Ghazali's account of desire preceding mature discernment explains why repentance is universally necessary.<Cite source="ghazali-repentance-stern" locator="p. 43" />

The remedy is God's guidance and mercy received in faith, tawba and changed conduct. The Qur'an forbids despair of mercy and describes God turning toward people so that they turn back. Repentance therefore cannot be understood as something that makes divine help irrelevant.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="39:53; 9:118" /> Al-Ghazali analyzes knowledge, remorse and a resolve expressed in action: learning a rule without a change of heart is not yet the complete remedy.<Cite source="ghazali-repentance-stern" locator="pp. 30–32" />

Deeds matter, but they do not independently purchase paradise. Bukhari's report denies that deeds alone save even the Prophet; mercy is essential.<Cite source="bukhari-sahih-khan" locator="6463" /> Purification and discipline are nevertheless real tasks. Al-Ghazali argues that counsel and training would be pointless if character could not change.<Cite source="ghazali-disciplining-soul" locator="p. 23" /> Grace and effort here should not be translated into a Reformed order of regeneration and justification, but neither may be suppressed.

Guilt is answered through forgiveness and sincere return, with interpersonal claims remaining serious. Muslim's bankrupt-person report shows religious observance failing to cancel the wrongs done to others.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="4:110; 25:70" /><Cite source="muslim-sahih-siddiqui" locator="2581" /> A person's remedy must therefore reach his treatment of neighbors as well as his expressed piety.

Final judgment gives the project its horizon. Bodily resurrection brings responsibility before God; paradise, God's good pleasure and the Sunni promise of vision describe final blessedness.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="36:78–79; 21:47; 9:72" /><Cite source="muslim-sahih-siddiqui" locator="181a; Book 1, Hadith 354" /> Hope in mercy should not become a claim to know every individual's verdict. Divine guidance and predestination also remain part of the Sunni account, as al-Ash'ari emphasizes; they are not replaced by unaided merit-making.<Cite source="ashari-ibana-klein" locator="pp. 51–52" />

## What this explains well

The account keeps repentance hopeful while making it concrete. The heart can change, mercy welcomes return, and the neighbor retains a claim against the wrongdoer. The Qur'an's account of God's prior turning and Bukhari's insistence on mercy also prevent reducing salvation to a contest of accumulated merits.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="9:118" /><Cite source="bukhari-sahih-khan" locator="6463" /> Genuine action remains meaningful within dependence on God.

## Christian response

The Christian shares the importance of mercy, repentance and inward renewal. His distinctive diagnosis includes guilt and corruption answered through Christ's satisfaction and the Spirit's life-giving action.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 3:21–26; Ephesians 2:1–10" /> Islam instead claims forgiveness through God's merciful judgment and commanded return. The issue is whether Christ's mediation is required for just reconciliation, rather than whether Islam contains no grace. Christian assertions about that requirement bear their own theological and historical burden.

## Pressure questions

- What grounds hope of acceptance without making either deeds or felt remorse the price of mercy?
- How does divine guidance relate to responsibility for refusing the good one knows?

## Deep dive

#### A diagnosis that admits both responsibility and help

Personal responsibility does not make the person independent of God. The Qur'an's own presentation of divine turning precedes the penitents' return, and al-Ash'ari's creed assigns guidance and the ordering of outcomes to God.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="9:118" /><Cite source="ashari-ibana-klein" locator="pp. 51–52" /> An account of faith and deeds that omits this dependence would misrepresent the Sunni center of gravity.

Conversely, divine action does not remove the commands to turn and purify. The Qur'an connects flourishing with purification and condemns corrupting the soul.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="91:9–10" /> The person is addressed as responsible. Classical explanations of God's action and human agency differ; this anchor does not settle every school debate by assigning Islam a single theory borrowed from Christianity.

#### Repentance as an actual change

Al-Ghazali's knowledge-remorse-action sequence joins recognition of the wrong to a movement of the heart and a changed resolve.<Cite source="ghazali-repentance-stern" locator="pp. 30–32" /> It explains why merely repeating a rule is insufficient and why a sorrowful feeling without a new direction is incomplete. His argument that character can change supports the importance of discipline and counsel.<Cite source="ghazali-disciplining-soul" locator="p. 23" /> The remedy is more than either information or ritual display.

Divine forgiveness likewise should not be presented as erasing every interpersonal obligation. The bankrupt-person report exposes an offender who brings religious acts but has injured others.<Cite source="muslim-sahih-siddiqui" locator="2581" /> The point is morally specific: the victim's standing is not swallowed by the offender's self-description as religious. Sincere return should therefore be examined in relation to the harms it acknowledges and the conduct it changes.

#### Mercy, confidence and the final horizon

The hadith denying salvation by deeds alone is central to the comparison.<Cite source="bukhari-sahih-khan" locator="6463" /> It does not say good conduct is irrelevant; it denies that conduct independently establishes a debt under which God must admit its performer. A critique that treats Islam only as merit accounting would miss its actual claim about mercy.

Confidence nonetheless has limits. A promise of forgiveness to the returning person is not a disclosure of every named person's final outcome. Reports about permitted intercession and deliverance of some believers from fire add hope without making judgment negligible.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="2:255; 39:44" /><Cite source="bukhari-sahih-khan" locator="7439; Book 97, Hadith 65" /> The public account should retain both dependence and accountability rather than selecting only the reassuring or threatening passages.

The Christian's deeper dispute concerns the ground of mercy. He claims God shows himself just and justifier through Christ; the Sunni account appeals to God's sovereign, merciful and righteous judgment. The mere presence of divine sovereignty on both sides does not make them identical, nor may sovereignty be treated as a sufficient answer only when Christianity invokes it. The comparison must explain and defend the different remedies.

The Qur'an's promise concerning changed deeds joins repentance, faith and righteous action.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="25:70" /> It does not divide an inward pardon from every outward manifestation of return. Yet the hadith on mercy prevents interpreting those manifestations as an independently sufficient payment. The combination provides a more exact test of the remedy than asking only whether Islam emphasizes grace or works: conduct matters within a relation that remains dependent on God's forgiveness.
