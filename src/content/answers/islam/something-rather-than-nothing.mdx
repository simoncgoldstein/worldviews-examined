## The view

The Qur'an's answer is direct. God is "the Originator of the heavens and the earth, and when He decrees something, He says only, 'Be,' and it is."<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="2:117" /> It challenges its hearers: "Were they created without any agent? Were they the creators? Did they create the heavens and the earth?"<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="52:35–36" /> And it insists that creation has a purpose: "We were not playing a pointless game when We created the heavens and earth."<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="44:38–39" /> There is something rather than nothing because God, who needs nothing, willed it.

Classical Muslim thinkers agreed on that dependence and then divided over how to understand it in time.

**Kalām theologians** (Ash'ari and Maturidi) held that the world began to exist, and that whatever begins needs something to bring it into being. Al-Maturidi argued from the world's origination to its Maker.<Cite source="harvey-transcendent-god" locator="p. 109" /> Al-Ghazali defended the idea that God's eternal will decreed the world to begin at the moment it did (our paraphrase of the Arabic).<Cite source="averroes-tahafut-bouyges" locator="Metaph. Disc. I, Alg. §8, approx. p. 7" /> He counted the philosophers' doctrine of an eternal world among their gravest errors.<Cite source="ghazali-confessions-field" locator="pp. 33–34" />

**Ibn Sina** argued instead that the world is possible in itself and necessitated by God, the Necessary Existent, from eternity. The world depends on God completely but has no first moment.<Cite source="sep-arabic-islamic-religion" locator="§§2.2, 3.1.1" />

**Ibn Taymiyya** took a third path. God has always been creating, one thing after another, so every created thing has a beginning but there was no first created thing.<Cite source="sep-ibn-taymiyya" locator="§4.4" />

## What this explains well

All three answers make the world's existence depend on a self-sufficient God, so it is never a brute fact, and the Qur'an's insistence on purpose makes creation meaningful rather than idle. The debate also separates two ideas often run together: depending on God and having a beginning.

## Christian response

The Christian shares the central claim: the world exists because God willed it and called it into being. The Reformed lane agrees with kalām that the world had a beginning, and it gives the same objection to Ibn Sina that al-Ghazali gave. If the world follows necessarily from God, creation is no longer a free act of will. Turretin argued that the world neither was nor could have been eternal.<Cite source="turretin-institutio-1847" locator="V.3.1–5" /> That puts him against Ibn Taymiyya's beginningless creating too. These are disagreements about creation and time, found in Christian history too, not charges of incoherence.

## Pressure questions

- If God has always been creating, as Ibn Taymiyya held, could God have refrained from creating at all?
- If God's eternal will chose one moment for the world to begin, what distinguished that moment from every earlier one?

## Deep dive

#### Al-Ghazali against the philosophers

The philosophers had argued that an eternal, unchanging God could not begin to act at a particular time, since nothing would distinguish one moment from another. Al-Ghazali's reply in *The Incoherence of the Philosophers* is that will is precisely the power to specify one of two equal alternatives. An eternal will can therefore decree that the world come to be at a particular time, without God changing.<Cite source="averroes-tahafut-bouyges" locator="Metaph. Disc. I, Alg. §8, approx. p. 7" /><Cite source="sep-arabic-islamic-religion" locator="§3.1.2" /> The text is read here in a 1930 Arabic edition with noisy scanned text and an approximate page, so it is paraphrased with the scholarly survey as a check.

#### Ibn Sina's proof from existence

Ibn Sina's argument begins not with the world's beginning but with existence itself. Whatever is possible in itself needs a cause. A whole chain of possible things also needs a cause outside it. So there must be a being necessary in itself. He called this the proof of "the truthful," because it reflects on being rather than on creatures (our paraphrase of the Arabic).<Cite source="ibn-sina-isharat-forget" locator="Namaṭ 4" /><Cite source="sep-arabic-islamic-religion" locator="§2.2" /> Because God's nature is unchanging, he held, its effect is eternal: dependence without a beginning.

#### Ibn Taymiyya's middle way

Ibn Taymiyya rejected both the kalām picture of a God who was once inactive and the philosophers' necessary emanation. God's perfection entails perpetual activity. The "genus" of created things has always existed, but no individual created thing is eternal alongside God. Our world was made in six days from materials God had created earlier.<Cite source="sep-ibn-taymiyya" locator="§4.4" /> He also held that God creates for wise purposes.<Cite source="sep-ibn-taymiyya" locator="§4.5" />

#### Two wordings of one hadith

Asked about the beginning, the Prophet is reported in one version to have said that at first there was nothing but God,<Cite source="bukhari-sahih-khan" locator="3191" /> and in another that there was God and nothing before him.<Cite source="bukhari-sahih-khan" locator="7418" /> The first wording suits a first moment of creation. The second allows Ibn Taymiyya's reading that nothing preceded God, without implying that God was ever without creatures.
