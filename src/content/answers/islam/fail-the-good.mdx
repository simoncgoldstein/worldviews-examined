## The view

The Qur'an directly rebukes those who urge others to do right while forgetting themselves.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="2:44" /> Human beings are born on the *fitra*, a God-given disposition, yet this is not a guarantee of mature obedience.<Cite source="bukhari-sahih-khan" locator="1385" /> The *nafs*, or soul, can incite evil; desire can become a person's ruling allegiance, and accumulated deeds can encrust the heart.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="12:53; 45:23; 83:14" /> Al-Ghazali describes someone who knows his conduct is wrong but whose desires have mastered his better judgment. Such a person is still capable of reform.<Cite source="ghazali-disciplining-soul" locator="p. 26" /> Temptation does not remove responsibility: Satan can call people, but cannot compel their response.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="14:22" />

## What this explains well

It distinguishes awareness from the heart's effective direction, and passing temptation from deliberate action. Al-Ghazali's sequence moves from thought and inclination to judgment and resolve: accountability is attached to the latter stages, not simply to an involuntary thought.<Cite source="ghazali-marvels-heart" locator="pp. 118–119" />

## Christian response

The Christian agrees that desire and habituation can corrupt practical judgment. The Qur'an's account of people inwardly recognizing truth yet rejecting it in pride resembles Paul's description of suppressing known truth.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="27:14" /><Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 1:18–21" /> The disagreement concerns how deep the disorder goes, and so what the remedy must be. Reformed theology holds that people are by nature dead in their trespasses, so that God must renew the will itself; correct information is not enough.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Ephesians 2:1–5" /> Islamic reform also depends on God's mercy, which the Qur'an names in the same verse that describes the inciting soul,<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="12:53" /> but it treats the heart as rusted by deeds and still capable of reform rather than as dead. Their shared account of moral failure does not decide between these views; the disagreement is theological, about the depth of the human problem.

## Deep dive

#### Different senses of ignorance

Knowing a rule, attending to it, and being moved by it are distinguishable. Al-Ghazali's person who knows better yet acts wrongly shows why an account of moral failure cannot stop at the possession of information. His analysis of thought, inclination, judgment, and resolve also explains how someone may set aside inhibitions before acting, rather than being simply overpowered by an external tempter.<Cite source="ghazali-marvels-heart" locator="pp. 118–119" />

Sunni explanations do not all assign ignorance the same role. In Jon Hoover's account, Ibn Taymiyya roots evil deeds in ignorance of their harm. Hoover notes a tension with his insistence that knowingly disobeying deserves full punishment.<Cite source="hoover-theodicy" locator="pp. 201–202" /> The ignorance in question need not be of the prohibition itself; it concerns how adequately the wrongdoer grasps the harm. Ibn Taymiyya's emphasis sits beside al-Ghazali's explicit recognition of acting against better judgment rather than replacing it.

On either account, moral awareness can coexist with a contrary orientation of desire. The fuller account of *fitra*, the heart, repentance, and responsibility is in <QuestionLink question="great-and-terrible" worldview="islam">the flagship anthropology</QuestionLink>.
