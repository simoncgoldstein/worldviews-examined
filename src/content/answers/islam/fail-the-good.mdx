## The view

The Qur'an directly rebukes those who urge others to do right while forgetting themselves.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="2:44" /> Human beings are born on the *fitra*, a God-given disposition, yet this is not a guarantee of mature obedience.<Cite source="bukhari-sahih-khan" locator="1385" /> The *nafs*, or soul, can incite evil; desire can become a person's ruling allegiance, and accumulated deeds can encrust the heart.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="12:53; 45:23; 83:14" /> Al-Ghazali describes someone who knows his conduct is wrong but whose desires have mastered his better judgment. Such a person is still capable of reform.<Cite source="ghazali-disciplining-soul" locator="p. 26" /> Temptation does not remove responsibility: Satan can call people, but cannot compel their response.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="14:22" />

## What this explains well

It distinguishes awareness from the heart's effective direction, and passing temptation from deliberate action. Al-Ghazali's sequence moves from thought and inclination to judgment and resolve: accountability is attached to the latter stages, not simply to an involuntary thought.<Cite source="ghazali-marvels-heart" locator="pp. 118–119" />

## Christian response

The Christian agrees that desire and habituation can corrupt practical judgment. The Qur'an's account of people inwardly recognizing truth yet rejecting it in pride resembles Paul's description of suppressing known truth.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="27:14" /><Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 1:18–21" /> The disagreement concerns the underlying condition and remedy: Reformed theology requires divine renewal of the will, not simply correct information.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Ephesians 2:1–5" /> Islamic reform likewise involves God's mercy; the Qur'anic description of the inciting soul explicitly allows for it.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="12:53" /> Their shared recognition of moral failure does not decide between these theological accounts.

## Deep dive

#### Different senses of ignorance

Knowing a rule, attending to it, and being moved by it are distinguishable. Al-Ghazali's person who knows better yet acts wrongly shows why an account of moral failure cannot stop at the possession of information. His analysis of thought, inclination, judgment, and resolve also explains how someone may set aside inhibitions before acting, rather than being simply overpowered by an external tempter.<Cite source="ghazali-marvels-heart" locator="pp. 118–119" />

Sunni explanations do not all assign ignorance the same role. In Jon Hoover's account, Ibn Taymiyya roots evil deeds in ignorance of their harm. Hoover notes a tension with his insistence that knowingly disobeying deserves full punishment.<Cite source="hoover-theodicy" locator="pp. 201–202" /> This need not mean ignorance of every stated prohibition; it raises the question of how adequately the wrongdoer apprehends its consequences. It should not replace al-Ghazali's explicit recognition of acting against better judgment.

The narrow point shared here is that moral awareness can coexist with a contrary orientation of desire. A complete account of *fitra*, the heart, repentance, and responsibility belongs in <QuestionLink question="great-and-terrible" worldview="islam">the flagship anthropology</QuestionLink>, rather than a survey of Sunni theology on this page.
