## The view

Islam teaches that God has sent messengers to every people, and that the line ends with Muhammad, "the seal of the prophets."<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="33:40" /> The Qur'an is not his composition: "he does not speak from his own desire. The Quran is nothing less than a revelation that is sent to him."<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="53:3–4" /> It was brought down by "the Trustworthy Spirit" to the Prophet's heart "in a clear Arabic tongue,"<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="26:192–195" /> and God promises to guard it.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="15:9" /> Twelver Shi'a Muslims, who look to a line of infallible Imams after the Prophet, count the Imams as his successors (*awṣiyāʾ*), not as prophets. The tenth-century creed of al-Saduq also insists that the Qur'an in the community's hands is the whole Qur'an revealed to him, though some Shi'a have held otherwise (see <QuestionLink question="ultimate-authority" worldview="islam">ultimate authority</QuestionLink>).<Cite source="saduq-shiite-creed-fyzee" locator="pp. 85–86, 92–95" />

Once the revelation is accepted, the Qur'an and Sunna govern (see <QuestionLink question="ultimate-authority" worldview="islam">ultimate authority</QuestionLink>). But how is it recognized in the first place? First, by a challenge to doubters: "produce a single sura like it," and "If you cannot do this- and you never will..."<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="2:23–24" /> Even if all humans and jinn worked together, "they could not produce anything like it."<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="17:88" /> This is the doctrine of inimitability (*iʿjāz*). It is not simply that the Qur'an is beautiful, but that its union of language, structure and meaning lies beyond human power, all the more so for a prophet who had never read or written a scripture before it.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="29:48" /> When opponents asked for other signs, the reply was: "Do they not think it is enough that We have sent down to you the Scripture that is recited to them?"<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="29:50–51" />

A book from anyone other than God would contain much inconsistency, and this one does not.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="4:82" /> It confirms the revelations before it, and it was itself "foretold in the scriptures of earlier religions."<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="10:37; 26:196–197" /> Where it differs from the Torah and the Gospel as Jews and Christians read them, Muslims explain the difference by *taḥrīf*. The earlier communities misread, concealed or in places altered what they had received, and the Qur'an confirms the original revelations while standing over them "with final authority."<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="5:13–15, 48" /> Classical scholars differed over how far the wording itself was changed; Ibn Taymiyya held that the change was mostly one of meaning and interpretation (see <QuestionLink question="jesus" worldview="islam">Jesus</QuestionLink>).<Cite source="ibn-taymiyya-jawab-sahih" locator="2:418–420; 5:123" note="Arabic; paraphrased" />

Al-Ghazali gave the classic account of how the evidence works together. Certainty about a prophet does not come from one marvel, "whether a rod has been changed into a serpent, or whether the moon has been split in two." It comes from knowing the facts of his life "by ocular evidence or by reliable tradition," studying the Qur'an and the traditions, and testing the truth of his teaching in experience "thousands of times."<Cite source="ghazali-confessions-field" locator="pp. 54–55" /> Conviction then forms as it does when many people report a fact and no single informant can be singled out.<Cite source="ghazali-confessions-field" locator="pp. 55–56" />

## What this explains well

The account grounds revelation in a text every believer can hear and recite, not in one disputed miracle, and al-Ghazali joins outward evidence to the inward test of a teaching lived and found true.

## Christian response

The Christian can recognize the Qur'an's remarkable preservation, coherence and influence, and still ask what shows that it comes from God. Inimitability is a judgement of literary excellence, largely inaccessible to those who do not read Arabic, as a king in Judah Halevi's dialogue objects;<Cite source="halevi-kuzari-hirschfeld" locator="I.6" /> and even granting unmatched excellence, the step from "no one has equalled it" to "God composed it" is an inference about human limits. Al-Ghazali himself warns that if eloquence of style were taken as proof of inspiration, an eloquent style could inspire a false belief.<Cite source="ghazali-confessions-field" locator="p. 55" /> The classical case is not only literary: al-Baqillani also argued from predictions fulfilled and from an unlettered prophet's knowledge of earlier history (see the Deep dive). Those are historical claims, and they have to be weighed as such. Early preservation, likewise, shows that a text was handed down carefully, not who its author was.<Cite source="birmingham-quran-manuscript" locator="release" />

Both faiths appeal to self-attesting scripture and to signs; they differ in the public sign. The Christian case rests on a datable event in history; the Islamic case rests chiefly on a text whose excellence must be perceived. Given *taḥrīf*, the Qur'an's claim to confirm the Torah and the Gospel while correcting them is coherent on its own terms. What remains is a historical question: whether the evidence supports the Qur'an's account of where the earlier record went wrong, above all over the cross. It is taken up under <QuestionLink question="jesus" worldview="islam">Jesus</QuestionLink>.

## Pressure questions

- If eloquence alone can mislead, as al-Ghazali says, what shows that the Qur'an's excellence comes from God rather than from extraordinary human genius?
- If, as Ibn Taymiyya held, the earlier scriptures were altered mostly in meaning rather than wording, what evidence apart from the Qur'an shows that the Gospels' account of the crucifixion is among what was changed or mistaken?

## Deep dive

#### The challenge and its theorists

The challenge recurs in several forms: produce ten suras like it, or one sura, or simply "one like it."<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="10:38; 11:13; 52:33–34" /> In Halevi's dialogue a Muslim scholar states the classical claim exactly: the Qur'an is "a miracle which we are bound to accept for its own sake, since no one is able to bring anything similar to it."<Cite source="halevi-kuzari-hirschfeld" locator="I.5" /> Later theorists tried to say where the excellence lies. The eleventh-century scholar al-Jurjani, "spurred by debate about how to judge the inimitability of the Koran," located it in the arrangement (*naẓm*) of meanings rather than in individual words.<Cite source="sep-arabic-islamic-language" locator="§2.2" /> The Ash'ari theologian al-Baqillani (d. 1013) gave the classic summary of the case in three parts. The Qur'an foretold hidden things that later came true, such as the victory of the new religion. Its unlettered Prophet told the history of earlier prophets and kings without having learned it from anyone. And its composition (*naẓm*) lies outside every known form of Arabic speech, poetry and rhymed prose alike, and stays equally eloquent through its whole length and across narrative, law, exhortation and warning, where even the greatest poets excel in one genre and falter in another.<Cite source="baqillani-ijaz-saqr" locator="pp. 33–36" note="Arabic; paraphrased" />

#### Early dissent

The doctrine was not uncontested in early Islam. Duncan Black Macdonald's 1903 history reports that the poet Bashshar ibn Burd compared verses of his own with the Qur'an's, that Ibn al-Muqaffaʿ is said to have planned an imitation, and that some Muʿtazili theologians resisted treating the text's exact words as perfect and divine.<Cite source="macdonald-muslim-theology" locator="pp. 149–151" /> These are reports of challenges, not proof that the challenge was met.

#### Converging lines of evidence

Al-Ghazali's account of prophecy is cumulative. Miracles "in isolation," he says, can be confused with magic, so the supernatural "should be only one of the constituents which go to form our belief."<Cite source="ghazali-confessions-field" locator="p. 55" /> He compares the result to knowledge of a fact reported by many, where certainty does not rest on any single witness. The structure resembles *tawātur*, mass transmission, on which Muslims also rely for the text of the Qur'an and the Prophet's life.

#### Preservation and inspiration

In 2015 the University of Birmingham reported that two leaves of a Qur'an manuscript in its collection are written on parchment radiocarbon-dated to between 568 and 645. Its specialist, David Thomas, judged that the text could therefore be dated "with a degree of confidence" to within two decades of Muhammad's death, supporting the view that it has undergone little or no alteration.<Cite source="birmingham-quran-manuscript" locator="release" /> The test dates the parchment, not the writing. Questions about early variant readings are beyond this page, which makes no claim either way about them. Above all, preservation shows faithful transmission. It does not show divine inspiration.
