## The view

The Qur'an describes people who deceive themselves without realizing it, claiming to reform while causing corruption.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="2:9–12" /> Others think their misguided work is good; evil deeds can appear attractive.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="18:103–104; 35:8" /> This differs from simple lack of information. The Qur'an also describes rejection of signs inwardly recognized as true and a person's testimony against himself despite his excuses.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="27:14; 75:14–15" /> Moral awareness and self-serving interpretation can coexist.

## What this explains well

Al-Ghazali's image of the heart's mirror explains how repeated wrongdoing alters perception: corruption can make vice appear desirable rather than merely hard to resist.<Cite source="ghazali-marvels-heart" locator="pp. 29–33" /> The account takes misleading reasoning seriously. Intellectual activity can serve the desire that needs correction instead of correcting it.

## Christian response

The Christian recognizes the similarity to Paul's account of suppressing known truth.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 1:18–21" /> Both accounts reject the inference that sincere confidence makes wrongdoing right. Neither licenses presuming another person's private dishonesty: a Christian cannot read a Muslim's motives off the Qur'an's diagnosis, any more than a Muslim can read a Christian's off a general teaching about darkened hearts. Where they differ is in the depth of the condition. Christianity traces self-deception to a heart corrupted by sin and in need of renewal by God; al-Ghazali traces it to a heart rusted by repeated wrongdoing, which repentance, discipline and God's mercy can restore. That is a theological disagreement about the human condition, and it is argued on other grounds than the shared observation.

## Pressure questions

- How can the heart's confidence be tested when desire has influenced what appears convincing?

## Deep dive

#### Distinguishing deliberate refusal from mistaken confidence

The Qur'anic passages describe more than one situation. Some people deny signs they inwardly acknowledge; others imagine they are doing good. One is knowingly rejecting evidence; the other is forming a flattering interpretation without recognizing its distortion.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="27:14; 18:103–104" />

Al-Ghazali supplies a developmental account: the heart's perception changes as wrongdoing accumulates. His description of the heart's rust is an interpretation of how habitual conduct and practical understanding interact, not evidence that every disputed belief has the same psychological cause.<Cite source="ghazali-marvels-heart" locator="pp. 32–33" /> A critic still has to show what evidence is being misread and why.

The relation between desire, responsibility, and the stages of voluntary action is treated in <QuestionLink question="fail-the-good" worldview="islam">moral failure</QuestionLink>. The larger account of the heart and its reform is in <QuestionLink question="great-and-terrible" worldview="islam">the flagship</QuestionLink>. Neither description diagnoses all religious disagreement, and an account of sincerity does not settle which revelation is true.
