## The view

The Qur'an gives Jesus (ʿĪsā) a place of high honour while denying that he is divine. He is "a Word from Him, whose name will be the Messiah, Jesus, son of Mary."<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="3:45" /> He was born to Mary though "no man has touched me," created by God's word "Be" as Adam was,<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="3:47; 3:59" /> and he healed the blind and raised the dead "with God's permission."<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="3:49" /> He confirmed the Torah and announced a messenger to come.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="61:6" /> But he is not God: he "was nothing more than a messenger of God, His word, directed to Mary, a spirit from Him," and God "is far above having a son."<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="4:171" /> To say that God is the Messiah is to defy God.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="5:72" /> This follows from God's absolute oneness (see <QuestionLink question="ultimate-reality" worldview="islam">ultimate reality</QuestionLink>).

On the cross the Qur'an speaks briefly. Of those who boasted, "We have killed the Messiah," it says: "They did not kill him, nor did they crucify him, though it was made to appear like that to them... they certainly did not kill him. God raised him up to Himself."<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="4:157–158" /> The Arabic *wa-lākin shubbiha lahum*, "but it was made to appear so to them," does not say how or name anyone else.

The classical commentators filled the gap with stories. Al-Tabari (d. 923) collects early reports: a disciple volunteered to take Jesus' likeness and die in his place; or the likeness fell on everyone in the house. He prefers the second, because it explains why Jesus' own companions honestly believed he had died.<Cite source="tabari-jami-al-bayan" locator="on 4:157, reports 10779–10792" note="Arabic; our paraphrase" /> Ibn Kathir (d. 1373), whose commentary represents a strong classical Sunni reading, tells of a young man who volunteered.<Cite source="ibn-kathir-tafsir" locator="on 4:157" note="Arabic; our paraphrase" /> Fakhr al-Din al-Razi (d. 1210) lists four versions and judges them "mutually conflicting," adding "God knows best."<Cite source="razi-mafatih-al-ghayb" locator="on 4:157, p. 2" note="Arabic; our translation" /> Muslim scholars have almost universally denied that Jesus died on the cross; a few modern scholars hold that he survived it or later died naturally.<Cite source="fatoohi-end-of-jesus-life" locator="pp. 1, 6" />

Why prefer this account to earlier reports? Because the Qur'an is God's speech: Ibn Kathir says that God clarified the matter in it, as the most truthful of speakers, who knows what is hidden in the heavens and the earth.<Cite source="ibn-kathir-tafsir" locator="on 4:157" note="Arabic; our paraphrase" /> Without a crucifixion there is no atoning death, and none is needed (see <QuestionLink question="self-salvation" worldview="islam">salvation</QuestionLink>).

## What this explains well

The account honours Jesus as Messiah and prophet without compromising God's unity, and fits Islam's teaching that God forgives the repentant without a sacrifice.

## Christian response

The historical comparison should be stated plainly. He was crucified under Pontius Pilate, as Paul, the Gospels and the Roman historian Tacitus attest.<Cite source="tacitus-annals-church-brodribb" locator="15.44" /> Paul wrote within about twenty-five years and knew leading witnesses.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Galatians 1:18–19" /> The Qur'an came some six centuries later. Within Islam its revelation is sufficient reason to correct earlier testimony. Historically, however, it does not supply an identifiable earlier source that overturns the first-century and early second-century evidence, and the substitution stories that interpret it come from later narrators and disagree with one another.

This is not the claim that later means false. The Muslim reason for preferring the Qur'an is revelation, so the question returns to whether that revelation is authenticated (see <QuestionLink question="revelation" worldview="islam">revelation</QuestionLink>). There is also a tension within the tradition itself. Al-Razi saw that if God cast one man's likeness on another, sense experience and mass testimony would become unreliable, and with them the transmission on which every prophet's message depends.<Cite source="razi-mafatih-al-ghayb" locator="on 4:157, p. 1" note="Arabic; our paraphrase" /> The Qur'an also presents itself as confirming the Gospel while denying its central event.

## Pressure questions

- If God made the death of another man look like Jesus' death even to his companions, how can eyewitness testimony, including the testimony that transmits the Qur'an, be trusted?
- What evidence, apart from the Qur'an's authority, shows that the first-century witnesses to the crucifixion were mistaken?

## Deep dive

#### The commentators compared

Al-Tabari records at least seven narrators. In Wahb ibn Munabbih's account God gave all seventeen companions in the house the likeness of Jesus; in Qatada's and al-Suddi's a volunteer was promised paradise; in Ibn Ishaq's the man was called Sergius; for Mujahid, "they crucified a man other than Jesus." Al-Tabari prefers Wahb's version because, had the companions watched one man change shape and Jesus ascend, they could not have been confused; this version explains how Jews and Christians alike came to agree that Jesus was killed.<Cite source="tabari-jami-al-bayan" locator="on 4:157, reports 10779–10792" note="Arabic; our paraphrase" /> Ibn Kathir instead says that those in the house saw Jesus raised, while other Christians believed what the Jews believed.<Cite source="ibn-kathir-tafsir" locator="on 4:157" note="Arabic; our paraphrase" /> The two commentators therefore disagree about whether Jesus' companions knew the truth.

#### Al-Razi's objection

Al-Razi states the problem sharply: allowing God to put one person's likeness on another "opens the door of sophistry." If we see Zayd, perhaps it is not Zayd; marriage, divorce and property would lose their certainty; and mass-transmitted reports (*tawātur*), which yield knowledge only because they rest at last on what was seen, would be undermined, and with them the prophecy of every prophet. He rejects the reply that such deceptions were confined to prophets' times.<Cite source="razi-mafatih-al-ghayb" locator="on 4:157, p. 1" note="Arabic; our paraphrase" /> His answers are that, according to many theologians, Jewish leaders crucified another man and misled a public who knew Jesus only by name, and that the Christian chain of transmission goes back to "a few people who could have agreed on a lie."<Cite source="razi-mafatih-al-ghayb" locator="on 4:157, p. 2" note="Arabic; our translation" />

#### What the earliest evidence shows

The earliest chain does not end in a few anonymous people. It runs through Paul's letters, which name Cephas and James, both of whom Paul met,<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Galatians 1:18–19" /> and it is confirmed independently by a hostile Roman historian writing in the early second century. Even the Talmud, which tells a different and legendary story, presupposes that Jesus was executed.<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Sanhedrin 43a" /> The Qur'an's denial is therefore a revelation claim set against the historical record, not a rival historical report.

#### Modern Muslim readings

Other verses speak of God taking Jesus and raising him up, and these underlie the minority readings.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="3:55; 5:117" /> Louay Fatoohi notes that the only premodern exception to the denial of death by crucifixion was a handful of Ismaʿili scholars. Modern minority views include Sayyid Ahmad Khan's claim that Jesus survived the cross and the view of Muhammad ʿAbduh, Mahmud Shaltut and others that he later died a natural death. Fatoohi himself argues that Jesus was raised alive and died later in heaven.<Cite source="fatoohi-end-of-jesus-life" locator="pp. 1, 6, 19" /> The expected return of Jesus is discussed under <QuestionLink question="history" worldview="islam">history</QuestionLink>.
