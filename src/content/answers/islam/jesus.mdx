## The view

The Qur'an gives Jesus (ʿĪsā) a place of high honour while denying that he is divine. He is "a Word from Him, whose name will be the Messiah, Jesus, son of Mary."<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="3:45" /> He was born to Mary though "no man has touched me," created by God's word "Be" as Adam was,<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="3:47; 3:59" /> and he healed the blind and raised the dead "with God's permission."<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="3:49" /> He confirmed the Torah and announced a messenger to come.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="61:6" /> But he is not God: he "was nothing more than a messenger of God, His word, directed to Mary, a spirit from Him," and God "is far above having a son."<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="4:171" /> To say that God is the Messiah is to defy God.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="5:72" /> This follows from God's absolute oneness (see <QuestionLink question="ultimate-reality" worldview="islam">ultimate reality</QuestionLink>).

On the cross the Qur'an speaks briefly. Of those who boasted, "We have killed the Messiah," it says: "They did not kill him, nor did they crucify him, though it was made to appear like that to them... they certainly did not kill him. God raised him up to Himself."<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="4:157–158" /> The Arabic *wa-lākin shubbiha lahum*, "but it was made to appear so to them," does not say how or name anyone else.

The classical commentators filled the gap with stories. Al-Tabari (d. 923) collects early reports in which the likeness fell on a volunteer, on one companion or on everyone in the house, and prefers those that explain why Jesus' own companions honestly believed he had died.<Cite source="tabari-jami-al-bayan" locator="on 4:157, reports 10779–10792" note="Arabic; our paraphrase" /> Ibn Kathir (d. 1373), whose commentary represents a strong classical Sunni reading, tells of a young man who volunteered.<Cite source="ibn-kathir-tafsir" locator="on 4:157" note="Arabic; our paraphrase" /> Fakhr al-Din al-Razi (d. 1210) lists four versions and judges them "mutually conflicting," adding "God knows best."<Cite source="razi-mafatih-al-ghayb" locator="on 4:157, p. 2" note="Arabic; our translation" /> Muslim scholars have almost universally denied that Jesus died on the cross; a few modern scholars hold that he survived it or later died naturally.<Cite source="fatoohi-end-of-jesus-life" locator="pp. 1, 6" />

Why prefer this account to earlier reports? Because the Qur'an is God's speech: Ibn Kathir says that God clarified the matter in it, as the most truthful of speakers, who knows what is hidden in the heavens and the earth.<Cite source="ibn-kathir-tafsir" locator="on 4:157" note="Arabic; our paraphrase" /> Without a crucifixion there is no atoning death, and none is needed (see <QuestionLink question="self-salvation" worldview="islam">salvation</QuestionLink>).

But if the Qur'an confirms the Gospel, why do the Gospels report the crucifixion? The classical answer is *taḥrīf*, the alteration of earlier scripture. The Qur'an accuses some in the earlier communities of distorting, concealing and forgetting what they had been given, and Muslim scholars differed over what was altered (see the Deep dive).<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="2:75–79; 3:78; 5:13–15" /> Ibn Taymiyya (d. 1328), in his *Correct Answer to Those Who Changed the Religion of the Messiah*, judged that where the earlier books as read contradict Muhammad, the fault lies mostly in their meaning and interpretation and only a little in their wording, and he denied that Muslims claim every copy was corrupted.<Cite source="ibn-taymiyya-jawab-sahih" locator="2:418–421; 5:123" note="Arabic; paraphrased" /> He distinguished the Injīl, the revelation given to Jesus, from the four Gospels, written after Jesus by two men who had known him and two who had not. On his account the crucifixion report was not forged into the text but mistaken. No evangelist, disciple or other follower of Jesus saw the execution. Those who did were a party of Jews, either deceived by the likeness, as most held, or lying, and the disciples who believed their report were mistaken, not dishonest.<Cite source="ibn-taymiyya-jawab-sahih" locator="2:302–304, 397" note="Arabic; paraphrased" />

## What this explains well

The account honours Jesus as Messiah and prophet without compromising God's unity, and fits Islam's teaching that God forgives the repentant without a sacrifice. Through *taḥrīf* it can also honour the Torah and the Gospel as revelation while explaining why the books in Jewish and Christian hands differ from the Qur'an.

## Christian response

*Taḥrīf* answers the charge that the Qur'an contradicts a scripture it confirms. On Ibn Taymiyya's account it confirms the revelation given to Jesus and what remains true in the Gospels, not every sentence of them. But his account of how the crucifixion report arose, from four writers passing on what a few onlookers believed, is a historical claim, and the historical evidence is wider. Paul's letters, the oldest Christian writings, begin about twenty years after the crucifixion. They already preach Christ crucified and pass on Christ's death as tradition Paul had received.<Cite source="martin-yale-new-testament" locator="L2 ch. 1" /><Cite source="bible-esv" locator="1 Corinthians 1:23; 15:3–4" /> Paul had also met Cephas and James, leaders of the first community, though neither is said to have watched the execution.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Galatians 1:18–19" /> Mark, written around 70, names women who watched from a distance, and John places women and the disciple Jesus loved beside the cross.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Mark 15:40–41; John 19:25–26" /> These are the Gospels' own reports, the very ones Ibn Taymiyya disputes, and how far they preserve eyewitness memory is a matter of historical judgement.

Outside the church, the Roman historian Tacitus, writing in the early second century, records the execution under Pontius Pilate as fact. He does not say where he learned it, but he was a hostile outsider.<Cite source="tacitus-annals-church-brodribb" locator="15.44" /> A much later rabbinic tradition in the Talmud tells a different and legendary story, but it too assumes that Jesus was put to death.<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Sanhedrin 43a" /> Codex Sinaiticus, from the middle of the fourth century, already contains the whole New Testament, so the Gospels' account of the crucifixion was in place long before Muhammad.<Cite source="codex-sinaiticus-project" locator="Date; Content" /> The evidence counts heavily against any claim that the crucifixion entered the Gospels through later alteration. It also shows that the crucifixion was proclaimed by Jesus' followers within living memory of the event. It does not settle what the Qur'an means by the Injīl, and it cannot strictly refute the narrower claim that the first witnesses mistook whom they saw die. Against that claim the Christian can say only that no first-century source suggests such a mistake.

This is not the claim that later means false. Within Islam the Qur'an's authority is reason enough to prefer its account, but that authority does not by itself establish what happened in Jerusalem. On the Christian side, that Jesus was crucified is a historical claim open to ordinary evidence, while the claim that the crucified man is the incarnate Son is a further theological one that history alone does not establish. The disagreement over the cross is therefore a historical conflict, and behind it lies a disagreement over revelation: whether the Qur'an's revelation is authenticated (see <QuestionLink question="revelation" worldview="islam">revelation</QuestionLink>). The tradition itself states the sharpest difficulty. Al-Razi sets out the objection that if God cast one man's likeness on another, sense experience and mass testimony would become unreliable, and with them the transmission on which every prophet's message depends.<Cite source="razi-mafatih-al-ghayb" locator="on 4:157, p. 1" note="Arabic; our paraphrase" /> He answers it, but the Christian asks whether the answers succeed.

## Pressure questions

- If God made the death of another man look like Jesus' death even to his companions, how can eyewitness testimony, including the testimony that transmits the Qur'an, be trusted?
- What evidence, apart from the Qur'an's authority, shows that the first-century witnesses to the crucifixion were mistaken?

## Deep dive

#### The commentators compared

Al-Tabari records at least seven narrators. In Wahb ibn Munabbih's account God gave all seventeen companions in the house the likeness of Jesus; in Qatada's and al-Suddi's a volunteer was promised paradise; in Ibn Ishaq's the man was called Sergius; for Mujahid, "they crucified a man other than Jesus." Al-Tabari rejects any version in which the companions watched one man change shape and Jesus ascend, since they could not then have been confused. He prefers one of two accounts from Wahb: either all in the house bore the likeness, so that no one could tell Jesus apart, or the companions had already scattered when one of them was transformed. Either explains how Jews and Christians alike came to agree that Jesus was killed, without making his companions liars.<Cite source="tabari-jami-al-bayan" locator="on 4:157, reports 10779–10792" note="Arabic; our paraphrase, with Mujahid's words in our translation" /> Ibn Kathir instead says that those in the house saw Jesus raised, while other Christians believed what the Jews believed.<Cite source="ibn-kathir-tafsir" locator="on 4:157" note="Arabic; our paraphrase" /> The two commentators therefore disagree about whether Jesus' companions knew the truth.

#### Al-Razi's objection

Al-Razi states the objection sharply before answering it: allowing God to put one person's likeness on another "opens the door of sophistry." If we see Zayd, perhaps it is not Zayd; marriage, divorce and property would lose their certainty; and mass-transmitted reports (*tawātur*), which yield knowledge only because they rest at last on what was seen, would be undermined, and with them the prophecy of every prophet. He rejects the reply that such deceptions were confined to prophets' times.<Cite source="razi-mafatih-al-ghayb" locator="on 4:157, p. 1" note="Arabic; our paraphrase" /> He then reports two lines of answer. Many theologians held that Jewish leaders crucified another man and misled a public who knew Jesus only by name, adding that the Christian chain of transmission goes back to "a few people who could have agreed on a lie." Others held that God did cast the likeness on someone, and for this he gives the four versions he calls "mutually conflicting."<Cite source="razi-mafatih-al-ghayb" locator="on 4:157, p. 2" note="Arabic; our translation" />

#### Earlier scripture and *taḥrīf*

The Qur'anic passages do not all describe the same thing. Some speak of twisting words or distorting their meaning, and in context these concern some of the Jews. Another condemns writing something by hand and calling it God's, and another speaks of concealment. Of the Christians the Qur'an says that they "forgot some of what they were told to remember."<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="2:75–79; 3:78; 4:46; 5:13–15, 41" /> Other verses, read plainly, assume that the Torah in the Jews' hands still contains God's judgment, and they tell Christians: "let the followers of the Gospel judge according to what God has sent down in it."<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="5:43–47, 68" /> Some classical scholars, however, read that command as addressed to Christians before Muhammad's mission.<Cite source="ibn-taymiyya-jawab-sahih" locator="2:424–425" note="Arabic; paraphrased" /> The Qur'an calls itself "confirming the Scriptures that came before it, and with final authority over them," and tells the Prophet, if in doubt, to "ask those who have been reading the scriptures before you."<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="5:48; 10:94" />

Interpreters read these verses differently. In a report in al-Bukhari, Ibn ʿAbbas says that the People of the Book changed God's scripture and wrote it with their own hands.<Cite source="bukhari-sahih-khan" locator="7363" /> Ibn Kathir cites him for the opposite view, that no one can remove a word from God's books and that they distort them by misinterpretation.<Cite source="ibn-kathir-tafsir" locator="on 3:78" note="Arabic; our paraphrase" /> That saying, however, stands without a chain in a chapter heading of al-Bukhari, and the hadith scholar Ibn Hajar noted that its second half may be al-Bukhari's own comment rather than Ibn ʿAbbas's words.<Cite source="ibn-hajar-fath-al-bari" locator="13:523" note="Arabic; paraphrased" /> Al-Tabari reads 3:78 as Jews around Medina adding to God's Book what he had not revealed.<Cite source="tabari-jami-al-bayan" locator="on 3:78, reports 7290–7295" note="Arabic; our paraphrase" /> Ibn Kathir held that the copies in Jewish and Christian hands had suffered alteration, while God's books as he revealed them remain preserved.<Cite source="ibn-kathir-tafsir" locator="on 3:78" note="Arabic; our paraphrase" /> Ibn Taymiyya reports that opinion on the wording ranged from no change at all to treating most of the Gospels as altered. He judged that little had been altered, and he noted that both sides agree meanings were distorted.<Cite source="ibn-taymiyya-jawab-sahih" locator="2:418–420" note="Arabic; paraphrased" /> He read 10:94 as implying neither doubt nor asking: its point is that the People of the Book possess what confirms the Prophet.<Cite source="ibn-taymiyya-jawab-sahih" locator="2:356–359" note="Arabic; paraphrased" /> From 5:43–47 and 5:68 he concluded that the Torah, and likewise the Gospel, still contained God's judgment after Jesus. Where a few narrative words had been altered, the unaltered text exposes the error, as sound hadith exposes a weak one.<Cite source="ibn-taymiyya-jawab-sahih" locator="2:435–442" note="Arabic; paraphrased" /> Anyone who claims that an earlier prophet contradicted Muhammad, he added, must establish the wording, its meaning and its translation.<Cite source="ibn-taymiyya-jawab-sahih" locator="5:123–125" note="Arabic; paraphrased" />

#### Modern Muslim readings

Other verses speak of God taking Jesus and raising him up, and these underlie the minority readings.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="3:55; 5:117" /> Louay Fatoohi notes that the only premodern exception to the denial of death by crucifixion was a handful of Ismaʿili scholars. Modern minority views include Sayyid Ahmad Khan's claim that Jesus survived the cross and the view of Muhammad ʿAbduh, Mahmud Shaltut and others that he later died a natural death. Fatoohi himself argues that Jesus was raised alive and died later in heaven.<Cite source="fatoohi-end-of-jesus-life" locator="pp. 1, 6, 19" /> The expected return of Jesus is discussed under <QuestionLink question="history" worldview="islam">history</QuestionLink>.
