## The view

In mainstream Sunni thought, one created nature explains both human greatness and human wickedness. God honoured every child of Adam,<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="17:70" /> and humanity alone took on the Trust that the heavens, the earth and the mountains refused, in the same verse called "inept and foolish."<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="33:72" /> Every child is born on the *fitra*, an original nature many scholars read as an orientation toward the Creator, and is then shaped by upbringing.<Cite source="bukhari-sahih-khan" locator="1385" /> Al-Ghazali shows how one root bears both kinds of fruit. The "lordly" element in the heart that longs to know all things also craves mastery and despotism, and the discernment that raises humans above the beasts makes cunning possible.<Cite source="ghazali-marvels-heart" locator="pp. 29–30" /> Evil is created power turned to the wrong end, and each soul answers for its own deeds.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="6:164" /> The remedy is purification of the soul through repentance and guidance,<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="91:9–10" /> under a mercy that forbids despair.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="39:53" />

## What this explains well

The account treats vices as good powers in disorder: anger is needed for self-defence and appetite for life, so the aim is to govern them, not to destroy them.<Cite source="ghazali-disciplining-soul" locator="pp. 27–28" /> That explains why great gifts and great dangers travel together. It explains why everyone falls while keeping responsibility personal, since desire matures before intellect<Cite source="ghazali-repentance-stern" locator="p. 43" /> and habit crusts over the heart.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="83:14" /> It maps knowing wrongdoing precisely, placing responsibility at the judgment that sets modesty and fear aside.<Cite source="ghazali-marvels-heart" locator="pp. 118–119" /> And it offers grounded hope: character can change, and God delights in repentance.<Cite source="bukhari-sahih-khan" locator="6309" />

## Christian response

Al-Ghazali's developmental account is a real explanation, and universality alone does not defeat it: if desire matures before intellect and habit then crusts over the heart, passion should prevail in everyone, as he says it does.<Cite source="ghazali-repentance-stern" locator="p. 43" /> The Christian presses a narrower point. The Qur'an describes people who rejected the signs "in their wickedness and their pride, even though their souls acknowledged them as true,"<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="27:14" /> and Paul describes those who knew God yet "suppress the truth."<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 1:18–21" /> The Christian reads such adult refusal as corruption in the will's orientation toward God. A Muslim can reply that early rust and knowledge short of certainty explain it too. The evidence underdetermines the choice, so this is a theological disagreement resting on revelation.

On pardon, Islamic teaching does not pass over wrongs against others: the wrongdoer must seek the victim's pardon, or his good deeds pass to the victim at judgment.<Cite source="bukhari-sahih-khan" locator="2449" /> The Christian's question concerns the offense against God himself. Reformed theology holds, as a premise, that God's justice requires that offense to be answered and not merely waived, and finds the answer in Christ, who bore it in the sinner's place (see <QuestionLink question="guilt" worldview="christianity">how Christianity relates justice and pardon</QuestionLink>).<Cite source="wcf" locator="11.3" /> Islamic teaching holds instead that no one is saved by deeds, only by God's mercy.<Cite source="bukhari-sahih-khan" locator="6463" /> This too is a theological disagreement, not a demonstrated deficiency.

## Pressure questions

- Does al-Ghazali's developmental sequence also explain adult refusal made "in their wickedness and their pride," against what the soul acknowledges?
- When someone sins fully aware of the harm, what is missing: knowledge, certainty, or love?
- For the offence against God himself, does justice require anything beyond sincere repentance and God's free pardon?

## Deep dive

#### Honour, trust and the angels' question

When God announces a successor (*khalifa*) on earth, the angels ask how He can place there "someone who will cause damage and bloodshed." God answers, "I know things you do not,"<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="2:30" /> and displays Adam's knowledge of the names, which the angels lack.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="2:31–33" /> Commentators disagree whether *khalifa* means a deputy of God or one generation succeeding another; either way, the same creature is both the one who sheds blood and the one who knows. Satan can only call: on the Day of Judgment he tells those who followed him to "blame yourselves."<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="14:22" />

#### Fitra and the will to worship

Adam and his wife confess, "we have wronged our souls,"<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="7:23" /> and God "accepted his repentance, and guided him."<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="20:122" /> Each descendant is born on the *fitra*. Ibn Taymiyya develops it furthest: the *fitra* exists at birth as a potency that becomes knowledge of God as knowing and willing mature, unless something impedes it.<Cite source="hoover-theodicy" locator="p. 41" /> In Jon Hoover's summary, a willing being "necessarily has a god toward which it directs its love."<Cite source="hoover-theodicy" locator="p. 42" /> Devotion and idolatry are thus one capacity for love, aimed rightly or wrongly; the Qur'an names the wrong aim as taking "his own desire as a god."<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="45:23" />

#### The heart and knowing wrong

For al-Ghazali the real human being is the heart, a subtle "lordly" reality that knows and is held responsible.<Cite source="ghazali-marvels-heart" locator="p. 6" /> In it appetite, anger, a demonic cunning and a lordly element meet, so that inside one skin live "a pig, a dog, a demon, and a sage."<Cite source="ghazali-marvels-heart" locator="p. 30" /> When intellect serves appetite, the result is "the possessor possessed, the lord lorded over."<Cite source="ghazali-marvels-heart" locator="p. 31" /> Each sin darkens the heart's mirror like smoke; obedience and repentance polish it.<Cite source="ghazali-marvels-heart" locator="pp. 32–33" /> One of his moral types "knows that he is not acting as he should," because desire has mastered his better judgment, yet can still be reformed.<Cite source="ghazali-disciplining-soul" locator="p. 26" /> Ibn Taymiyya is more intellectualist: "The root of what makes people fall into evil deeds is ignorance" of their harm, a view Hoover notes sits in tension with his claim that only knowing disobedience deserves full punishment.<Cite source="hoover-theodicy" locator="pp. 201–202" />

#### School differences and hard texts

In Ash'ari theology God creates every human act, disobedience included,<Cite source="ashari-ibana-klein" locator="p. 104" /> and, as Jon McGinnis summarizes the school, moral obligation rests on revelation rather than on properties reason detects.<Cite source="sep-arabic-islamic-religion" locator="§7.2" /> Maturidi theology describes a person as material nature plus intellect, the test being passed by rational enquiry,<Cite source="harvey-transcendent-god" locator="p. 27" /> and affirms real human choice within God's creation of acts. The tradition's own record includes hard verses that classical commentators read in different ways. The human created "in the finest state" is reduced "to the lowest of the low," except those who believe and do good;<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="95:4–6" /> al-Ash'ari's creed says God "causes the infidels to err."<Cite source="ashari-ibana-klein" locator="pp. 51–52" /> On these readings the honour of 17:70 belongs to every child of Adam, while final standing follows faith and God's decree.

#### A Christian note: mercy that turns first

The Qur'an places God's turning before the sinner's return: "He turned to them in mercy in order for them to return."<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="9:118" /> Islamic repentance therefore does not rest on self-reform alone, and the Christian should not present it that way. The remaining questions are whether God's turning renews the will at its root, so that sinners come "being made willing by his grace,"<Cite source="wcf" locator="10.1" /> and whether pardon needs a ground beyond repentance.
