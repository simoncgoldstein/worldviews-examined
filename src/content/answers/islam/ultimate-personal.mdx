## The view

The Qur'an presents God as intensely personal. He is "the Ever Living, the Ever Watchful," untouched by slumber or sleep. He knows what is before people and what is behind them.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="2:255" /> He is "the Lord of Mercy, the Giver of Mercy," "the Holy One, Source of Peace," "the Creator, the Originator, the Shaper," and "the best names belong to Him."<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="59:22–24" /> He speaks, commands, forgives and answers those who call on him.

The same scripture insists that "there is nothing like Him: He is the All Hearing, the All Seeing."<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="42:11" /> God hears and sees, and his hearing and seeing are unlike any creature's. Islam's answer holds both sides of that verse together.

Sunni theology made this the creed. Al-Ash'ari affirms that God truly has knowledge, hearing, sight and power, and that his speech is uncreated. The Qur'an's mentions of God's face and hands are to be affirmed "without asking how," neither denied nor pictured.<Cite source="ashari-ibana-klein" locator="pp. 50–51" /> The Mu'tazila and the philosophers, wary of anything that might multiply eternal realities, identified the attributes with God's essence.<Cite source="sep-arabic-islamic-religion" locator="§§6.2.1–6.2.3" /> Ibn Taymiyya affirmed every revealed attribute in its plain sense, as befitting God alone.<Cite source="sep-ibn-taymiyya" locator="§4.1" />

## What this explains well

A God who hears and speaks makes prayer, revelation and moral accountability intelligible. The names of mercy keep God's power from being bare power. And the insistence that nothing is like him guards against making God a projection of human desires.

## Christian response

The Christian shares the conviction that the ultimate reality is a living God who knows, wills and speaks, and also the warning against picturing him. The difference concerns God's inner life. Christians believe God is three persons in one being, so that knowing and loving are eternal relations within God: the Father loved the Son "before the foundation of the world."<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="John 17:24" /> Sunni theology answers that God's attributes, mercy among them, are eternal, and that God eternally knows the creatures toward whom he shows it.<Cite source="sep-arabic-islamic-religion" locator="§§6.2.1–6.2.3" /> The schools then explain God's acts differently. Maturidis hold that God's acts are themselves eternal and only their effects are bound to time, the doctrine that most distinguishes them from the Ash'aris.<Cite source="harvey-transcendent-god" locator="p. 175" /> Ibn Taymiyya speaks of voluntary attributes that God exercises when he wills (see the Deep dive). The Christian asks whether love can be fully actual in God without another to love. This is a theological question, and it depends on Christian revelation. It is not a claim that Islamic doctrine is incoherent.

## Pressure questions

- If, as the Maturidis hold, God's merciful act is eternal and only its effects are temporal, what is that act before it has any effect, and how does it differ from God's power to show mercy?
- How can God's face and hands be affirmed "without asking how" without either emptying the words or picturing God?

## Deep dive

#### Why the attributes mattered

The classical dispute over attributes was, underneath, a dispute about how personal God is. Identifying God's knowledge and power with his bare essence, as the Mu'tazila and the philosophers did, protected his oneness but risked making him an abstraction. The Sunni insistence on real, eternal attributes kept the God who knows, hears and speaks.<Cite source="sep-arabic-islamic-religion" locator="§§6.2.1–6.2.3" /> The formula they used is discussed in <QuestionLink question="ultimate-reality" worldview="islam">what ultimately exists</QuestionLink>.

#### Al-Ghazali against the philosophers' God

Al-Ghazali counted three of the philosophers' teachings as beyond the bounds of Islam. One was that God knows only universals and not particular things.<Cite source="ghazali-confessions-field" locator="pp. 33–34" /> A God who did not know individual people and their deeds could not judge or answer them. Al-Ghazali's objection shows how central a knowing, personal God is to the tradition.

#### Ibn Taymiyya's living God

Ibn Taymiyya went further than the kalām schools in affirming God's dynamism. He held that God has "voluntary attributes," such as speaking and creating, which he exercises when he wills.<Cite source="sep-ibn-taymiyya" locator="§4.4" /> God acts for wise purposes, which belong to his perfection.<Cite source="sep-ibn-taymiyya" locator="§4.5" /> This makes God more vividly personal and active, and the kalām theologians objected that it introduced change into God. How the many attributes relate to God's oneness is taken up in <QuestionLink question="one-and-many" worldview="islam">the one and the many</QuestionLink>.
