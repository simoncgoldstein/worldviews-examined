## The view

In Islam, people can know because God equips them to know. The Qur'an says that God brought human beings out of the womb "knowing nothing," and then gave them "hearing and sight and minds."<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="16:78" /> These faculties are a trust: "ears, eyes, and heart, you will be questioned about all these."<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="17:36" /> Every person is born with a natural disposition, the *fiṭra*, which God "instilled in mankind."<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="30:30" /> The world is full of signs "for those with understanding."<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="3:190" /> The Qur'an itself argues: it tells opponents, "Produce your evidence," and says that assumptions "can be of no value at all against the Truth."<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="2:111; 10:36" />

Classical Sunni theology turned this into an explicit account. The creed of al-Nasafi, a standard text of the Maturidi school, opens against the sceptics: "the real natures of things are validly established and... the science of them is certain." Knowledge has three sources: "the sound Senses, true Narration (*khabar*), and Reason (*aql*)." Reason grasps some things immediately, such as that a whole is greater than its parts, and infers others, as fire from smoke.<Cite source="macdonald-muslim-theology" locator="App. I, pp. 308–309" /> Ibn Taymiyya, from a different school, works with the same three sources and frames them all within the *fiṭra*.<Cite source="sep-ibn-taymiyya" locator="§3.1" /> He held, for example, that the premise that whatever comes to be needs a maker is innate and self-evident, which is why the Qur'an can state it as a question: "Were they created without any agent?"<Cite source="ibn-taymiyya-radd-mantiqiyyin" locator="pp. 252–253" note="Arabic; paraphrased" /><Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="52:35" />

What is *not* a source matters as much: the creed denies that private inner illumination (*ilhām*) is "one of the causes of knowledge as to the soundness of anything."<Cite source="macdonald-muslim-theology" locator="App. I, p. 309" />

Al-Ghazali tested this confidence hardest. He wanted a certainty that would not waver even if a miracle-worker claimed that three is more than ten.<Cite source="ghazali-confessions-field" locator="pp. 14–15" /> Reason corrects the senses; might something above reason correct reason, as waking corrects dreams? For about two months he was, in his words, morally and essentially a sceptic. He was healed "not to a concatenation of proofs and arguments, but to the light which God caused to penetrate into my heart," and the primary assumptions of reason regained "all their stringency and force."<Cite source="ghazali-confessions-field" locator="pp. 15–19" /> The light added no new doctrine. It restored trust in the ordinary foundations of thought.

## What this explains well

The account gives ordinary knowledge full confidence without naivety, and it has faced radical doubt from the inside. It explains how testimony can yield certainty, and it treats belief as morally serious: the mind is accountable for what it accepts.

## Christian response

The Christian account is very close: God made the faculties and the world for each other, knowledge of God is inborn and confirmed by signs in creation, and private inspiration is not a general source of truth. Paul's teaching that God's power and nature are "clearly perceived... in the things that have been made"<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 1:20" /> parallels the Qur'an's signs and *fiṭra*.

The difference lies elsewhere. The Christian traces the mind's resistance to what it knows of God to a corruption of the will that only grace heals, where Islamic thought gives more weight to upbringing and habit (see <QuestionLink question="great-and-terrible" worldview="islam">human greatness and misery</QuestionLink>). And the two lanes disagree over which revelation completes natural knowledge. That is a theological disagreement within a largely shared account of knowing, not a charge of incoherence, and it moves the comparison to <QuestionLink question="ultimate-authority" worldview="islam">the ultimate authority for truth</QuestionLink>.

## Pressure questions

- If al-Ghazali's trust in reason was restored by God's light rather than by argument, can certainty about reason be secured without God at all?
- If the *fiṭra* is universal and sound, why do sound minds disagree so widely about God?

## Deep dive

#### Two kinds of report

Al-Nasafi distinguishes two kinds of true report. A report transmitted by very many independent witnesses (*mutawātir*) produces knowledge that is *necessary*, like knowledge of past kings and distant lands. A report from a messenger "aided by miracle" produces knowledge that is *deduced*, yet in certainty it resembles necessary knowledge.<Cite source="macdonald-muslim-theology" locator="App. I, p. 308" /> Testimony is therefore not a second-class source. Ibn Taymiyya goes further. He holds that abundant transmission among people of sound *fiṭra* secures not only revelation but also the basic axioms of reason against the speculations of theologians and philosophers.<Cite source="sep-ibn-taymiyya" locator="§3.1" />

#### The stages of knowing

Al-Ghazali describes human knowing as a ladder. The senses come first, then the power of discrimination around the age of seven, then reason, which "discerns things necessary, possible, and impossible." Beyond reason lies a further faculty, prophecy, which perceives what reason cannot reach, as reason perceives what the senses cannot.<Cite source="ghazali-confessions-field" locator="pp. 51–52" /> This explains why he can trust reason fully within its range and still hold that it is not the highest form of knowing. His recovery from doubt was not a private revelation of that higher kind. It restored the "primary assumptions" which, he says, "have not got to be sought for, since they are always present to our minds."<Cite source="ghazali-confessions-field" locator="p. 19" />

#### How far reason reaches

The schools differ on how much reason can know before revelation. Maturidi theologians give reason substantial scope in knowing God and good and evil.<Cite source="harvey-transcendent-god" locator="p. 27" /> Many Ash'aris hold that obligation is known only through revelation.<Cite source="sep-arabic-islamic-religion" locator="§7.2" /> Ibn Taymiyya holds that the *fiṭra* knows God and basic moral truths, and that revelation perfects it by removing corruption.<Cite source="sep-ibn-taymiyya" locator="§3.1" />

#### Real natures

Al-Nasafi's creed affirms that the natures of things are real and knowable, while also teaching the theologians' atomism of substances and accidents.<Cite source="macdonald-muslim-theology" locator="App. I, pp. 308–309" /> How those "natures" fit with the occasionalism that many Ash'aris drew from atomism is a live question within Sunni thought, taken up under <QuestionLink question="order" worldview="islam">order</QuestionLink> and <QuestionLink question="induction" worldview="islam">induction</QuestionLink>.
