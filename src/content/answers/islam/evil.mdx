## The view

The Qur'an places both good and ill within God's government, while also relating what befalls a person adversely to the person himself.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="4:78–79" /> These adjacent claims require a distinction between divine government and human moral responsibility, rather than assigning good events to God and bad events to an independent rival power. Seeking refuge from harm in what God has created likewise distinguishes creation from the harms encountered within it.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="113:1–2" />

Moral evil includes a wrong direction of the soul. The Qur'an depicts Iblis's pride in refusing the command concerning Adam, and warns that evil deeds can become attractive to their agents.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="7:12; 35:8" /> Al-Ghazali explains disorder through appetite, anger and cunning dominating the heart's better direction; repeated sins darken its capacity to perceive rightly.<Cite source="ghazali-marvels-heart" locator="pp. 29–33" /> This is an account of responsible disorder, not inherited guilt from Adam.

Sunni theology does not supply one identical philosophical explanation. Al-Ash'ari affirms that God wills and creates human acts, including disobedience.<Cite source="ashari-ibana-klein" locator="pp. 51–52, 104" /> Ash'ari moral obligation is grounded in revelation, whereas Maturidi thought gives reason a greater role in recognizing moral value and relates that order to divine wisdom.<Cite source="sep-arabic-islamic-religion" locator="§7.2" /><Cite source="harvey-transcendent-god" locator="p. 27; pp. 217–218" /> Ibn Taymiyya, as presented by Hoover, treats evil as partial or relative within a wise divine purpose rather than as something God creates as purely evil.<Cite source="hoover-theodicy" locator="pp. 201–203" />

Good and ill in conditions must likewise be distinguished from obedience and disobedience in action. A hardship can test a faithful person, while a welcome circumstance need not reveal the agent's moral soundness.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="21:35; 29:2–3" /> This limits any inference from worldly success to divine approval. It also means that the question about God's government of harms cannot be answered solely by an account of a wrongdoer's motives: the explanation must consider both conditions and culpable conduct.

## What this explains well

The account preserves one divine government while distinguishing wrongdoing from God's approval of it. It can also explain why the agent's confidence is unreliable: wrongdoing may become alluring, and intellectual powers may serve desire. Al-Ghazali's analysis makes cultivation of judgment and character part of the response. The variety of Sunni explanations is itself important: a wisdom-centred account and an account of obligation established by revelation should be assessed according to their own claims, rather than merged into a single voluntarist formula.

## Christian response

The Christian shares the assertion that divine government includes events without making God the sinful agent; Westminster similarly distinguishes providence over sin from its creaturely sinfulness.<Cite source="wcf" locator="3.1; 5.4" /> That common structure exposes a shared difficulty: how is divine blamelessness established when God governs the events in question? The disagreement also concerns the moral order and the human condition. Christianity grounds evil in departure from God's law and humanity's fallen history; Qur'anic personal responsibility rejects transferring another person's burden of guilt.<Cite source="westminster-larger-catechism" locator="Q. 24" /><Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="6:164" /> The Christian claim must be defended from its own premises rather than treated as already accepted by the Muslim.

## Pressure questions

- How is creating or governing an act distinguished from approving its wrongdoing?
- What warrants confidence in a wise purpose when a particular harm's purpose remains unknown?

## Deep dive

#### Decree and responsibility are different claims

The Qur'anic juxtaposition of 4:78 and 4:79 prevents a simple division of reality into a region governed by God and a region outside his control. It simultaneously preserves moral significance for human conduct.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="4:78–79" /> Theological interpretation must explain how those levels relate. Neither verse should be used as a shortcut that cancels the other, and their relation should not be turned into a diagnosis of every sufferer's personal offence.

Al-Ash'ari's account gives divine creation of acts a central role. His creed includes disobedience and guidance within the divine will.<Cite source="ashari-ibana-klein" locator="pp. 51–52, 104" /> This makes the distinction between God's creation of an event and the human agent's blame especially important. An explanation of acquisition cannot be assumed to settle the critic's question simply by naming it; the issue is whether that relation adequately establishes responsibility and divine justice.

#### Wisdom and obligation

The dispute about moral value changes the terms of the comparison. On the Ash'ari account summarized in the philosophical survey, obligation depends on revelation rather than an independent property which reason discovers and imposes on God. Maturidi thought instead emphasizes reason's access to moral value within a wisdom-governed order.<Cite source="sep-arabic-islamic-religion" locator="§7.2" /><Cite source="harvey-transcendent-god" locator="pp. 217–218" /> <QuestionLink question="know-the-good" worldview="islam">Knowledge of the good</QuestionLink> develops that contrast. Mu'tazili justice-centred rationalism is a further position, not a synonym for either Sunni school.<Cite source="sep-arabic-islamic-religion" locator="§§5, 7.1–7.2" />

Ibn Taymiyya's account then places harms within wider wise purposes and denies that evil is pure or universal from every perspective.<Cite source="hoover-theodicy" locator="pp. 201–203" /> This differs from saying that victims' injuries are unreal. A wider account of purpose must still acknowledge the particular harm. Its explanatory strength rests on the warrant for confidence in divine wisdom, especially where that purpose is not available to the observer.

#### Disorder and the limits of explanation

Al-Ghazali's moral psychology connects evil with the heart's misgovernment. Desire and anger need discipline; cunning can make intellect an instrument of what it should govern.<Cite source="ghazali-marvels-heart" locator="pp. 29–33" /> <QuestionLink question="fail-the-good" worldview="islam">Failure to do the good</QuestionLink> addresses how judgment and action diverge, while <QuestionLink question="self-deception" worldview="islam">self-deception</QuestionLink> addresses the misleading appearance of doing well.

The Qur'an also says that people may dislike something good for them.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="2:216" /> Within the account this qualifies immediate evaluation, but it does not identify a good purpose behind every loss. <QuestionLink question="suffering" worldview="islam">Suffering</QuestionLink> distinguishes testing, consequence and expiation; <QuestionLink question="death" worldview="islam">death</QuestionLink> treats mortality's appointed place. Those distinctions limit what this anchor claims to explain without removing its central assertion of divine government and accountable human conduct.

The same distinction constrains claims about explanation. Naming a wise purpose is one thing; establishing that all events fall within divine wisdom is another; identifying a particular event's purpose is a third. Ibn Taymiyya's general account addresses the wider order. It should not be made to promise the observer detailed knowledge of each injury. The Christian reply must accept the corresponding limit in its own providence doctrine if it invokes unknown reasons while questioning the Muslim's appeal to wisdom.
