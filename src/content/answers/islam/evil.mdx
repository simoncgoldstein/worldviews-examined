## The view

The Qur'an places both good and ill within God's government, while also relating what befalls a person adversely to the person himself.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="4:78–79" /> Read together, the two verses distinguish divine government from human moral responsibility; they do not assign good events to God and bad events to a rival power. Seeking refuge from harm in what God has created likewise distinguishes creation from the harms encountered within it.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="113:1–2" />

Moral evil includes a wrong direction of the soul. The Qur'an depicts Iblis's pride in refusing the command concerning Adam, and warns that evil deeds can become attractive to their agents.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="7:12; 35:8" /> Al-Ghazali explains disorder through appetite, anger and cunning dominating the heart's better direction; repeated sins darken its capacity to perceive rightly.<Cite source="ghazali-marvels-heart" locator="pp. 29–33" /> This is an account of responsible disorder, not inherited guilt from Adam.

Sunni theologians explain this relation in more than one way. Al-Ash'ari affirms that God wills and creates human acts, including disobedience.<Cite source="ashari-ibana-klein" locator="pp. 51–52, 104" /> Ash'ari moral obligation is grounded in revelation, whereas Maturidi thought gives reason a greater role in recognizing moral value and relates that order to divine wisdom.<Cite source="sep-arabic-islamic-religion" locator="§7.2" /><Cite source="harvey-transcendent-god" locator="p. 27; pp. 217–218" /> Ibn Taymiyya, as presented by Hoover, treats evil as partial or relative within a wise divine purpose rather than as something God creates as purely evil.<Cite source="hoover-theodicy" locator="pp. 201–203" />

Good and ill in conditions must likewise be distinguished from obedience and disobedience in action. A hardship can test a faithful person, while a welcome circumstance need not reveal the agent's moral soundness.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="21:35; 29:2–3" /> Worldly success is therefore no sign of divine approval, and an account of evil has to cover harmful conditions as well as culpable conduct, not only the wrongdoer's motives.

## What this explains well

The account preserves one divine government while distinguishing wrongdoing from God's approval of it. It can also explain why the agent's confidence is unreliable: wrongdoing may become alluring, and intellectual powers may serve desire. Al-Ghazali's analysis makes cultivation of judgment and character part of the response. The Sunni schools also offer more than one resource: a wisdom-centered account and an account of obligation established by revelation, which are not a single voluntarist formula.

## Christian response

The Christian shares the assertion that divine government includes events without making God the sinful agent; the Westminster Confession similarly distinguishes providence over sin from the sinfulness that belongs to the creature.<Cite source="wcf" locator="3.1; 5.4" /> That common structure exposes a shared difficulty: how is divine blamelessness established when God governs the events in question? Christianity offers no fuller explanation of particular harms than Islam does. Its distinctive resource is the cross, where God's sovereign purpose and real human guilt meet and the outcome is redemption (see <QuestionLink question="evil" worldview="christianity">the Christian answer</QuestionLink>). The deeper disagreement concerns the human condition. Christianity grounds evil in departure from God's law within humanity's fallen history; the Qur'an's teaching that no soul bears another's burden rejects any transfer of guilt.<Cite source="westminster-larger-catechism" locator="Q. 24" /><Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="6:164" /> That is a theological disagreement between two revelations, and the Christian premise of a fallen history has to be argued, not assumed.

## Pressure questions

- How is creating or governing an act distinguished from approving its wrongdoing?
- What warrants confidence in a wise purpose when a particular harm's purpose remains unknown?

## Deep dive

#### Decree and responsibility are different claims

The Qur'anic juxtaposition of 4:78 and 4:79 prevents a simple division of reality into a region governed by God and a region outside his control. It simultaneously preserves moral significance for human conduct.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="4:78–79" /> Theology has to explain how the two levels relate without letting either verse cancel the other, and without turning their relation into a diagnosis of every sufferer's personal offense.

Al-Ash'ari's account gives divine creation of acts a central role. His creed includes disobedience and guidance within the divine will.<Cite source="ashari-ibana-klein" locator="pp. 51–52, 104" /> That makes the distinction between God's creation of an event and the human agent's blame especially important. Naming acquisition does not by itself settle the critic's question; the issue is whether that relation adequately establishes responsibility and divine justice.

Fakhr al-Din al-Razi shows how this account reads the two verses. He takes "Both come from God" (4:78) to cover obedience and disobedience as well as fortune and misfortune, and he supports the reading by argument: everything other than God is merely possible in itself and so depends on him, whether it is an angel, an inanimate thing or a creature's act. The Mu'tazili theologian al-Jubbaʾi had divided the verses, assigning misfortune to God and sin to the sinner. Al-Razi answers that "from yourself" (4:79) is a matter of reverent speech, as when Abraham says that God cures him "when I am ill" without denying that God creates illness. The Mu'tazila objected that God's wonder at the people's failure to understand (4:78) would make no sense if God himself created their understanding or its absence. Al-Razi replied that this appeal to praise and blame is met by God's foreknowledge, which raises the same difficulty.<Cite source="razi-mafatih-al-ghayb" locator="on 4:78, pp. 2–3; on 4:79, pp. 1–2" note="Arabic; paraphrased" /><Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="26:80" /> On his reading God creates the act. How the agent is then answerable is a further question, which acquisition, divine wisdom and the doctrine of moral value answer differently in the different schools, and al-Razi's arguments do not by themselves settle it.

#### Wisdom and obligation

The dispute about moral value changes the terms of the comparison. On the Ash'ari account summarized in the philosophical survey, obligation depends on revelation rather than an independent property which reason discovers and imposes on God. Maturidi thought instead emphasizes reason's access to moral value within a wisdom-governed order.<Cite source="sep-arabic-islamic-religion" locator="§7.2" /><Cite source="harvey-transcendent-god" locator="pp. 217–218" /> <QuestionLink question="know-the-good" worldview="islam">Knowledge of the good</QuestionLink> develops that contrast. Mu'tazili justice-centered rationalism is a third position, distinct from both Sunni schools.<Cite source="sep-arabic-islamic-religion" locator="§§5, 7.1–7.2" />

Ibn Taymiyya's account then places harms within wider wise purposes and denies that evil is pure or universal from every perspective.<Cite source="hoover-theodicy" locator="pp. 201–203" /> The injury remains real within that wider purpose. The account's strength rests on the warrant for confidence in divine wisdom, especially where the purpose is hidden from the observer.

#### Disorder and the limits of explanation

Al-Ghazali's moral psychology connects evil with the heart's misgovernment. Desire and anger need discipline; cunning can make intellect an instrument of what it should govern.<Cite source="ghazali-marvels-heart" locator="pp. 29–33" /> <QuestionLink question="fail-the-good" worldview="islam">Failure to do the good</QuestionLink> addresses how judgment and action diverge, while <QuestionLink question="self-deception" worldview="islam">self-deception</QuestionLink> addresses the misleading appearance of doing well.

The Qur'an also says that people may dislike something good for them.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="2:216" /> Within the account this qualifies immediate evaluation, but it does not identify a good purpose behind every loss. <QuestionLink question="suffering" worldview="islam">Suffering</QuestionLink> distinguishes testing, consequence and expiation; <QuestionLink question="death" worldview="islam">death</QuestionLink> treats mortality's appointed place. Together these keep the central assertion, divine government and accountable human conduct, without claiming to explain every loss.

Naming a wise purpose is one thing; establishing that all events fall within divine wisdom is another; identifying a particular event's purpose is a third. Ibn Taymiyya's general account addresses the wider order and promises the observer no detailed knowledge of each injury. Christian providence doctrine accepts the same limit, since the Reformed confessions also decline to explain particular harms. Neither tradition can fault the other merely for appealing to a wisdom it cannot trace.
