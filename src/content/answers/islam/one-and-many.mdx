## The view

Islam does not pose a formal "problem of the one and the many." It teaches *tawḥīd*, the oneness of God, and a creation full of distinct things that point back to him.

God is one, and the unity of the world depends on it: "If there had been in the heavens or earth any gods but Him, both heavens and earth would be in ruins."<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="21:22" /> One God means one ordered creation.

The many are real. Creation is a profusion of distinct beings, and each has its own way of praising God: "There is not a single thing that does not celebrate His praise, though you do not understand their praise."<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="17:44" />

Within God, Sunni theology faced a subtler question: God has many real attributes, such as knowledge, power and will. The Ash'aris and Maturidis held that these are distinct from God's essence yet "neither identical with it nor other than it." That is how they kept many attributes without many gods.<Cite source="sep-arabic-islamic-religion" locator="§§6.2.1–6.2.3" />

## What this explains well

The view explains the world's coherence by one Creator and its variety by his abundant creativity, keeping every creature dependent and giving the many a shared purpose: to point to the One.

## Christian response

The Christian shares the conviction that one God makes one ordered world of real, dependent creatures. The difference lies in God's own unity. Christians confess one God in three persons;<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Matthew 28:19" /> Islam excludes that, while admitting a real distinction between God's essence and attributes. The Christian asks why one kind of distinction within God is acceptable and the other not. This is a theological disagreement grounded in competing revelations.

## Pressure questions

- Does the variety of creatures reflect anything in God himself, or only the choices of his will?
- If, as some Sufis say, there is no true existence but God, how are the many creatures real?

## Deep dive

#### Ibn 'Arabi and "oneness of being"

The Sufi metaphysician Ibn 'Arabi held that there is no real existence (*wujūd*) but God: everything other than God, taken in itself, is unreal and is neither found nor finds. William Chittick notes that this restates in Sufi terms the philosophers' view that everything except the Necessary Existent is contingent.<Cite source="sep-ibn-arabi" locator="§3.1" /> Ibn 'Arabi never used the phrase *waḥdat al-wujūd*, "oneness of being." The first author to attribute it to him seems to have been his critic Ibn Taymiyya, who called it worse than unbelief because, he said, it erased the distinction between God and the world.<Cite source="sep-ibn-arabi" locator="§3.1" /> The doctrine remains contested, and it is not the Sunni creed.

#### Necessity and the one

Ibn Sina held that "from the One only one proceeds," so the world's multiplicity arises through a chain of emanations rather than directly from God.<Cite source="sep-arabic-islamic-religion" locator="§3.1.1" /> The kalām theologians rejected this, holding that God creates the many directly by his will.
