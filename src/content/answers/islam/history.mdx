## The view

The Qur'an presents history as one story under God, who sent a messenger to every community with the same call: "Worship God and shun false gods."<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="16:36" /> The line of messengers ends with Muhammad, the seal of the prophets (see <QuestionLink question="revelation" worldview="islam">revelation</QuestionLink>), and history then moves toward the Hour. On that Day God will "roll up the skies as a writer rolls up [his] scrolls" and "reproduce creation just as We produced it the first time."<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="21:104" /> The Trumpet sounds, the dead rise and "fair judgement will be given."<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="39:68–69" /> In the end "everything will perish except His Face."<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="28:88" />

Within that single line the Qur'an points to recurring patterns. "Travel through the land, and see what was the end of those who disbelieved."<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="3:137" /> "God does not change the condition of a people [for the worse] unless they change what is in themselves,"<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="13:11" /> and "there is a time set for every people."<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="7:34" /> The rise and fall of nations is a lesson in God's justice, not a wheel that turns forever.

The historian Ibn Khaldun gave this pattern a famous political form. Dynasties, he observed, rarely last more than three generations, about 120 years, as the hardiness and solidarity of their founders give way to ease and then to helplessness. When the dynasty's hour comes, "no one could advance or delay it"; empires, "like individuals, have an existence, a life of their own."<Cite source="ibn-khaldun-prolegomenes-slane" locator="pp. 348–350" note="French; our translation" /> His cycles describe states, and they run inside the linear sacred history that ends in judgement. Islam does not teach that history as a whole is cyclical.

## What this explains well

The view gives history moral weight: the fortunes of nations answer to justice, and every act will be weighed at the end.

## Christian response

The Christian shares almost the whole frame: one Creator, a history of prophets, nations under judgement, resurrection and a final day. The difference is the centre. For Islam history's decisive moment is the final revelation of God's guidance. For Christianity it is an event, the death and resurrection of the Son, who "appeared once for all at the end of the ages."<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Hebrews 9:26" /> Because the Qur'an denies that Jesus was crucified, the two histories disagree about what happened at their turning point. That historical question is examined under <QuestionLink question="jesus" worldview="islam">Jesus</QuestionLink>.

## Pressure questions

- The Qur'an says the rabbis and scholars were "entrusted to preserve" the Torah, while God himself guards the Qur'an.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="5:44; 15:9" /> If every messenger brought the same message, why were the earlier revelations left in a keeping that let them be misread and altered?
- How is God's moral judgement on nations to be distinguished in history from the natural decline that Ibn Khaldun describes?

## Deep dive

#### The end of the world

The Qur'an's pictures of the end are cosmic: "the sun is shrouded in darkness," the stars are dimmed, the mountains move and "the seas boil over."<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="81:1–6" /> Creation is not abandoned but remade, "just as We produced it the first time."<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="21:104" /> Only God endures: "all that remains is the Face of your Lord."<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="55:26–27" /> History therefore has a real end, not a reset.

#### Three generations

Ibn Khaldun reckons a generation at about forty years. The founding generation keeps the hardiness of desert life and a strong group solidarity. The second, raised in power and comfort, exchanges shared authority for one ruler's rule but remembers the old virtues. The third has forgotten them, depends on hired defenders and falls when God permits.<Cite source="ibn-khaldun-prolegomenes-slane" locator="pp. 348–349" note="French; our translation" /> The phrase about an hour no one can advance or delay echoes the Qur'an's "a time set for every people."<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="7:34" />

#### The return of Jesus

Prophetic tradition adds a further event before the end. The son of Mary "will soon descend amongst you and will judge mankind justly"; he will "break the Cross and kill the pigs," and abolish the tax on non-Muslims.<Cite source="bukhari-sahih-khan" locator="3448" /> The overwhelming majority of Muslim scholars have held that Jesus lives in heaven and will descend before the end, though this was never a matter of consensus.<Cite source="fatoohi-end-of-jesus-life" locator="pp. 2–3" /> Some modern scholars argue that the reports are not mass-transmitted and so not a required belief.<Cite source="fatoohi-end-of-jesus-life" locator="p. 19" /> Either way, the return belongs within the line toward judgement, not to a new cycle.

#### The Mahdi

Many Muslims have also expected a restorer before the end. Ibn Khaldun reports a long-standing belief that a man of the Prophet's family, called the Mahdi ("the rightly guided"), will appear and make justice prevail, with Jesus descending in his time. He also records that the reports behind it, collected by al-Tirmidhi, Abu Dawud and others, had their authenticity contested. On his own review only a very small number escaped criticism, and some scholars held that there would be no Mahdi but Jesus.<Cite source="ibn-khaldun-prolegomenes-slane-2" locator="pp. 158–159, 188" note="French; our paraphrase" /> Twelver Shi'a belief differs in kind. The Mahdi is the twelfth Imam, Muhammad ibn al-Hasan, already born and now present on earth but hidden from sight. He will appear to fill the earth with justice as it is now filled with oppression, and Jesus will descend and pray behind him. However long his occultation lasts, al-Saduq's creed insists, there will be no other.<Cite source="saduq-shiite-creed-fyzee" locator="pp. 95–99" /> For Sunnis the Mahdi is an expected figure; for Twelvers he is a living Imam whose concealment shapes the present age. Both place him within the one line of history that ends in resurrection and judgement.
