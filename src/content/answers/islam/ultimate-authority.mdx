## The view

For Sunni Islam the final authority is God's own speech in the Qur'an, together with the Sunna, the authoritative example of the Prophet Muhammad. The Qur'an tells believers to "obey God and the Messenger," and gives the rule for disputes: "If you are in dispute over any matter, refer it to God and the Messenger."<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="4:59" />

Reason is not a rival to this authority but the faculty that recognizes it. The Qur'an invites examination: "Will they not think about this Quran? If it had been from anyone other than God, they would have found much inconsistency in it."<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="4:82" /> The creed of al-Nasafi counts the report of a messenger "aided by miracle" as a source of knowledge which, though reached by inference, is as certain as necessary knowledge.<Cite source="macdonald-muslim-theology" locator="App. I, p. 308" /> Reason establishes that the Messenger is genuine and then submits to what he brings.

Revelation must also be applied to cases it does not mention. The classical scheme of the jurist al-Shafi'i, as Duncan Black Macdonald summarized it in 1903, recognizes four sources of law: the Qur'an, the Prophet's usage, analogy (*qiyās*) and the agreement of the community (*ijmāʿ*). Over time agreement came to carry great weight; in Macdonald's words, it "must put its stamp on every rule to make it valid."<Cite source="macdonald-muslim-theology" locator="pp. 106–108" note="a 1903 summary, not a reading of al-Shafi'i's Risala" /> So Sunni authority is not "the Qur'an alone" but the Qur'an and Sunna, read through agreement, analogy and the methods of the law schools.

The great theologians disagreed about what happens when reason and the plain sense of a text seem to clash. Al-Ghazali held that "a [valid] rational demonstration is never wrong." If demonstration proves something contrary to the literal meaning of a verse, the verse must be interpreted figuratively; without such proof, the literal meaning stands.<Cite source="sep-al-ghazali" locator="§4" /> Ibn Taymiyya denied that any real conflict exists between sound reason and authentic revelation, and he criticized theologians such as Fakhr al-Din al-Razi for, as he saw it, subjecting the Qur'an to alien standards of reason.<Cite source="sep-ibn-taymiyya" locator="§4.2" />

## What this explains well

The structure joins a fixed revealed text to disciplined methods for applying it. It gives reason a real role without letting private reasoning or inspiration override revelation.

## Christian response

The structure closely parallels the Reformed one. Both lanes hold a scripture that attests itself and invites examination for coherence: the Qur'an's appeal to its consistency resembles the Westminster Confession's appeal to "the consent of all the parts."<Cite source="wcf" locator="1.5" /> Both give reason a ministerial, not a sovereign, role. Two differences remain. The Reformed lane gives no human consensus the power to "stamp" a ruling: Scripture interprets Scripture, and no council or majority stands above "the truth of God."<Cite source="belgic-confession" locator="Art. 7" /> And the two lanes disagree about which book is God's word. That is not settled by self-attestation, since both books claim it. It is a historical and revelatory question, to be taken up in the Revelation & History domain. On final authority, then, the disagreement is theological and historical, not a charge of incoherence.

## Pressure questions

- If the community's agreement must validate every ruling, what corrects an agreement that has departed from the text?
- If reason can establish that a messenger is genuine, may reason also judge what he brings, as al-Ghazali's rule implies, or not, as Ibn Taymiyya held?

## Deep dive

#### The four sources

Macdonald describes al-Shafi'i as a systematizer between the traditionists and the rationalists. He accepted analogy where the reason for a command could be found in the text itself, and he made agreement a formal source. A tradition cited in support says, "My People will never agree in an error." Shafi'i law books begin each section by stating the basis of a rule "before the agreement," and Macdonald reports that the other existing schools broadly accepted the fourfold classification.<Cite source="macdonald-muslim-theology" locator="pp. 106–108" /> Because the account is a short summary from 1903, this page keeps to its outline and does not attribute wording to al-Shafi'i's own *Risāla*.

#### Clear and ambiguous verses

The Qur'an distinguishes verses "definite in meaning," which are "the cornerstone of the Scripture," from others that are ambiguous. It warns against those who chase the ambiguous verses to fix a meaning of their own, and it says that "only God knows the true meaning."<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="3:7" /> Interpreters differ on how far scholars may go in explaining the ambiguous verses, but all agree that they are read in the light of the clear ones.

#### Reason and revelation

Al-Ghazali's rule makes demonstration the trigger for figurative reading. Almost all later Muslim theologians accepted his approach, and through Ibn Rushd it influenced Jewish and Latin medieval thought.<Cite source="sep-al-ghazali" locator="introduction; §4" /> Ibn Taymiyya's whole massive work *Averting the Conflict between Reason and Revealed Tradition* argues instead that revelation already contains the sound rational proofs, so that correct reason and true revelation never truly conflict.<Cite source="sep-ibn-taymiyya" locator="§4.2" /> Ibn Taymiyya's works are cited here through the Stanford Encyclopedia, since no checked edition of them was available.

#### No private revelation

Prophecy is closed, and the creed denies that private inner illumination is a source of knowledge (see <QuestionLink question="knowledge-possible" worldview="islam">why we can know anything</QuestionLink>). A mystic's experience therefore cannot become a rival authority to the Qur'an and Sunna.<Cite source="macdonald-muslim-theology" locator="App. I, p. 309" />
