## The view

For Sunni Islam the final authority is God's own speech in the Qur'an, together with the Sunna, the authoritative example of the Prophet Muhammad. The Qur'an tells believers to "obey God and the Messenger," and gives the rule for disputes: "If you are in dispute over any matter, refer it to God and the Messenger."<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="4:59" />

Reason is not a rival to this authority but the faculty that recognizes it. The Qur'an invites examination: "Will they not think about this Quran? If it had been from anyone other than God, they would have found much inconsistency in it."<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="4:82" /> The creed of al-Nasafi counts the report of a messenger "aided by miracle" as a source of knowledge which, though reached by inference, is as certain as necessary knowledge.<Cite source="macdonald-muslim-theology" locator="App. I, p. 308" /> Reason establishes that the Messenger is genuine and then submits to what he brings.

Revelation must also be applied to cases it does not mention. The jurist al-Shafi'i (d. 820), in his treatise on legal method, the *Risāla*, held that no case befalls a Muslim without guidance pointing to it in God's Book.<Cite source="shafii-risala-shakir" locator="§48, p. 20" note="Arabic; paraphrased" /> That guidance comes in several forms. Some is an explicit text. Some is a duty the Qur'an lays down and the Prophet details, such as the number of daily prayers. Some is a ruling the Prophet gave where the Qur'an is silent, accepted because the Qur'an itself commands obedience to him. And some is left to disciplined effort (*ijtihād*), as when worshippers far from Mecca find the direction of prayer by the stars.<Cite source="shafii-risala-shakir" locator="§§53–67, pp. 21–24" note="Arabic; paraphrased" /> No one, he concluded, may call anything lawful or forbidden except from knowledge, and knowledge comes from "the report in the Book or the Sunna, or consensus, or analogy."<Cite source="shafii-risala-shakir" locator="§120, p. 39" note="Arabic; our translation" /> The four are ranked: consensus answers to the Sunna, and analogy works only where Book, Sunna and consensus are silent, yielding what is right "in the outward," not certainty (see the Deep dive).<Cite source="shafii-risala-shakir" locator="§§1309–1312, 1321–1332, pp. 471–479" note="Arabic; paraphrased" /> In later Shafi'i law books, Duncan Black Macdonald wrote in 1903, agreement came to carry such weight that it "must put its stamp on every rule to make it valid."<Cite source="macdonald-muslim-theology" locator="pp. 107–108" /> So Sunni authority is not "the Qur'an alone" but the Qur'an and Sunna, read through agreement, analogy and the methods of the law schools.

The great theologians disagreed about what happens when reason and the plain sense of a text seem to clash. Al-Ghazali held that "a [valid] rational demonstration is never wrong." If demonstration proves something contrary to the literal meaning of a verse, the verse must be interpreted figuratively; without such proof, the literal meaning stands.<Cite source="sep-al-ghazali" locator="§4" /> Fakhr al-Din al-Razi and his followers, as Ibn Taymiyya reports, made this a general rule: when reason and revealed text conflict, reason, as the ground on which revelation is accepted, comes first, and the text is reinterpreted or its meaning left to God. Ibn Taymiyya, who noted that al-Ghazali and others had used such a rule before al-Razi, denied that such a conflict ever really arises. What clear reason knows, he argued, is never contradicted by authentic revelation; an apparent conflict comes from a fabricated report or a weak inference.<Cite source="ibn-taymiyya-dar-taarud" locator="1:4–6, 147" note="Arabic; paraphrased" />

Twelver Shi'a Muslims share the Qur'an and the Prophet's Sunna but locate authoritative interpretation differently. In the creed of Ibn Babawayh al-Saduq (d. 991), one of the earliest Twelver creeds, God's "proofs" after the Prophet are twelve Imams, from ʿAli ibn Abi Talib to Muhammad ibn al-Hasan. They are "the interpreters of His revelations," immune from sin and error, and the creed applies to them the Qur'anic phrase "those in authority," whose command is God's command.<Cite source="saduq-shiite-creed-fyzee" locator="pp. 95–96, 99–100" /> They are not prophets. The creed counts them as the Prophet's appointed successors, as every prophet had his successor, and it holds that the Qur'an in the community's hands is the complete Qur'an.<Cite source="saduq-shiite-creed-fyzee" locator="pp. 85, 92–95" /> Their authority therefore differs in kind from that of a Sunni law school, whose scholars reason fallibly from the sources and may be corrected by them. Al-Shafi'i accepted the reading of the same phrase in 4:59 as the commanders the Prophet appointed, owed only a conditional obedience.<Cite source="shafii-risala-shakir" locator="§§259–264, pp. 79–80" note="Arabic; paraphrased" /> In Twelver belief the twelfth Imam is alive but hidden (see <QuestionLink question="history" worldview="islam">history</QuestionLink>).

## What this explains well

The structure joins a fixed revealed text to disciplined methods for applying it. It gives reason a real role without letting private reasoning or inspiration override revelation.

## Christian response

The structure closely parallels the Reformed one. Both lanes hold a scripture that attests itself and invites examination for coherence: the Qur'an's appeal to its consistency resembles the Westminster Confession's appeal to "the consent of all the parts."<Cite source="wcf" locator="1.5" /> Both give reason a ministerial, not a sovereign, role. Two differences remain. The Reformed lane gives no human consensus, council or holy interpreter a place beside the text. No human writings, "no matter how holy their authors may have been," equal Scripture, and no council or majority stands "above the truth of God."<Cite source="belgic-confession" locator="Art. 7" /> Al-Shafi'i also made consensus answer to the Sunna, as a witness to it rather than a rival, so the difference is narrower than it first appears. It lies in whether any human authority can be an unerring guide to what revelation requires: for al-Shafi'i the agreement of the whole community, for Twelvers an Imam protected from error. And the two lanes disagree about which book is God's word. That is not settled by self-attestation, since both books claim it. It is a historical and revelatory question, to be taken up in the Revelation & History domain. On final authority, then, the disagreement is theological and historical, not a charge of incoherence.

## Pressure questions

- If consensus binds because the Sunna cannot escape the whole community, as al-Shafi'i argued, how can anyone establish that the whole community has in fact agreed on a given point?
- If reason can establish that a messenger is genuine, may a rational demonstration also decide how his words are to be read, as al-Ghazali's rule allows, or does sound revelation already contain sound reason, as Ibn Taymiyya held?

## Deep dive

#### Al-Shafi'i's *Risāla*

Al-Shafi'i grounds the Sunna's authority in the Qur'an itself. The "wisdom" God taught along with the Book, he argues, can only be the Prophet's Sunna, which explains what God meant. Nothing may therefore be called obligatory except by the Book and then the Sunna.<Cite source="shafii-risala-shakir" locator="§§252–257, pp. 78–79" note="Arabic; paraphrased" /> Consensus binds because the Sunna, though it may escape some of the community, cannot escape all of it, and the community as a whole does not agree against it or on an error.<Cite source="shafii-risala-shakir" locator="§§1309–1312, pp. 471–472" note="Arabic; paraphrased" /> Analogy is not itself a text. It is the disciplined search for the ruling of a case that Book, Sunna and consensus leave open, and al-Shafi'i calls analogy and *ijtihād* "two names for one meaning." Only a Qur'anic text or a Sunna transmitted by the many gives knowledge certain both outwardly and inwardly. Analogy gives what is right outwardly, and those who reason by it sometimes differ.<Cite source="shafii-risala-shakir" locator="§§1321–1334, pp. 476–479" note="Arabic; paraphrased, with the quoted phrase in our translation" />

Macdonald's 1903 history presents al-Shafi'i as a systematizer between the traditionists and the rationalists who, it seems, accepted analogy where the reason for a command could be found in the text itself. Macdonald reports that Shafi'i law books came to begin each section with the basis of a rule "before the agreement," and that the other existing schools broadly accepted al-Shafi'i's classification of the sources.<Cite source="macdonald-muslim-theology" locator="pp. 106–108" /> Macdonald lists the four as Qur'an, usage, analogy and agreement. The *Risāla* itself ranks consensus before analogy and argues for each in turn rather than stating a finished scheme. The later legal theory of four ranked "sources" built on it.

#### Clear and ambiguous verses

The Qur'an distinguishes verses "definite in meaning," which are "the cornerstone of the Scripture," from others that are ambiguous. It warns against those who chase the ambiguous verses to fix a meaning of their own, and it says that "only God knows the true meaning."<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="3:7" /> Interpreters differ on how far scholars may go in explaining the ambiguous verses, but all agree that they are read in the light of the clear ones.

#### Reason and revelation

Al-Ghazali's rule makes demonstration the trigger for figurative reading. Almost all later Muslim theologians accepted his approach, and through Ibn Rushd it influenced Jewish and Latin medieval thought.<Cite source="sep-al-ghazali" locator="introduction; §4" /> Ibn Taymiyya answered that human reasonings disagree among themselves, so that referring disputes to them would only multiply disputes. That is why the Qur'an refers disputes "to God and the Messenger." Prophets, he held, tell of what reason cannot reach, never of what reason knows to be impossible.<Cite source="ibn-taymiyya-dar-taarud" locator="1:146–147" note="Arabic; paraphrased" /><Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="4:59" /> His massive *Averting the Conflict between Reason and Revealed Tradition* argues that revelation already contains the sound rational proofs, so that correct reason and true revelation never truly conflict.<Cite source="sep-ibn-taymiyya" locator="§4.2" />

#### No private revelation

Prophecy is closed, and the creed denies that private inner illumination is a source of knowledge (see <QuestionLink question="knowledge-possible" worldview="islam">why we can know anything</QuestionLink>). A mystic's experience therefore cannot become a rival authority to the Qur'an and Sunna.<Cite source="macdonald-muslim-theology" locator="App. I, p. 309" />
