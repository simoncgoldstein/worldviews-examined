## The view

Worship (*'ibada*) is the purpose of human creation: God created jinn and humans "only to worship Me."<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="51:56" /> The opening chapter, recited in every prayer, says, "It is You we worship; it is You we ask for help."<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="1:5" /> The object of worship is the one God: "He is God the One … No one is comparable to Him."<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="112:1–4" />

The opposite of worship is *shirk*, associating partners with God. Luqman tells his son that attributing partners to God "is a terrible wrong."<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="31:13" /> The Qur'an rejects even intermediaries whom people serve "because they bring us nearer to God," declaring that "true devotion is due to God alone."<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="39:3" /> Abraham, watching a star, the moon and the sun set, said "I do not like things that set" and turned to their Creator.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="6:76–79" />

Worship is a matter of love as well as obedience. Some people love rivals to God "with the love due to God, but the believers have greater love for God."<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="2:165" /> Desire itself can become an idol: the Qur'an speaks of one "who has taken his own desire as a god."<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="45:23" /> Ibn Taymiyya drew the anthropological conclusion. In Jon Hoover's summary, a willing being "necessarily has a god toward which it directs its love"; the *fitra* is a potency for knowing and loving God, and worship will go somewhere.<Cite source="hoover-theodicy" locator="pp. 41–42" />

## What this explains well

The account explains both the universality of religion and the variety of its errors: worship is natural, and the only question is its object. And it extends the analysis to the inner life, so that love of wealth, status or one's own desire can be recognized as false worship.

## Christian response

The Christian largely agrees. Scripture also teaches that God alone is to be worshipped, that idolatry exchanges the Creator for the creature, and that greed is idolatry.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 1:21–25; Colossians 3:5" /> Ibn Taymiyya's claim that a willing being always has a god is close to Calvin's mind as a forge of idols.

The disagreement concerns who the one God is. Christians worship the Father, Son and Holy Spirit and confess Jesus as God incarnate; the Qur'an regards such worship as associating a partner with God. The Christian holds that the Trinity is not three gods but the one God's own life, so that worship of the Son is not *shirk*. This is a revelatory disagreement between rival scriptures, not an incoherence in Islamic monotheism.

## Pressure questions

- If love is the heart of worship, what is it about God that makes him lovable, and not only obeyed?
- Where is the line between honouring prophets and saints and seeking intermediaries?
- If desire can become a god, how does a person know whether their worship is really directed to God?

## Deep dive

#### Worship as whole-life orientation

The five pillars give worship its public form, but the Prophet's account of excellence (*ihsan*) asks the believer to worship God as if seeing Him, knowing that He sees.<Cite source="bukhari-sahih-khan" locator="50" /> On this basis Muslim scholars describe any lawful act done for God's sake as worship. The Qur'an's call to "worship your Lord, who created you" links worship directly to creation.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="2:21" />

#### Fitra and misdirected love

Ibn Taymiyya develops the *fitra* as a natural capacity that grows into knowledge and love of God unless something corrupts it.<Cite source="hoover-theodicy" locator="p. 41" /> The capacity for love is single; it is aimed rightly or wrongly. That is why the Qur'an can describe idolaters as loving their rivals with a love that belongs to God.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="2:165" /> The broader anthropology of the *fitra* is set out in <QuestionLink question="what-is-man" worldview="islam">the human-nature anchor</QuestionLink>.

#### Love of God in al-Ghazali

Al-Ghazali holds that love of God is the final aim of worship. He defines love as the inclination toward what is pleasant, and argues that an inner sense perceives a beauty in God greater than any outward beauty, so that all lesser loves, rightly understood, are love of His work.<Cite source="ghazali-alchemy-field" locator="ch. VIII, pp. 100–103" /> His account survives in an abridged English rendering and is paraphrased here.

#### Saints and intermediaries

Muslims disagree over the veneration of saints and visits to their tombs. Many Sufi traditions practise it as honour to God's friends, while Ibn Taymiyya and later reformers condemned much of it as approaching *shirk*. That dispute is not adjudicated here; both sides appeal to the principle of 39:3 that devotion is due to God alone.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="39:3" />
