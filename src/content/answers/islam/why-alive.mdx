## The view

The Qur'an answers the question directly. God says, "I created jinn and mankind only to worship Me," and adds that He wants no provision from them, for God is the Provider.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="51:56–58" /> Worship here benefits the worshipper, not God. Those who reflect on creation confess, "You have not created all this without purpose."<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="3:191" /> God created death and life to test people and reveal which of them does best.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="67:2" /> The believer is told to say that prayer, sacrifice, life and death are all for God.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="6:162" />

Worship (*'ibada*) covers more than ritual. The five pillars give it a structure, but the Prophet defines excellence (*ihsan*) as worshipping God as if one sees Him, knowing that even if one does not see Him, He sees.<Cite source="bukhari-sahih-khan" locator="50" /> In a sacred hadith, God says that the servant draws near through obligatory worship and then voluntary deeds until God loves him, and becomes the hearing with which he hears and the sight with which he sees.<Cite source="bukhari-sahih-khan" locator="6502" />

Al-Ghazali drew these together. Love of God, he says, is the final aim of human life and the height of its perfection.<Cite source="ghazali-alchemy-field" locator="ch. VIII, p. 100" /> Worship is thus not bare obedience but knowing love.

## What this explains well

The account gives a single, clear answer to why humans exist, rooted in the nature of God and accessible to every person. It makes all of life potentially worship, from prayer to work and family. It explains the seriousness of moral choice: life is a test, and deeds matter. And it protects God's transcendence, since humans exist for God's glory without God needing anything from them.

## Christian response

The Christian shares the conviction that humans were made for God, and much of the language is close. Scripture also teaches that God gives life to all while needing nothing.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Acts 17:24–28" /> The difference lies in the relation the purpose creates. The Islamic account centres on the servant's worship and testing, with love as its crown; the Christian account on enjoying God in a communion God himself secures. Mainstream Sunni thought holds that deeds alone do not admit anyone to paradise, so the Christian does not charge Islam with merit-only religion. The Christian question concerns whether the test can be passed by those who fail it without a ground of pardon beyond God's will to forgive. That is a theological disagreement treated in <QuestionLink question="self-salvation" worldview="islam">the salvation anchor</QuestionLink>.

## Pressure questions

- If God needs nothing, does creation have a purpose for God, or only for the creatures?
- Is the love of God al-Ghazali describes the essence of worship or one of its rewards?
- If God does not punish "until We have sent a messenger,"<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="17:15" /> is someone tested fairly who met the message only in a distorted form? Christians face the same question about those who hear the gospel badly or not at all.

## Deep dive

#### Worship as the end of creation

The Qur'an's statement that God created jinn and humans only to worship Him is usually read together with its denial that God needs anything.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="51:56–58" /> Commentators therefore describe worship as the fulfilment of the creature, the purpose its nature was made for, rather than a service God requires. The people are addressed to worship their Lord who created them, so that they may be mindful of Him.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="2:21" /> Worship and created nature are linked, which is why the *fitra* can be described as an orientation toward God; see <QuestionLink question="what-is-man" worldview="islam">the human-nature anchor</QuestionLink>.

#### Does God act for purposes?

The theological schools disagree over whether God's acts have purposes at all. In Ash'ari theology God acts as the absolute owner of creation, and His acts are not to be explained by ends that bind Him.<Cite source="sep-arabic-islamic-religion" locator="§5" /> Ibn Taymiyya held instead that God acts for wise purposes (*hikma*), while needing nothing from creatures.<Cite source="hoover-theodicy" locator="pp. 41–44" /> The contrast is stated here at the level of these secondary accounts; it affects how "purpose" is understood, not the shared claim that humans exist to worship God.

#### Love as the final aim

Al-Ghazali complains that some theologians reduced love of God to obedience. For him love is real love, the inclination of the soul toward what it finds beautiful and perfect, and God is its truest object.<Cite source="ghazali-alchemy-field" locator="ch. VIII, pp. 100–103" /> The *Alchemy* is an abridged translation of an Urdu version, so this account is paraphrased. Its claim is clear enough: the test of life is meant to bring the soul to love. What that love finally receives, paradise and the vision of God, is treated in <QuestionLink question="final-end" worldview="islam">the final-end question</QuestionLink>.
