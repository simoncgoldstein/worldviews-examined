## The view

The Qur'an presents death and life as created by God to test human conduct, and pairs the universal experience of death with final recompense.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="67:2; 3:185" /> Mortality is therefore neither an independent power outside God's rule nor a morally purposeless end to the account. Both favorable and unfavorable conditions belong to testing.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="21:35" />

Death cannot be escaped by location or defensive strength. The Qur'an also describes an appointed term that cannot simply be postponed or advanced when it arrives.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="4:78; 62:8; 16:61" /> This gives death a place in divine government without treating every death as evidence of inherited guilt. The governing explanation is God's appointment of finite life, testing and return to him, rather than the Reformed account of death through Adam's transgression.

## What this explains well

Mortality can painfully interrupt earthly hopes, but within this account that loss does not make death a defeat of divine justice. The created test and final recompense supply its wider meaning.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="67:2; 3:185" />

The account also gives finite life urgency. The person who wants to return at death to amend past conduct is denied that return; a barrier remains until resurrection.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="23:99–100" /> Mortality thus places a limit on deferral. Human conduct cannot be indefinitely postponed on the assumption that another opportunity will always come. The theme connects with al-Ghazali's warning against heedlessness and his emphasis on attending to one's inner life.<Cite source="ghazali-marvels-heart" locator="pp. 29–33" />

## Christian response

The Christian shares finite life's seriousness and accountability before God, but understands death as an enemy within a condition introduced by sin.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 5:12; 1 Corinthians 15:26" /> The difference is whether death is part of the created test or an intruder into creation. The Qur'an places death among God's created works, appointed for testing and return to him; Christianity holds that death entered through sin and is an enemy, defeated by Christ's resurrection and finally to be destroyed (see <QuestionLink question="death" worldview="christianity">the Christian answer</QuestionLink>). The Christian therefore presses the question of death's painful character. If death is God's created instrument of testing, its pain belongs to that design; if it is an enemy, grief is a fitting protest against it. Muslims grieve, and Christians affirm physical mortality; the disagreement concerns the larger account of humanity, and neither view licenses claiming the particular divine purpose behind a bereavement.

## Pressure questions

- How does an appointed term relate to meaningful efforts to preserve life?
- What explains mortality's painful character within a divinely created test?

## Deep dive

#### A limit to postponement

The Qur'anic passages distinguish the certainty of mortality from knowledge of its exact time. Saying that the term is appointed does not mean that human beings possess that timetable. Likewise, acknowledging God's rule is different from claiming to know why a specific person dies at a specific age. The description of death as an inescapable encounter with truth directs attention to the person's attempts to avoid facing it.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="50:19" />

Testing also means more than exposure to hardship. Since good as well as bad circumstances test people, prolonged comfort is not itself proof that one's life is morally sound. The point bears on mortality: a longer life is additional opportunity, but length alone does not measure the quality of the response. <QuestionLink question="suffering" worldview="islam">The suffering answer</QuestionLink> distinguishes testing, consequence, expiation and the believer's response rather than applying them indiscriminately to every loss.

The passage about a denied return names the seriousness of completed earthly opportunity.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="23:99–100" /> It supplies a canonical basis for urgency without requiring speculation about the detailed experience of the intermediate state. Those details belong to <QuestionLink question="after-death" worldview="islam">the after-death question</QuestionLink>. Al-Ghazali's account of heedlessness helps explain why knowledge of mortality may fail to govern action; it is a contribution about attention and conduct, not a substitute for the Qur'an's teaching about death.

The Islamic claim is limited but substantial: death occurs within God's created and appointed order, and human life is answerable to him. The dispute with Christianity concerns how this framework explains death's relation to evil and the human condition. <QuestionLink question="evil" worldview="islam">The evil anchor</QuestionLink> treats divine justice and wisdom more directly.
