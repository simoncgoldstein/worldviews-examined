## The view

The heart of Islam's answer is *tawḥīd*, the oneness of God. A short chapter of the Qur'an, often called its essence, says it all: God is One and eternal, he begot no one and was not begotten, and "no one is comparable to Him."<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="112:1–4" /> He is "the First and the Last; the Outer and the Inner."<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="57:3" /> Human beings stand in need of God, while "God needs nothing."<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="35:15" />

Everything other than God is created. God is "the Creator of all things,"<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="13:16" /> and when he wills something, he says "Be," and it is.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="36:82" /> Everything will perish except his Face.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="28:88" /> So reality is God and his creation, and the two are never confused: "There is nothing like Him."<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="42:11" />

This God is not a bare abstraction. The Sunni creed, in al-Ash'ari's statement, affirms that God truly knows, hears, sees, has power and speaks. The Qur'an's language of God's face and hands is to be affirmed "without asking how," neither explained away nor pictured in human terms.<Cite source="ashari-ibana-klein" locator="pp. 50–51" />

Classical thinkers then divided over how to describe this. Ash'ari and Maturidi theologians held that God's attributes are real, distinct from his essence, and yet not something "other than" God. The Mu'tazila and the philosophers identified them with his essence.<Cite source="sep-arabic-islamic-religion" locator="§§6.2.1–6.2.3" /> Ibn Sina offered the most influential philosophical vocabulary, describing God as the one being whose existence is necessary in itself. That system was a powerful alternative within Islamic thought, not the creed.

## What this explains well

The view gives reality a single source and a single ruler, so the world is one order rather than a contest of powers. It draws a sharp line between Creator and creature, which guards against worshipping anything created. It explains the world's fragility, since all of it exists only as long as God wills. And it keeps God's transcendence and his knowing, speaking presence together.

## Christian response

The Christian shares much of this: one Creator, a real and wholly dependent creation, creation by God's word, and God's absolute independence. The disagreement concerns who God is. The Qur'an denies that God begets or is begotten,<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="112:1–4" /> and Christians confess Father, Son and Spirit as one God.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Matthew 28:19" /> This is a disagreement between competing claims to revelation, addressed in Revelation & History; it is not a contradiction within Islam.

There is also a shared problem. Sunni theology affirms many real attributes in God that are neither identical with him nor other than him. That is itself a claim about distinction within divine unity. Christians face a parallel question in the Trinity. The Christian asks why one kind of real distinction in God is acceptable and the other impossible. This is a question about consistency, not a proof.

## Pressure questions

- If God's attributes are neither identical with him nor other than him, what kind of distinction is that? Could there be distinctions of persons within the one God?
- If, as Ibn Sina held, the world necessarily follows from God, how does creation remain a free act of God's will?

## Deep dive

#### The attributes debate

The Qur'an names God by many names: the Ever-Living, the All Hearing, the All Seeing, the Merciful.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="42:11" /> The Mu'tazila feared that eternal attributes distinct from God would be additional eternal beings, which would compromise *tawḥīd*. So they identified the attributes with God's essence. The Ash'aris replied that the attributes are real and distinct from the essence, yet "neither identical with it nor other than it." The Maturidis held a similar position.<Cite source="sep-arabic-islamic-religion" locator="§§6.2.1–6.2.3" /> Ibn Taymiyya, a Hanbali traditionalist, rejected both philosophical identity and theological distinctions he thought unscriptural. He affirmed every revealed attribute in its plain sense, as uniquely befitting God.<Cite source="sep-ibn-taymiyya" locator="§4.1" />

#### Ibn Sina's Necessary Existent

Ibn Sina divided everything that exists into two kinds. Some things are possible in themselves and need a cause to exist. One being is necessary in itself. He argued that any chain of possible things, taken as a whole, needs a cause outside itself, and that this cause must be necessary through itself. He called this the proof of "the truthful," because it reasons from existence as such rather than from the world's beginning (our paraphrase of the Arabic).<Cite source="ibn-sina-isharat-forget" locator="Namaṭ 4" /><Cite source="sep-arabic-islamic-religion" locator="§2.2" /> On his view the world proceeds eternally from God, and that is why al-Ghazali and the kalām theologians opposed him. The dispute is treated in <QuestionLink question="something-rather-than-nothing" worldview="islam">why there is anything at all</QuestionLink>.

#### Arguments from the world

Al-Maturidi argued from the world to its Maker. Things cannot bring themselves into existence. Opposed natures could not combine and stay ordered without one wise arranger. And the Qur'an's own argument is that rival gods would ruin heaven and earth.<Cite source="harvey-transcendent-god" locator="pp. 120–121" /> These are arguments from creation's dependence, not from Ibn Sina's metaphysics of necessity.

#### God, and nothing besides him

Asked about the beginning of creation, the Prophet is reported to have said that at first there was nothing but God. Then his throne was over the water, he wrote everything in the Book, and he created the heavens and the earth.<Cite source="bukhari-sahih-khan" locator="3191" /> It sums up the Sunni picture of ultimate reality: God alone, then creation by his will. A Sufi current associated with Ibn 'Arabi went further and held that there is no true existence except God. The label "oneness of being" for this view was first fixed on him by his critic Ibn Taymiyya.<Cite source="sep-ibn-arabi" locator="§3.1" /> That contested strand is discussed in <QuestionLink question="one-and-many" worldview="islam">the one and the many</QuestionLink>.
