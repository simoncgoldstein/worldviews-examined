## The view

The Qur'an speaks of a created human disposition, the soul's awareness of rebellion and piety, and a self-reproaching soul.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="30:30; 91:7–10; 75:2" /> Bukhari's report about children born on the *fitra* supplies a related account of an original orientation rather than a guarantee of perfect adult conduct.<Cite source="bukhari-sahih-khan" locator="1385" /> A report in Muslim connects vice to what troubles the heart and what a person would not wish others to discover.<Cite source="muslim-sahih-siddiqui" locator="2553a" />

These passages establish moral awareness, but do not by themselves settle every question about rational obligation. Ash'ari accounts locate binding obligation in revelation; Mu'tazili accounts affirm rationally knowable moral values and stronger requirements of divine justice. Maturidi accounts give reason a substantial role in recognizing good and bad while affirming the authority of revelation.<Cite source="sep-arabic-islamic-religion" locator="§§7.1–7.2" /><Cite source="harvey-transcendent-god" locator="pp. 217–218" /> Mu'tazila is a comparative contrast, not a synonym for the Sunni lane.

## What this explains well

The account connects moral awareness with guidance, responsibility, and the possibility of distorted judgment. It does not treat human beings as lacking every moral orientation until they learn a command. Nor does it identify the discomfort of conscience with an infallible verdict. The Qur'an's description of people calling corruption reform shows that confidence can coexist with serious error.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="2:9–12" /> Revelation and disciplined interpretation are therefore meaningful sources of correction, not decorative additions to self-approval.

## Christian response

The Christian recognizes the similarities to conscience and its distortion. Paul likewise describes accusing and defending judgments alongside suppression of known truth.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 2:14–15; Romans 1:18–21" /> The precise disagreement varies with the school: an Ash'ari account of obligation poses different questions from a Maturidi account of rational discernment. Reformed Christianity grounds moral obligation in God's law and character while distinguishing that authority from the agent's reliable perception of it.<Cite source="wcf" locator="19.1–2; 20.2" /> A comparison must test the actual account of authority; it should not make school diversity itself an objection.

## Pressure questions

- When reason, inward conviction, and a received interpretation diverge, which standard adjudicates them?
- How does the school distinguish recognizing benefit from knowing a binding moral duty?

## Deep dive

#### Moral awareness is not yet a theory of obligation

It is possible to recognize that an action benefits or injures someone while disagreeing about what makes it obligatory or forbidden. The school distinctions concern this further issue, not a claim that some Muslim theologians thought people never perceive suffering or advantage. The repository's philosophical survey separates these moral-value debates from broader claims about God's power and wisdom.<Cite source="sep-arabic-islamic-religion" locator="§§7.1–7.2" />

Harvey's reading of al-Maturidi emphasizes wisdom-grounded moral knowledge; reason is not simply abolished, although revelation remains authoritative.<Cite source="harvey-transcendent-god" locator="p. 27; pp. 217–218" /> The Ash'ari contrast must therefore not stand for every Sunni position. Likewise, the stronger Mu'tazili rational account should not be silently substituted for classical Sunni theology merely because it resembles another lane's preferred premises.

There is a separate problem of reliability. The Qur'an can speak both of recognition and denial: some reject signs which they inwardly acknowledge.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="27:14" /> Knowledge of an obligation need not determine the will's response. That is why the question about the source of moral awareness cannot settle the question of why people act against it.
