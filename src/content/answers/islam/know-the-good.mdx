## The view

The Qur'an speaks of a created human disposition, the soul's awareness of rebellion and piety, and a self-reproaching soul.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="30:30; 91:7–10; 75:2" /> Bukhari's report about children born on the *fitra* supplies a related account of an original orientation rather than a guarantee of perfect adult conduct.<Cite source="bukhari-sahih-khan" locator="1385" /> A report in Muslim connects vice to what troubles the heart and what a person would not wish others to discover.<Cite source="muslim-sahih-siddiqui" locator="2553a" />

The schools then differ over what reason can know and what makes obligation binding. Ash'ari accounts locate binding obligation in revelation; Mu'tazili accounts affirm rationally knowable moral values and stronger requirements of divine justice. Maturidi accounts give reason a substantial role in recognizing good and bad while affirming the authority of revelation.<Cite source="sep-arabic-islamic-religion" locator="§§7.1–7.2" /><Cite source="harvey-transcendent-god" locator="pp. 217–218" /> Mu'tazili rationalism is an important alternative within Islamic theology; the classical Sunni mainstream is Ash'ari and Maturidi.

## What this explains well

The account connects moral awareness with guidance, responsibility, and the possibility of distorted judgment. Human beings are not blank until they learn a command, yet the discomfort of conscience is not an infallible verdict. The Qur'an's description of people calling corruption reform shows that confidence can coexist with serious error.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="2:9–12" /> That is why revelation and disciplined interpretation are needed to correct it.

## Christian response

The Christian recognizes the similarities in the account of conscience and its distortion. Paul likewise describes conscience accusing and excusing alongside the suppression of known truth.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 2:14–15; Romans 1:18–21" /> Reformed Christianity grounds moral obligation in God's law and character, and distinguishes that authority from how reliably anyone perceives it.<Cite source="wcf" locator="19.1–2; 20.2" /> On its account the moral law is commanded because it is just, rooted in God's own holiness, and it is written on the heart before any prophet speaks.<Cite source="turretin-institutio-1847" locator="XI.1.4, 7" note="Latin; paraphrased" /> The point of difference therefore depends on the school. Against an Ash'ari account, in which binding obligation comes from revelation, the Christian holds that conscience already registers real obligation. With a Maturidi account, which gives reason a substantial role in discerning good and bad, the two agree more than they differ about moral knowledge; the disagreement moves to which revelation corrects conscience and who the God is whose character the law expresses. Both are theological disagreements about the ground of obligation (see <QuestionLink question="know-the-good" worldview="christianity">the Christian answer</QuestionLink>).

## Pressure questions

- When reason, inward conviction, and a received interpretation diverge, which standard adjudicates them?
- How does the school distinguish recognizing benefit from knowing a binding moral duty?

## Deep dive

#### Moral awareness is not yet a theory of obligation

It is possible to recognize that an action benefits or injures someone while disagreeing about what makes it obligatory or forbidden. The school debates concern that further issue, not whether people can perceive suffering or advantage. The philosophical survey separates these moral-value debates from broader claims about God's power and wisdom.<Cite source="sep-arabic-islamic-religion" locator="§§7.1–7.2" />

Harvey's reading of al-Maturidi emphasizes wisdom-grounded moral knowledge; reason is not simply abolished, although revelation remains authoritative.<Cite source="harvey-transcendent-god" locator="p. 27; pp. 217–218" /> The Ash'ari position therefore does not speak for every Sunni theologian. The stronger Mu'tazili rational account, though closer to some Christian natural-law premises, is not classical Sunni theology either.

There is a separate problem of reliability. The Qur'an can speak both of recognition and denial: some reject signs which they inwardly acknowledge.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="27:14" /> Knowing an obligation does not settle how the will responds, so knowing where moral awareness comes from does not explain why people act against it.
