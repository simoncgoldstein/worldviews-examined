## The view

The Qur'an presents spouses and kinship as signs of God's care. God created humanity "from a single soul, and from it created its mate," spreading men and women across the earth, and warns against "severing the ties of kinship."<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="4:1" /> Among His signs is that He created spouses "for you to live with in tranquillity" and "ordained love and kindness between you."<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="30:21" /> Through spouses He gives children and grandchildren.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="16:72" /> Children are also a test: wealth and children are "only a test for you."<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="64:15" />

Marriage is the normal path. Earlier messengers were given wives and offspring,<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="13:38" /> and monasticism was something Christians invented, not something God ordained.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="57:27" /> The Prophet rejected a vow of perpetual celibacy, saying that he himself married, and urged young people who could afford it to marry.<Cite source="bukhari-sahih-khan" locator="5063; 5065" /> Parents stand next to God: "worship none but Him, and … be kind to your parents," especially in their old age.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="17:23–24" />

## What this explains well

The account joins love, mercy and duty in marriage. It explains strong family solidarity, including care for ageing parents, and it treats children as both gift and responsibility.

## Christian response

The Christian shares the view of marriage as a sign of God's goodness and of children as a trust. The difference lies in singleness. Islam rejects vowed celibacy, while Paul commends a gift of singleness for devotion to the Lord.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="1 Corinthians 7:7, 32–35" /> Al-Ghazali's own case-by-case weighing shows that Islam does not treat marriage as unconditionally superior for every person, so the gap is narrower than it first appears.

## Pressure questions

- If marriage is the way of the prophets, what is the place of a believer who cannot marry?
- When does love for spouse and children become a rival to love of God?

## Deep dive

#### Al-Ghazali's weighing

Al-Ghazali lists marriage's benefits: it multiplies those who worship God, children may intercede for their parents, companionship refreshes the heart for devotion, the household is managed, and patience with a spouse is itself part of religion. He also sets out its disadvantages for a devotee, above all the risk of distraction from God, and leaves each person to weigh them.<Cite source="ghazali-alchemy-field" locator="ch. VII, pp. 87–90" /> His treatment survives in an abridged English rendering and is paraphrased here; its household roles reflect his time.

#### Love, test and rival

The Qur'an warns that spouses and children can sometimes act as enemies to faith, while urging forgiveness of their faults.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="64:14" /> Wealth and children are "the attractions of this worldly life," but lasting good works are better.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="18:46" /> Family is good, but not ultimate. Polygyny, divorce and gender roles in Islamic law are outside the scope of this answer.
