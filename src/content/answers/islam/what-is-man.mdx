## The view

The Qur'an describes the human being as made by God from clay. God tells the angels that he is creating a mortal from dried clay formed from dark mud, and that when he has fashioned him and breathed his spirit into him they are to bow down.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="15:28–29" /> God began the creation of man from clay, made his descendants from a lowly fluid, then shaped him, breathed into him from his spirit and gave him hearing, sight and minds.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="32:7–9" /> The body is God's crafted work, made "in the finest state."<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="95:4" />

The human is distinguished by honour, knowledge and trust. God has honoured the children of Adam and favoured them above much of creation.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="17:70" /> Adam is appointed a *khalifa* on earth and taught the names of things that the angels do not know.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="2:30–33" /> Every child is born on the *fitra*, an original nature many scholars read as an orientation toward the Creator, and is then shaped by its upbringing.<Cite source="bukhari-sahih-khan" locator="1385" /> The soul is formed and inspired to know its own rebellion and piety, and succeeds or fails as it is purified or corrupted.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="91:7–10" />

Al-Ghazali gives the classical psychology. The real human being, the part that knows, is addressed and is held responsible, is the heart: not the physical organ but a subtle, "lordly" spiritual reality connected to it. The same reality is also called spirit, soul and intellect in different respects.<Cite source="ghazali-marvels-heart" locator="pp. 6–8" /> Humans stand between beasts and angels. They share appetite and anger with animals, but knowledge and the capacity to know God lift them toward the angels.<Cite source="ghazali-marvels-heart" locator="p. 25" />

## What this explains well

The account joins dependence and dignity. Humans are made of the same earth as everything else and remain wholly God's servants, yet they are honoured, taught and entrusted. It explains why moral and religious awareness appears so widely: the *fitra* is part of what a human is, not a later addition. It locates identity in a knowing, willing center while treating the body as God's good craftsmanship. And it explains the doubleness of human life as one nature that can rise toward the angels or sink below the beasts.

## Christian response

The Christian agrees with much: creation from earth, a divinely given life, a dignity that belongs to every child of Adam, and a heart that is the center of the person. Scripture uses similar language of dust and breath.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Genesis 2:7" />

The difference concerns the relation in which the creature stands. The Qur'an speaks of honour, stewardship and breathed spirit rather than of a divine image, and insists that "there is nothing like Him."<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="42:11" /> A sound hadith does say that God created Adam in his form (*sura*), but whether "his" refers to God or to Adam is disputed among Muslim interpreters, so it does not supply an agreed doctrine of a divine image.<Cite source="bukhari-sahih-khan" locator="6227" /> The Christian holds that humans were made in God's image and likeness,<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Genesis 1:26–27" /> so that dignity rests on a resemblance as well as a bestowed honour, and that this image is restored in Christ. That is a theological disagreement grounded in rival revelations, not an inconsistency in Islam.

A second issue is the condition of the nature. If the *fitra* is sound and only obscured, the Christian asks whether that explains how deeply and universally the heart turns away; that question is pursued in <QuestionLink question="great-and-terrible" worldview="islam">the flagship</QuestionLink>.

## Pressure questions

- If the heart is a subtle spiritual reality, in what sense is the resurrected body the same person's body?
- Does honour bestowed by God differ from bearing God's image, and what follows for how humans should treat each other?
- If every child is born on the *fitra*, what accounts for the near-universal drift away from it?

## Deep dive

#### Clay, spirit and the angels' question

The creation accounts are repeated across the Qur'an with different emphases. The angels object that a *khalifa* will cause damage and bloodshed, and God answers by showing Adam's knowledge of the names.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="2:30–33" /> Commentators differ on whether *khalifa* means God's deputy or one generation succeeding another; either way, the human is both the one who can corrupt the earth and the one who knows. The breath of God's spirit is not usually read as a share in the divine essence. It marks the life God gives and the special honour of this creature.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="15:28–29" />

#### Heart, soul and the nafs

Al-Ghazali's four terms, heart, spirit, soul and intellect, name one subtle reality in its different relations; each also has a lower, bodily sense.<Cite source="ghazali-marvels-heart" locator="pp. 6–8" /> The Qur'an speaks of the soul as inclined both ways and of a soul at peace that returns to its Lord well pleased and well pleasing.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="91:7–10; 89:27–30" /> The point for ontology is that the self is not static. It is a heart that can be purified or darkened.

#### Fitra as potency

Ibn Taymiyya developed the *fitra* most fully: it is present at birth as a potency that grows into knowledge of God as a person's knowing and willing mature, unless something impedes it.<Cite source="hoover-theodicy" locator="p. 41" /> On this view the orientation to God is natural to humans, while particular religions are learned. The same capacity for love can be directed to God or to something else, a theme taken up in <QuestionLink question="worship" worldview="islam">the worship question</QuestionLink>.

#### Body, death and resurrection

The Qur'an answers doubters who ask who could revive decayed bones: the one who created them the first time will give them life again.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="36:78–79" /> God can reshape even the fingertips.<Cite source="quran-haleem" locator="75:3–4" /> The person who is judged is therefore an embodied person, not a disembodied mind. What happens between death and resurrection is treated in <QuestionLink question="after-death" worldview="islam">the after-death question</QuestionLink>.

#### School differences

The theological schools place the human differently. Maturidi theology describes a person as a material nature joined to an intellect, and frames life's test as the use of that intellect.<Cite source="harvey-transcendent-god" locator="p. 27" /> Ibn Sina, a philosopher whose views the theologians often contested, treated the rational soul as an immaterial substance that survives the body.<Cite source="sep-ibn-sina" locator="§4" /> These differences affect how the soul's survival is argued; they do not change the shared Qur'anic picture of an honoured, accountable creature made from clay.
