## The view

Buddhism has a sophisticated tradition of logic and inference, developed over centuries of debate with Hindu schools. It is a mistake to read the Buddha's warnings about reasoning as a rejection of logic. When a discourse says that a teacher who "relies on logic and inquiry" may produce something "well reasoned, some poorly reasoned, some true, and some otherwise,"<Cite source="majjhima-nikaya-sujato" locator="MN 76:27.1–3" /> the target is reasoning used alone to build views about ultimate matters, not valid inference itself.

The epistemological school of Dignāga and Dharmakīrti made inference its central subject. A good reason, they held, must meet three conditions: it must belong to the subject under discussion; it must be present in similar cases; and it must be absent from dissimilar cases.<Cite source="sep-dharmakirti" locator="§3.1" /><Cite source="stcherbatsky-buddhist-logic-2" locator="Nyāyabindu II.5ff." />

Why does such a reason compel assent? Dharmakīrti's answer is that only two kinds of real relation support a necessary inference. One is identity of nature: this is a tree because it is an aśoka, a kind of tree. The other is causation: there is fire because there is smoke. "A fact which is not so dependent upon another one, cannot be invariably and necessarily concomitant with the latter."<Cite source="stcherbatsky-buddhist-logic-2" locator="Nyāyabindu II.19–24" /> Logic binds because it tracks how things actually depend on one another.

The Madhyamaka of Nāgārjuna uses logic differently. It draws out the absurd consequences of an opponent's thesis without, Nāgārjuna says, advancing any thesis of its own.<Cite source="nagarjuna-vigrahavyavartani-gretil" locator="v. 29" note="Sanskrit; our paraphrase" /> It also asks what grounds the means of knowledge at all (see <QuestionLink question="knowledge-possible" worldview="buddhism">why we can know anything</QuestionLink>), a critique of logic's foundations carried out with logic.

## What this explains well

The account explains why some inferences carry certainty and others do not, by tying necessity to real dependence. It avoids abstract objects and fits Buddhism's view of reality as dependent arising.

## Christian response

The Christian agrees that logic tracks real relations and is not a convention. The pressure is internal. For Dharmakīrti only momentary particulars are fully real and universals are constructed. If so, what makes a relation of identity or causation hold *necessarily* across every case, including cases no one has observed? Dharmakīrti sometimes speaks of natures "in" things that make fire produce smoke, which sits uneasily with strict nominalism.<Cite source="sep-dharmakirti" locator="§1.5" /> This is an internal tension, not a contradiction, and Buddhist scholars have offered answers. The Christian holds that necessity is better grounded in a God whose truthfulness gives the world stable natures.

## Pressure questions

- If only particulars are real, what makes "every aśoka is a tree" true of aśokas not yet seen?
- If Madhyamaka is right that the means of knowledge have no foundation, what gives its own reductio arguments their force?

## Deep dive

#### Good reasons, not just valid forms

SEP warns that calling this tradition "logic" can mislead readers into thinking good reasons are simply formally valid ones. Formal validity is "not itself explicitly discussed by Buddhist theoreticians." A good reason must also be sound, its three features must be ascertained by the knower, and the opponent must want to know something new.<Cite source="sep-dharmakirti" locator="§3.1" /> Logic here is part of a theory of how real people come to know.

#### Nominalism

Dharmakīrti was a nominalist. Only momentary particulars are fully real; general concepts such as "tree" or "fire" are constructions of the mind, though useful ones because they track real causal powers.<Cite source="sep-dharmakirti" locator="§§1.1, 2.1" /> This is the background to the pressure question above.

#### Three kinds of reason

Dharmakīrti recognizes three kinds of reason: identity, effect, and non-perception, by which we infer absence from failing to perceive what would be perceived if present. He insists that "the above mentioned three relations alone can prove something."<Cite source="stcherbatsky-buddhist-logic-2" locator="Nyāyabindu II.11, II.19–21" /> Necessity is not a free-floating abstract fact; it rests on dependence among real things.

#### Logic and the two truths

Later Indian and Tibetan Buddhists reconciled Nāgārjuna and Dharmakīrti by giving logic and the means of knowledge full validity at the conventional level while denying them an ultimate, self-standing foundation. Nāgārjuna himself held that the ultimate is not taught without relying on the conventional.<Cite source="nagarjuna-mmk" locator="24.8–10" /> On this view logic binds in the world we live in, without being part of an ultimate reality behind it.
