## The view

The Buddha's teaching starts from a practical question: what can be relied on to end suffering? Its answer is neither credulous nor merely rationalist: claims are to be tested. Villagers confused by rival teachers are told not to settle the matter by tradition, scripture, logic or a teacher's reputation alone, but to judge "when you know for yourselves" whether a way of acting is unskilful, "criticized by sensible people," and leads to harm.<Cite source="anguttara-nikaya-sujato" locator="AN 3.65:3.5–4.3" />

The discourses explain why testing matters. Faith, approval, tradition, reasoning and considered views can all "turn out in two different ways." Something held with full conviction may be "vacuous, hollow, and false," and something doubted may be true. So a careful person preserves truth by saying "Such is my faith," without concluding that this alone is true.<Cite source="majjhima-nikaya-sujato" locator="MN 95:14.3–15.15" /> Knowledge in the full sense comes at the end of a path. One examines a teacher, places faith, listens, considers the meaning and strives, and at last one "directly realize[s] the ultimate truth, and see[s] it with penetrating wisdom."<Cite source="majjhima-nikaya-sujato" locator="MN 95:20.2" />

Later Buddhist philosophers built a rigorous theory on this base. Dharmakīrti, in the seventh century, opens his short treatise on logic with the words "All successful human action is preceded by right knowledge."<Cite source="stcherbatsky-buddhist-logic-2" locator="Nyāyabindu I.1" /> There are two means of knowledge. Perception grasps unique, momentary particulars and involves "neither construction (judgment) nor illusion." Inference works with general concepts, which the mind constructs.<Cite source="stcherbatsky-buddhist-logic-2" locator="Nyāyabindu I.2, I.4, I.12, I.17" /> A cognition counts as knowledge when it is non-deceptive: it leads to the object having the effects we expected.<Cite source="dharmakirti-pv-gretil" locator="PV II.1" note="Sanskrit; our paraphrase" />

The knower in this picture is not an enduring self looking out at the world. In keeping with the teaching of no-self, cognition is a stream of momentary events, each arising from conditions (see <QuestionLink question="what-is-man" worldview="buddhism">what a human being is</QuestionLink>).

Not every Buddhist school accepts even this much. Nāgārjuna asks what establishes the means of knowledge. Other means would lead to a regress, the means cannot establish themselves, and their objects cannot establish them either.<Cite source="nagarjuna-vigrahavyavartani-gretil" locator="vv. 31–33, 40, 51" note="Sanskrit; our paraphrase" /> For his Madhyamaka school, knowledge is valid in practice but has no ultimate foundation.

## What this explains well

The account combines suspicion of mere authority with respect for testing and for trustworthy teachers. It treats error as normal and correctable, offers a detailed theory of perception and inference, and ties knowledge to action and to liberation rather than to speculation for its own sake.

## Christian response

The Christian agrees that claims should be tested<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Acts 17:11; 1 Thessalonians 5:21" /> and that knowledge is bound up with how one lives. The pressure falls elsewhere. Inference needs a knower who holds evidence and conclusion together in one act of judgment. If the knower is a stream of momentary events, what unifies that act? Buddhist epistemologists answer with the causal continuity of the mental stream. The Christian thinks continuity explains succession but not the unity of one mind grasping a connection. A second question concerns the final appeal to direct realization: what distinguishes a veridical insight from a deeply practised conviction? These are questions of explanatory adequacy, not charges of contradiction.

## Pressure questions

- If each moment of cognition is distinct, what holds evidence and conclusion together in a single inference?
- If the means of knowledge have no ultimate foundation, as Nāgārjuna argues, what makes the Buddha's teaching more than one convention among others?

## Deep dive

#### Tested faith in the discourses

The canon does not oppose faith to knowledge. The Buddha invites his followers to investigate even him. Faith that has been examined in this way is "grounded faith that's based on evidence."<Cite source="majjhima-nikaya-sujato" locator="MN 47:16.1" /> Disciples are asked whether they speak "out of esteem for our teacher," and they agree that they speak "only of what you have known and seen and realized for yourselves."<Cite source="majjhima-nikaya-sujato" locator="MN 38:24.4–15" /> The Buddha also says that he has directly known far more than he has taught, as the leaves of a forest outnumber the few in his hand. He taught only what leads to liberation.<Cite source="samyutta-nikaya-sujato" locator="SN 56.31" />

#### Dharmakīrti's two means of knowledge

Dharmakīrti's two means of knowledge match two kinds of object. Particulars are real and causally effective; universals are mental constructions that help us act on particulars. Scholars describe his view of truth as a weak form of correspondence, combined with a practical test of justification: we confirm a cognition when the object turns out to have the causal powers we expected.<Cite source="sep-dharmakirti" locator="§2.1" /> His commentator Dharmottara glosses right knowledge as "uncontradicted" knowledge that makes us reach the object. That gloss is the commentator's, not Dharmakīrti's own sentence.<Cite source="stcherbatsky-buddhist-logic-2" locator="Nyāyabindu I.1, with Dharmottara's commentary" />

#### The Madhyamaka critique

Nāgārjuna presses the regress against the epistemologists of his day. If the means of knowledge are proved by further means, there is no end. If they need no proof, the opponent has abandoned his own principle that things are established by means of knowledge, and owes a reason why these alone are exempt.<Cite source="nagarjuna-vigrahavyavartani-gretil" locator="vv. 31–33" note="Sanskrit; our paraphrase" /> He also says that he himself advances no thesis.<Cite source="nagarjuna-vigrahavyavartani-gretil" locator="v. 29" note="Sanskrit; our paraphrase" /> This does not abolish ordinary knowledge. Nāgārjuna holds that the ultimate truth cannot be taught without relying on conventional truth.<Cite source="nagarjuna-mmk" locator="24.8–10" /> Later Indian and Tibetan thinkers reconciled him with Dharmakīrti at the conventional level, but the two remain distinct Buddhist positions.

#### Yogācāra

The Yogācāra school goes further in another direction. Vasubandhu teaches that what is conceptually constructed does not exist, and that all this is cognition only.<Cite source="vasubandhu-trimsika" locator="v. 17" note="Sanskrit; our paraphrase" /> On this view the apparent division between a knowing mind and external objects is itself a construction.
