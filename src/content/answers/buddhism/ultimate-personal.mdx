## The view

Buddhism's answer is not a simple "no." There is no personal creator, the goal of the path is not a person, and yet persons, above all the Buddhas, matter enormously.

**There is no personal creator.** The discourses know of a great god, Brahmā, who believes himself "the Maker, the Creator, the First," and thinks, "These beings were created by me!" The Buddha explains this as a mistake. Brahmā is a powerful, long-lived being who happened to be reborn first in a new world-cycle, and others assumed he made them.<Cite source="digha-nikaya-sujato" locator="DN 1:2.5–2.6" /> The gods are real, but they are beings within the round of rebirth, not its source.

**The ultimate is not a person.** What the Buddhist path aims at is the unconditioned, nirvāṇa: "freedom from rebirth, freedom from what has been produced, made, and conditioned."<Cite source="udana-sujato" locator="Ud 8.3" /> It is not a mind that knows and wills, and it is not an impersonal force that produces the world either. It is release.

**Persons matter enormously.** The Buddha was a human being who awakened and taught out of compassion. Later traditions revere many Buddhas and bodhisattvas. In Mahāyāna, every being is said to have the potential for Buddhahood, its "Buddha-nature."<Cite source="ratnagotravibhaga-johnston" locator="I.28" /> In Pure Land Buddhism, devotees entrust themselves to the vow of Amida Buddha to bring all who call on him into his land.<Cite source="three-pure-land-sutras-inagaki" locator="Larger Sutra, vow 18, p. 16" /> This devotion can be deeply personal. But Amida is a Buddha who vowed to save beings, not a creator or the ground of reality.

## What this explains well

The view honours compassion and devotion without making the universe depend on anyone's will, and so avoids a creator responsible for suffering. It also lets the gods of popular religion be respected without being treated as ultimate.

## Christian response

The Christian agrees that a god inside the world, like Brahmā, would not be the true God, and that compassion is among the highest virtues. The disagreement is about where personhood stands. Buddhism treats "a person" as a conventional designation for a stream of mental and physical events.<Cite source="buddhaghosa-path-of-purification" locator="XVIII.25–28" /> Christianity holds that personhood is ultimate, because God himself is personal and has made persons in his image.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Genesis 1:27" /> The Christian asks a question of explanatory adequacy: if persons are ultimately conventional, what grounds the infinite worth Buddhists themselves give to compassion for persons? Buddhists answer that compassion arises precisely from seeing that no one is a fixed self. This is a disagreement about the nature of persons, not a contradiction.

## Pressure questions

- If persons are conventional designations, is compassion finally compassion for no one?
- In Pure Land devotion, what makes Amida's vow trustworthy if he is not the ground of reality?

## Deep dive

#### Brahmā's story

The *Brahmajāla Sutta* explains how belief in a creator arises. When a world-system re-forms, a being is reborn alone in an empty divine mansion. He longs for company; other beings are reborn there; and he concludes that he made them. They agree, because he was there first and seems eternal.<Cite source="digha-nikaya-sujato" locator="DN 1:2.2–2.6" /> The story does not deny Brahmā's existence. It denies that he is what he thinks he is.

#### Not a person, not a source

The Udāna's unconditioned is described only negatively: unborn, unmade, unconditioned.<Cite source="udana-sujato" locator="Ud 8.3" /> It is never said to know, will or create. Nirvāṇa is therefore personal neither in the theistic sense nor as a world-soul, and it is not an impersonal source of the world either. The translation question is discussed in <QuestionLink question="ultimate-reality" worldview="buddhism">what ultimately exists</QuestionLink>.

#### Buddha-nature and the Dharma body

Mahāyāna texts speak of the *dharmakāya*, the "Dharma body" of the Buddhas, and of a Buddha-nature present in all beings, obscured only by defilements that do not belong to it.<Cite source="ratnagotravibhaga-johnston" locator="I.28, I.63" /> Such language can sound like a universal mind. The tradition's own guardrail is that it describes the potential for awakening, not a creator or a self in the Hindu sense. Some Buddhists read it more substantively than others.

#### Shinran and Other Power

The Japanese teacher Shinran taught that the person who entrusts himself to Amida's vow is saved by "Other Power" rather than by his own effort.<Cite source="shinran-tannisho-cws" locator="chs. 1, 3" /> That makes Pure Land among the most personal forms of Buddhist devotion, as <QuestionLink question="worship" worldview="buddhism">worship</QuestionLink> explains. Amida's vow is the power of a Buddha's compassion, not the will of a world-maker.
