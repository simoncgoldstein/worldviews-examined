## The view

The early discourses distinguish unwholesome conduct, including killing, stealing and false speech, from its wholesome opposites, and identify greed, hatred and delusion as unwholesome roots.<Cite source="majjhima-nikaya-sujato" locator="MN 9.4–7" /> These roots generate harm to oneself and others. AN 3.69 explicitly connects their domination with abuse of power, not merely private dissatisfaction.<Cite source="anguttara-nikaya-sujato" locator="AN 3.69:2.3, 9.3" /> Evil here is a moral direction of intention and action, not a creator's transgression or an independent evil substance.

Craving is also implicated in dukkha, which extends beyond culpable wrongdoing to the broader instability and distress of conditioned existence.<Cite source="samyutta-nikaya-sujato" locator="SN 56.11" /> A victim's suffering is therefore distinct from the wrongdoer's unwholesome intention. Canonical emphasis on intention gives conduct moral significance.<Cite source="anguttara-nikaya-sujato" locator="AN 6.63" />

Buddhaghosa denies that ignorance is a causeless first principle, and describes latent tendencies through which defilements can arise repeatedly.<Cite source="buddhaghosa-path-of-purification" locator="XVII.36–37; XXII.60" /> His account does not require a permanent self behind conduct. Conventional agency and the causal relation between action and result remain meaningful.<Cite source="buddhaghosa-path-of-purification" locator="XIX.19–20" /> Buddhist explanation is thus neither a doctrine of inherited sin nor a claim that no one acts and nothing matters.

Dependent origination places ignorance, formations and the conditions ending in aging and death within a connected process.<Cite source="samyutta-nikaya-sujato" locator="SN 12.1" /> It is therefore not a story in which a perfectly good creator first makes evil, nor a theory that evil is an autonomous cosmic competitor. The origin question here concerns the conditions that sustain disorder and how they cease. Why a creator permits evil is not a Buddhist question, since there is no creator to permit it.

## What this explains well

This account connects destructive conduct with identifiable mental conditions while retaining a practical distinction between wholesome and unwholesome action. It explains why domination can be rooted in the same dispositions that generate private dissatisfaction. Because the conditions are not an immutable essence, change remains intelligible. Śāntideva's treatment of wrongdoers emphasizes the conditions driving them while still allowing restraint.<Cite source="shantideva-bodhicaryavatara" locator="VI.22–33, 41" /> Understanding the aggressor's conditions need not mean exposing victims to continued harm.

## Christian response

The Christian agrees that intention matters and that evil need not be an independent substance. Christianity nevertheless locates responsibility in persons created by God, and sin in failure to conform to his law.<Cite source="westminster-larger-catechism" locator="Q. 24" /> The Buddhist account of conventional agency without a permanent self is not nihilism: action and result remain linked in one causal continuity.<Cite source="buddhaghosa-path-of-purification" locator="XIX.19–20" /> On whether the one who reaps a deed's result is the one who did it, Buddhaghosa calls dependent origination a middle way that rejects both "he who acts is he who reaps" and "one acts while another reaps."<Cite source="buddhaghosa-path-of-purification" locator="XVII.24" /> The question the Christian presses is whether that middle way grounds full accountability, the kind in which the one judged for a deed is the one who did it. On the Christian view accountability is finally personal, owed by persons God created to the God who will judge them. That is a disagreement about the nature of the agent, not a charge that Buddhism ignores responsibility. A further contrast is specific to one school: Augustine's privation account differs from Tiantai's teaching that evil is inherently included in buddhahood.<Cite source="augustine-enchiridion" locator="11, 13" /><Cite source="sep-buddhism-tiantai" locator="§4.1" /> That is a Tiantai teaching, not Buddhism's generally.

## Pressure questions

- How does causal conditioning relate to responsibility without requiring an unconditioned personal self?
- How are teachings about emptiness or inherent evil related to the continuing prohibition of harmful conduct?

## Deep dive

#### Roots, intention and harm

The early account identifies greed, hatred and delusion as roots, not merely labels attached after an action has produced pain. Their wholesome opposites can support a different course of conduct.<Cite source="majjhima-nikaya-sujato" locator="MN 9.4–7" /> AN 3.69 then traces the roots into domination and injury. The account therefore concerns social wrongs as well as an individual's lack of peace.<Cite source="anguttara-nikaya-sujato" locator="AN 3.69:2.3, 9.3" /> <QuestionLink question="know-the-good" worldview="buddhism">Knowledge of the good</QuestionLink> examines moral discernment; <QuestionLink question="fail-the-good" worldview="buddhism">failure to do the good</QuestionLink> addresses why understanding may fail to uproot desire.

Craving and dukkha supply a wider diagnosis. They do not license saying that an injured person freely chose the pain inflicted by someone else. <QuestionLink question="suffering" worldview="buddhism">The suffering answer</QuestionLink> distinguishes bodily pain from the additional distress described through the two arrows. The moral evaluation of an aggressor's intention and the analysis of a victim's experience require related but separate attention.

#### Conditioned agency and change

Buddhaghosa's account of conventional agency connects action and result while denying a doer existing over and above the process.<Cite source="buddhaghosa-path-of-purification" locator="XIX.19–20" /> Consequences do not disappear when permanent selfhood is denied, and any challenge to this basis for responsibility has to meet the causal account itself. Ignorance and latent tendencies explain the continuation of disorder without making it a first, self-sufficient metaphysical principle.

Nāgārjuna argues that moral change becomes problematic if things are fixed by intrinsic nature. Emptiness instead permits the transformations needed for abandoning wrongdoing; the cessation of ignorance matters within the account.<Cite source="nagarjuna-mmk" locator="24.32–33; 26.11" /> Far from dissolving moral distinctions, emptiness is what makes moral change possible.

Śāntideva's discussion of anger further distinguishes understanding conditions from endorsing injury. His treatment of the wrongdoer's dependence on conditions qualifies hatred toward the person, while restraint remains appropriate.<Cite source="shantideva-bodhicaryavatara" locator="VI.22–33, 41" /> Compassion and prevention can therefore belong to the same response, though his text is a discipline of the mind, not a modern legal theory.

#### School differences remain material

Dōgen's treatment of refraining from evil denies that evils have a fixed nature.<Cite source="dogen-shobogenzo-ja" locator="Shoaku makusa" /> Tiantai's doctrine that evil is inherently included in buddhahood supplies a different specialist account from the idea that every defilement is merely an adventitious obstruction.<Cite source="sep-buddhism-tiantai" locator="§4.1" /> Inclusion in that sense is not approval of harmful acts, and neither position erases the canonical distinction between wholesome and unwholesome conduct. <QuestionLink question="self-deception" worldview="buddhism">Self-deception</QuestionLink> and <QuestionLink question="great-and-terrible" worldview="buddhism">the flagship</QuestionLink> take up further differences in the analysis of mind.

The word root names the canonical diagnosis of unwholesome conduct; it is not an empirical theory of every human injury. And because ignorance has no causeless first moment, the account places disorder in a conditioned sequence rather than in the history of a first fall. It can be assessed on those terms, without assuming that a guilty ancestral act must be the starting point.
