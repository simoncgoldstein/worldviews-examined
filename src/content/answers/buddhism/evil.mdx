## The view

The early discourses distinguish unwholesome conduct, including killing, stealing and false speech, from its wholesome opposites, and identify greed, hatred and delusion as unwholesome roots.<Cite source="majjhima-nikaya-sujato" locator="MN 9.4–7" /> These roots generate harm to oneself and others. AN 3.69 explicitly connects their domination with abuse of power, not merely private dissatisfaction.<Cite source="anguttara-nikaya-sujato" locator="AN 3.69:2.3, 9.3" /> Evil here is a moral direction of intention and action, not a creator's transgression or an independent evil substance.

Craving is also implicated in dukkha, which extends beyond culpable wrongdoing to the broader instability and distress of conditioned existence.<Cite source="samyutta-nikaya-sujato" locator="SN 56.11" /> The two questions must remain distinct: the suffering of a victim is not itself the same thing as the wrongdoer's unwholesome intention. Canonical emphasis on intention gives conduct moral significance.<Cite source="anguttara-nikaya-sujato" locator="AN 6.63" />

Buddhaghosa denies that ignorance is a causeless first principle, and describes latent tendencies through which defilements can arise repeatedly.<Cite source="buddhaghosa-path-of-purification" locator="XVII.36–37; XXII.60" /> His account does not require a permanent self behind conduct. Conventional agency and the causal relation between action and result remain meaningful.<Cite source="buddhaghosa-path-of-purification" locator="XIX.19–20" /> Buddhist explanations therefore should not be reduced either to inherited original sin or to the assertion that no one acts and nothing matters.

Dependent origination places ignorance, formations and the conditions ending in aging and death within a connected process.<Cite source="samyutta-nikaya-sujato" locator="SN 12.1" /> It is therefore not a story in which a perfectly good creator first makes evil, nor a theory that evil is an autonomous cosmic competitor. The appropriate origin question concerns the conditions sustaining disorder and how they cease. That difference changes the comparison: demanding an account of a creator's permission would assume a premise this tradition does not affirm.

## What this explains well

This account connects destructive conduct with identifiable mental conditions while retaining a practical distinction between wholesome and unwholesome action. It explains why domination can be rooted in the same dispositions that generate private dissatisfaction. Because the conditions are not an immutable essence, change remains intelligible. Śāntideva's treatment of wrongdoers emphasizes the conditions driving them while still allowing restraint.<Cite source="shantideva-bodhicaryavatara" locator="VI.22–33, 41" /> Understanding the aggressor's conditions need not mean exposing victims to continued harm.

## Christian response

The Christian agrees that intention matters and that evil need not be an independent substance. Christianity nevertheless locates responsibility in persons created by God, and sin in failure to conform to his law.<Cite source="westminster-larger-catechism" locator="Q. 24" /> The Buddhist account of conventional agency and no permanent self brings different premises. The serious comparison asks whether those premises sufficiently ground accountable action, rather than assuming that no-self simply means nihilism. Augustine's privation account also differs from Tiantai's teaching that evil is inherently included in buddhahood.<Cite source="augustine-enchiridion" locator="11, 13" /><Cite source="sep-buddhism-tiantai" locator="§4.1" /> That specialist contrast must not be assigned to all Buddhism.

## Pressure questions

- How does causal conditioning relate to responsibility without requiring an unconditioned personal self?
- How are teachings about emptiness or inherent evil related to the continuing prohibition of harmful conduct?

## Deep dive

#### Roots, intention and harm

The early account identifies greed, hatred and delusion as roots, not merely labels attached after an action has produced pain. Their wholesome opposites can support a different course of conduct.<Cite source="majjhima-nikaya-sujato" locator="MN 9.4–7" /> AN 3.69 then traces the roots into domination and injury. The account therefore concerns social wrongs as well as an individual's lack of peace.<Cite source="anguttara-nikaya-sujato" locator="AN 3.69:2.3, 9.3" /> <QuestionLink question="know-the-good" worldview="buddhism">Knowledge of the good</QuestionLink> examines moral discernment; <QuestionLink question="fail-the-good" worldview="buddhism">failure to do the good</QuestionLink> addresses why understanding may fail to uproot desire.

Craving and dukkha supply a wider diagnosis. They do not license saying that an injured person freely chose the pain inflicted by someone else. <QuestionLink question="suffering" worldview="buddhism">The suffering answer</QuestionLink> distinguishes bodily pain from the additional distress described through the two arrows. The moral evaluation of an aggressor's intention and the analysis of a victim's experience require related but separate attention.

#### Conditioned agency and change

Buddhaghosa's account of conventional agency connects action and result while denying a doer existing over and above the process.<Cite source="buddhaghosa-path-of-purification" locator="XIX.19–20" /> A critic may challenge that basis for responsibility, but should engage the actual causal account. The position is not that consequences disappear because permanent selfhood is denied. Ignorance and latent tendencies explain the continuation of disorder without making it a first, self-sufficient metaphysical principle.

Nāgārjuna argues that moral change becomes problematic if things are fixed by intrinsic nature. Emptiness instead permits the transformations needed for abandoning wrongdoing; the cessation of ignorance matters within the account.<Cite source="nagarjuna-mmk" locator="24.32–33; 26.11" /> This is not an argument that moral distinctions vanish. The denial of fixed intrinsic being and the practical significance of change have to be considered together.

Śāntideva's discussion of anger further distinguishes understanding conditions from endorsing injury. His treatment of the wrongdoer's dependence on conditions qualifies hatred toward the person, while restraint remains appropriate.<Cite source="shantideva-bodhicaryavatara" locator="VI.22–33, 41" /> Compassion and prevention can therefore belong to the same response. The claim should not be enlarged into a modern legal theory that his text does not supply.

#### School differences remain material

Dōgen's treatment of refraining from evil denies that evils have a fixed nature.<Cite source="dogen-shobogenzo-ja" locator="Shoaku makusa" /> Tiantai's doctrine that evil is inherently included in buddhahood supplies a different specialist account from the idea that every defilement is merely an adventitious obstruction.<Cite source="sep-buddhism-tiantai" locator="§4.1" /> The latter claim is about how evil is included within that school's account; it is not safely restated as approval of harmful acts. A fuller explanation would require more than the present narrow comparison. Neither position erases the canonical distinctions with which this answer begins. <QuestionLink question="self-deception" worldview="buddhism">Self-deception</QuestionLink> and <QuestionLink question="great-and-terrible" worldview="buddhism">the human-nature anchor</QuestionLink> preserve further differences in the analysis of mind.

The account also requires care with the word root. Greed, hatred and delusion are not a biological experiment's demonstrated explanation of every human injury. They are the canonical diagnosis of unwholesome conduct. Likewise, the absence of a causeless first ignorance does not supply a historical narrative of a first fall. It places disorder in a conditioned sequence. These claims can be assessed for their explanatory scope without being translated into the assumption that one guilty ancestral act must be the starting point.
