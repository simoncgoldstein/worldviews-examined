## The view

The Buddhist diagnosis begins with dukkha and its arising through craving, grasping and ignorance. The Four Noble Truths connect the problem with an origin, a cessation and a path; the aggregates that people grasp as self are impermanent and not an enduring self.<Cite source="samyutta-nikaya-sujato" locator="SN 56.11; 12.1; 22.59" /> The task is not to give the same grasping ego endless opportunities for gratification.

The remedy is the ending of those roots through the Noble Eightfold Path. Right view, intention, speech, action, livelihood, effort, mindfulness and concentration integrate wisdom, ethics and meditation.<Cite source="samyutta-nikaya-sujato" locator="SN 45.8" /> Information alone is insufficient: Mahānāma can understand that greed, hatred and delusion corrupt the mind while still being overcome by them.<Cite source="majjhima-nikaya-sujato" locator="MN 14" /> Cultivation must change the conditions under which craving holds the person.

Effort is real, but it is not the effort of an isolated individual inventing his own rescue. The teacher's instruction and spiritual companions have a place in recognizing harm and undertaking restraint. Rāhula is told to examine actions and disclose harmful bodily or verbal deeds.<Cite source="majjhima-nikaya-sujato" locator="MN 61" /> Moral responsibility remains conventional and consequential despite the rejection of a permanent self. Cultivation can also change the fruit of a trivial bad action, as the salt analogy explains.<Cite source="anguttara-nikaya-sujato" locator="AN 3.100" />

Mahāyāna extends the aspiration toward complete awakening for other beings, joining renunciation with compassion.<Cite source="tsongkhapa-three-principal-aspects" locator="verses 7–9" /> Pure Land presents a further material difference: the Larger Sutra's eighteenth vow promises birth to those who sincerely entrust themselves to Amida, desire birth in his land and think of him even ten times (traditionally read as calling his Name ten times), while Shinran's reported teaching stresses Other Power for people unable to escape through self-calculated practice.<Cite source="three-pure-land-sutras-inagaki" locator="Larger Sutra, vow 18, p. 16; note 6, p. 97" /><Cite source="shinran-tannisho-cws" locator="chs. 1, 3" /> Its assurance of settled birth is not equivalent to uninterrupted joy; continued blind passions are acknowledged.<Cite source="shinran-tannisho-cws" locator="ch. 9" />

The final state also resists a uniform formula. Nikāya nirvana is the ending of greed, hatred and delusion, with ordinary after-death predicates rejected for a liberated one. Pure Land birth serves Buddhahood and benefit to others.<Cite source="samyutta-nikaya-sujato" locator="SN 43.1" /><Cite source="majjhima-nikaya-sujato" locator="MN 72" /><Cite source="shinran-tannisho-cws" locator="chs. 4–5" /> Neither account requires an eternal soul migrating unchanged through its experiences.

## What this explains well

The path addresses the production of suffering rather than treating its removal as a matter of satisfying every desire. It also explains why knowing better may leave harmful tendencies intact. Ethics, meditation and wisdom work together in the remedy, and teacher and community mean that “self-salvation” misdescribes the tradition.<Cite source="majjhima-nikaya-sujato" locator="MN 14; 61" /> Pure Land, where reliance on compassionate aid changes the account, has its own answer.

## Christian response

The Christian argues that the human problem includes accountable rebellion against God and needs Christ's reconciliation, not only the ending of craving or self-grasping.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 3:23–26; Ephesians 2:1–10" /> The test he proposes is moral experience: every wrong, including a wrong against a neighbor, is also an offense against God, and that guilt remains owed to him whether or not craving ends (see <QuestionLink question="guilt" worldview="christianity">the Christian account of guilt</QuestionLink>). The Buddhist does not accept a diagnosis that turns on a personal God who has been wronged, and holds that clinging, not an unpaid debt, is what binds. With Pure Land the comparison changes. Shinran's reliance on Amida's vow is real dependence on aid from beyond oneself, so the question is the character and warrant of that aid. Amida is not a creator whose law was broken, and the vow answers bondage to karmic evil rather than satisfying justice; the difference from Christian atonement lies in what the aid answers and who gives it, not in whether either tradition relies on compassion beyond the self.

## Pressure questions

- What turns an understanding of the diagnosis into a transformation deep enough to end its causes?
- How does release answer past moral injury, and how does that answer differ between the path and Pure Land reliance?

## Deep dive

#### The path changes conditions

The Four Noble Truths distinguish suffering from its origin and the path from its goal.<Cite source="samyutta-nikaya-sujato" locator="SN 56.11" /> Their structure prevents two shortcuts: identifying liberation with ordinary pleasure, or assuming that agreement with a proposition about suffering already constitutes freedom. MN 14 makes the second limit explicit: understood teaching can coexist with continuing corruption of the mind.<Cite source="majjhima-nikaya-sujato" locator="MN 14" />

The Eightfold Path supplies a coordinated remedy. Ethical speech and action are not dispensable decorations around meditation, and wisdom is not simply more skill in satisfying desire.<Cite source="samyutta-nikaya-sujato" locator="SN 45.8" /> The prescribed effort belongs together with the instruction and companionship through which training occurs. No-self does not require abandoning the conventional practitioner who undertakes those tasks.

#### Accountability without a permanent bearer

The rejected self is an enduring substance identified with the aggregates, not ordinary responsibility in everyday life.<Cite source="samyutta-nikaya-sujato" locator="SN 22.59" /><Cite source="buddhaghosa-path-of-purification" locator="XIX.19–20" /> Recognition, disclosure, amends and restraint remain intelligible. Their task is to alter harmful conduct and its conditions, rather than persuade a creator to change a legal verdict.

The salt analogy qualifies a mechanical account of karmic repayment. It concerns a trivial bad act in differently developed persons; it does not state that cultivation makes any grave injury inconsequential.<Cite source="anguttara-nikaya-sujato" locator="AN 3.100" /> The karmic remedy does not claim that a past wrong simply dissolves when its author feels peaceful.

#### Other Power and settled trust

The Pure Land source hierarchy matters. The Larger Sutra's eighteenth vow includes both promise and a grave-offense exclusion, while the Contemplation Sutra's lowest-grade narrative offers help to grave offenders.<Cite source="three-pure-land-sutras-inagaki" locator="Larger Sutra, vow 18, p. 16; Contemplation Sutra, lowest grade, pp. 84–85" /> Their relation is an interpretive question within the Pure Land tradition.

The Tannishō reports Shinran's insistence that reliance on self-power misses the vow's purpose. It distinguishes settled entrusting from calculating how each recitation erases a quantity of karma, and rejects purposely doing evil as a means of attaining birth.<Cite source="shinran-tannisho-cws" locator="chs. 3, 13–14" /> This is substantial dependence on compassionate aid. It is not the Reformed satisfaction of divine justice under another name, and it does not represent every Buddhist path.

Shinran's reported assurance also distinguishes birth's settlement from emotional enthusiasm, admitting continued blind passions.<Cite source="shinran-tannisho-cws" locator="ch. 9" /> That makes the grace/effort comparison richer than an opposition between self-reliant Buddhists and dependent Christians. The traditions differ over what the problem ultimately is, what kind of aid answers it, and what the final liberated condition means.

The Nikāya and Pure Land accounts consequently need different descriptions of progress. A path that cultivates ethics, concentration and insight is not identical with Shinran's settled entrusting while passions remain.<Cite source="samyutta-nikaya-sujato" locator="SN 45.8" /><Cite source="shinran-tannisho-cws" locator="ch. 9" /> The assurance of one is not the assurance of the other, and their contrast is substantive, not two vocabularies for one self-improvement project.
