## The view

The Buddhist scriptures, early and Mahāyāna alike, say nothing about Jesus. What Buddhists have said about him belongs to the modern encounter between Buddhism and Christianity, and it varies from teacher to teacher.

Soyen Shaku, a Japanese Zen abbot who lectured in America, explained that Buddhism has no personal creator. Its equivalent of "God" is the Dharmakāya, the eternal moral law and body of truth, which "was incarnated not only in Gautama-Buddha, but also in all great men in a higher or lesser degree, foremost among them in Jesus Christ." He called Jesus "the greatest master and teacher that appeared in the West after Buddha." The miracles he set aside: "I do not deny the miracles nor do I believe them; I only claim that they are irrelevant."<Cite source="soyen-shaku-sermons" locator="Reply to a Christian Critic, pp. 123–125" />

The Dalai Lama, speaking to Christian meditators in 1994, called Jesus "a fully enlightened being or a bodhisattva." He also stated the limit: the Buddhist principle of interdependence makes it "almost impossible to have any room for an atemporal, eternal, absolute truth. Nor is it possible to accommodate the concept of a divine Creation."<Cite source="cobb-review-good-heart" locator="p. 580" note="the Dalai Lama, as quoted by Cobb" />

These views are not "the Buddhist view" of Jesus, but they share a pattern. Jesus is honoured for his compassion and teaching, and placed within a Buddhist frame. The Christian doctrines are declined for principled reasons. There is no creator (see <QuestionLink question="ultimate-personal" worldview="buddhism">whether ultimate reality is personal</QuestionLink>); there is no enduring self to be raised in a body; and liberation comes from insight and practice rather than from another's death on one's behalf.

## What this explains well

The receptions honour the moral and spiritual greatness of Jesus' teaching, and they are consistent with Buddhism's own account of reality and liberation.

## Christian response

These are modern interpretations, not historical evidence about the first century, and they generally do not dispute the history. He was crucified under Pontius Pilate, as Paul, the Gospels and the Roman historian Tacitus attest.<Cite source="tacitus-annals-church-brodribb" locator="15.44" /> The reviewer John Cobb observes that history matters far more to the Bible than to the Buddhist scriptures.<Cite source="cobb-review-good-heart" locator="p. 580" /> That is the crux. Within a few years of his death his followers were proclaiming that God had raised him, and within about two decades Jewish followers were praying to Jesus and calling him Lord alongside the one God.<Cite source="hurtado-worship-of-christ" locator="Early devotion to Christ" /> The earliest Gospel shows Jesus praying to God as "Abba, Father,"<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Mark 14:36" /> and his followers built everything on his resurrection.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="1 Corinthians 15:17" /> To treat him as an enlightened teacher or bodhisattva requires setting aside the very claims that the earliest sources put at the centre.

Pure Land Buddhism comes closest to the Christian pattern. Shinran taught reliance on Amida Buddha's vow rather than on one's own power.<Cite source="shinran-tannisho-cws" locator="chs. 1, 3" /> But Amida's vow belongs to a sacred narrative set in another world and age, not to datable history as the cross and resurrection do (see <QuestionLink question="self-salvation" worldview="buddhism">salvation</QuestionLink>). The disagreement is finally about which picture of reality is true.

## Pressure questions

- If Jesus and his first followers took for granted a personal Creator and a coming resurrection, can he be honoured as an enlightened teacher without revising what the earliest sources say he taught?
- If miracles are irrelevant, as Soyen Shaku says, is the resurrection irrelevant too, though the earliest Christians made everything depend on it?

## Deep dive

#### Two receptions in context

Soyen Shaku's addresses, published in 1906, were given in America, partly in reply to a Christian critic. His praise of Jesus came with criticism. He thought the Gospel picture was "marred" by miracles such as the great catch of fish, a "useless destruction of life," and that Jesus fell short of the Buddha's "calmness and dignity" when anger "overtook him in the temple." The Buddha, he said, would have converted the traders, "for kind words strike deeper than the whip."<Cite source="soyen-shaku-sermons" locator="Reply to a Christian Critic, pp. 124–125" /> He also placed Jesus beside other great men, adding Washington and Lincoln to his list of the Dharmakāya's manifestations.<Cite source="soyen-shaku-sermons" locator="Reply to a Christian Critic, pp. 123–124" />

The Dalai Lama's comments come from a seminar on Gospel passages with Catholic meditators. Cobb notes that the virtues taught are "quite similar," while the metaphysics differs deeply.<Cite source="cobb-review-good-heart" locator="p. 580" /> Other modern Buddhist teachers, such as Thich Nhat Hanh, have written on Jesus, but their works were not consulted for this page.

#### Why a creator is excluded

The early discourses tell of a god, Great Brahmā, who arises at the start of a world-cycle and comes to believe that he made the beings who appear after him. The Buddha treats this as a misunderstanding: Brahmā was himself reborn there when his previous life ran out, and the others simply arrived later.<Cite source="digha-nikaya-sujato" locator="DN 1:2.2–2.6" /> On that view, a figure like Jesus can be a great teacher or even a Buddha, but not the incarnation of a Creator, because there is no Creator to be incarnate.
