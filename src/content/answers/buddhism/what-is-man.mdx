## The view

Buddhist traditions analyse a person into five aggregates: bodily form, feeling, perception, volitional formations and consciousness. None of them is a self. Each is impermanent and cannot be controlled at will; so of each one should see, "This is not mine, I am not this, this is not my self."<Cite source="samyutta-nikaya-sujato" locator="SN 22.59" /> There is no further self behind the aggregates that owns them.

This is not the claim that persons do not exist. Buddhaghosa states the balance: there is "no doer over and above the doing," yet the wise speak of a doer as a matter of common usage.<Cite source="buddhaghosa-path-of-purification" locator="XIX.19–20" /> The person is a real, conventionally designated stream of events, held together morally by intention. The Buddha says that intention is action: having intended, one acts by body, speech and mind.<Cite source="anguttara-nikaya-sujato" locator="AN 6.63" />

Consciousness is part of the process, not its owner. When the monk Sāti claims that the same consciousness roams and transmigrates, the Buddha rebukes him: consciousness arises dependent on conditions, and without conditions it does not arise.<Cite source="majjhima-nikaya-sujato" locator="MN 38" /> Continuity across a life, and across lives, is therefore causal rather than substantial. Asked whether the one who acts is the one who experiences the result, the Buddha rejects both "the same" and "another," teaching by the middle way of dependent origination.<Cite source="samyutta-nikaya-sujato" locator="SN 12.17" />

Human beings share this condition with all sentient beings, who are reborn among many realms. What distinguishes the human birth is opportunity. It is as rare as a blind turtle surfacing through the hole of a yoke drifting on the ocean, and it is the birth in which the teaching can be heard and practised.<Cite source="samyutta-nikaya-sujato" locator="SN 56.47–48" /> The mind is also said to be luminous, its defilements only visitors, which is why the human condition can be purified.<Cite source="anguttara-nikaya-sujato" locator="AN 1.49–52" />

## What this explains well

The analysis fits a self that changes constantly, in which no unchanging core is ever observed, and explains why clinging to a fixed self produces anxiety and conflict. It treats persons as fully responsible without positing a soul, since the moral weight lies in intentions and their consequences. And it gives a unified account of kinship with animals, since all sentient beings belong to one cycle of conditioned existence, while still marking human life as precious.

## Christian response

The Christian agrees that the self is not self-made or self-sufficient, and that grasping at a self independent of everything else is a root of misery. Scripture calls the human creature dust, sustained moment by moment by God's breath.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Genesis 2:7" /> The difference is that the Christian locates the person's unity and persistence in God's creating and sustaining will, which makes each person a particular subject who is known, addressed and raised. The Buddhist locates it in causal continuity and convention.

The Christian pressure is a question of explanatory adequacy, not a charge of contradiction. If there is no subject over and above the stream, what makes one stream's awakening its own, and what grounds the particular worth of a person rather than the rarity of a birth? The schools have careful answers; the Christian holds that they explain continuity better than particular identity. The moral consequences of this anthropology are treated in <QuestionLink question="great-and-terrible" worldview="buddhism">the flagship</QuestionLink>.

## Pressure questions

- If the doer is only designated by convention, what makes it fitting to praise or blame this stream rather than another?
- Does the value of a human life lie in its opportunity for awakening, or in something about this particular person?
- How can a process with no owner be the one who understands that it has no owner?

## Deep dive

#### Aggregates and not-self

The not-self teaching is an argument from control. If bodily form were self, it would not lead to affliction, and one could have it as one wished; since none of the aggregates is like that, none is self.<Cite source="samyutta-nikaya-sujato" locator="SN 22.59" /> The teaching is practical as well as metaphysical. It removes the object of clinging, without denying that bodies, feelings and thoughts are really there. Buddhaghosa's commentary preserves the conventional person while denying a substantial agent: actions and their results occur, but no one is found apart from them.<Cite source="buddhaghosa-path-of-purification" locator="XIX.19–20" />

#### Consciousness, continuity and rebirth

The rebuke of Sāti rules out a migrating consciousness-substance.<Cite source="majjhima-nikaya-sujato" locator="MN 38" /> The middle way of SN 12.17 rules out both strict identity and complete difference between the one who acts and the one who later experiences.<Cite source="samyutta-nikaya-sujato" locator="SN 12.17" /> How that continuity works after death is the subject of <QuestionLink question="after-death" worldview="buddhism">the after-death question</QuestionLink>.

#### School differences

The schools refine the same analysis in different directions. Abhidharma breaks the aggregates into momentary elements (*dharmas*). Vasubandhu's Yogācāra adds a deeper layer: beneath ordinary awareness a subtle mentation constantly takes the mind-stream to be a self, accompanied by self-view, self-conceit and self-love.<Cite source="vasubandhu-trimsika" locator="6" /> It also explains continuity across lives through karmic impressions carried in that stream.<Cite source="vasubandhu-trimsika" locator="19" /> Madhyamaka, following Nāgārjuna, argues that nothing has intrinsic nature, and that this is what makes agency and change possible at all.<Cite source="nagarjuna-mmk" locator="24.32–33" /> Buddha-nature texts teach that the mind's nature is luminous and its defilements adventitious; how far that differs from a self is debated among the schools.<Cite source="ratnagotravibhaga-johnston" locator="I.63" />

#### Humans and other beings

Human beings are not a separate kind of creature. They are one of the rebirths open to beings who act, alongside animals, ghosts, gods and others. Their dignity therefore lies chiefly in what human birth makes possible: the turtle and the yoke stress how rare and precious the chance is, and how easily it is wasted.<Cite source="samyutta-nikaya-sujato" locator="SN 56.47–48" /> Sexual difference and family belong to the conventional life of a human birth, and the tradition gives that life an ethic without making it the goal; see <QuestionLink question="offspring-family" worldview="buddhism">the family question</QuestionLink>. Whether the purpose of a human life is awakening for oneself or for all beings is taken up in <QuestionLink question="why-alive" worldview="buddhism">the purpose question</QuestionLink>.
