## The view

Mahānāma tells the Buddha that he understands greed, hate, and delusion as corruptions, yet they still occupy his mind. The reply distinguishes intellectual recognition from abandoning sensual attraction: without experiencing a greater happiness apart from sensual pleasures, one can remain vulnerable to them.<Cite source="majjhima-nikaya-sujato" locator="MN 14.2–4" /> The Aṅguttara Nikāya describes people overcome by these unwholesome roots committing harmful acts.<Cite source="anguttara-nikaya-sujato" locator="AN 3.65:5.1–15.1; AN 3.69" /> Craving, aversion, and ignorance can dominate action even when the person judges them harmful.

## What this explains well

It explains why agreeing that an appetite is harmful need not remove its attraction. Practice must reach dispositions which outlast conscious judgment. Buddhaghosa describes latent tendencies as conditions for defilements to arise repeatedly.<Cite source="buddhaghosa-path-of-purification" locator="XXII.60" /> That gives persistent failure a practical target beyond merely repeating a rule.

## Christian response

The Christian agrees that recognition alone does not transform desire, and that harmful habits require change. Calvin likewise allows for knowingly choosing wrong.<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="II.2.23" /> The disagreement concerns what the wrongdoing involves beyond its harmful mental roots. Christianity holds that it is also an offence against God, requiring his forgiveness; Psalm 51 addresses God in confession.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Psalm 51:1–5" /> Buddhism need not deny responsibility or making amends: the early discourses commend recognizing a transgression and properly accepting another's confession.<Cite source="anguttara-nikaya-sujato" locator="AN 3.4" /> The comparative question concerns answerability to God, not whether Buddhists take wrongdoing seriously.

## Pressure questions

- Does removing a harmful disposition fully address the wrong already committed, or is a further kind of reconciliation needed?

## Deep dive

#### Recognition, disposition, and transformation

Mahānāma's question is valuable precisely because he already accepts the diagnosis. He does not need to be told for the first time that greed is harmful. His difficulty is the persistence of attraction despite that understanding. The Buddha's reply points to a change in the person's experience of happiness, rather than a bare inference which must automatically govern conduct.<Cite source="majjhima-nikaya-sujato" locator="MN 14.2–4" />

Latent tendencies help explain this gap. A tendency need not be an explicit proposition someone currently endorses; it can remain a condition for an unwholesome state to recur. On this account, keeping a defilement out of consciousness at a given moment need not show that its underlying tendency is gone.<Cite source="buddhaghosa-path-of-purification" locator="XXII.60" /> This does not make every failure inevitable or excuse its harm. The canonical framework still distinguishes harmful actions and their roots from their wholesome opposites.<Cite source="majjhima-nikaya-sujato" locator="MN 9.4–9.7" />

The Christian comparison should therefore engage an account of cultivated transformation, not caricature Buddhism as simply recommending more information. The broader differences over personhood and liberation are addressed in <QuestionLink question="great-and-terrible" worldview="buddhism">the flagship anthropology</QuestionLink>. Here the shared observation is narrower: knowing the good and having desires reliably ordered toward it are different achievements.
