## The view

The Buddhist goal is not merely a better rebirth. The Four Noble Truths connect dukkha's cessation with the ending of craving and the path that leads to it. A Nikāya text defines the unconditioned through the ending of greed, hatred and delusion.<Cite source="samyutta-nikaya-sujato" locator="SN 56.11; 43.1" /> Nirvana thus answers the roots of suffering rather than promising their endless gratification.

It also cannot responsibly be presented as an everlasting substantial self living elsewhere. The no-self teaching rejects identification of the changing aggregates with an enduring self; MN 72 resists the usual after-death predicates for a liberated one.<Cite source="samyutta-nikaya-sujato" locator="SN 22.59" /><Cite source="majjhima-nikaya-sujato" locator="MN 72" /> The alternative is not to equate liberation with simple annihilation, which another discourse rejects.<Cite source="samyutta-nikaya-sujato" locator="SN 22.85" />

Mahāyāna foregrounds awakening in relation to other beings. Tsongkhapa's account joins renunciation to the aspiration for complete awakening for their sake; compassion is not an optional distraction from private release.<Cite source="tsongkhapa-three-principal-aspects" locator="verses 7–9" /> That does not make every bodhisattva account a refusal ever to attain the goal.

Pure Land offers birth in Amitābha's land as a path to awakening. The sutras and Shinran's reported teaching frame the destination in relation to Buddhahood and the capacity to benefit beings, not merely an indefinitely pleasant residence.<Cite source="three-pure-land-sutras-inagaki" locator="Contemplation Sutra, lowest grade, pp. 84–85" /><Cite source="shinran-tannisho-cws" locator="chs. 4–5" /> These distinctions matter even where the traditions share the aim of ending bondage to suffering.

## What this explains well

The remedy addresses the generation of suffering at its roots. More opportunities to satisfy craving would not answer a diagnosis that makes craving part of the problem. The Mahāyāna aspiration also refuses to let personal tranquility exhaust the good while other beings suffer.<Cite source="tsongkhapa-three-principal-aspects" locator="verses 7–9" /> A final end must therefore be understood in terms of insight, freedom and compassion, not simply survival.

## Christian response

Christian final hope is holy communion of distinct creatures with God, including embodied renewal.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="1 John 3:1–3; Revelation 21:1–4" /> It differs from the Buddhist rejection of an ultimate enduring self. The Christian can ask whether liberation preserves what is fitting about personal relationship, but cannot fairly argue that Buddhism's only goal is self-extinction or that compassion has no place in its final good.

## Pressure questions

- What positive account of final freedom remains when ordinary self-based descriptions fail?
- How are liberation and the enduring task of benefiting other beings related?

## Deep dive

#### The cessation of roots

SN 43.1 identifies the unconditioned through the ending of greed, hatred and delusion, then gives a practice leading to it.<Cite source="samyutta-nikaya-sujato" locator="SN 43.1" /> The pairing matters: a description of the goal should not erase the cultivation through which it is approached. The Four Noble Truths similarly distinguish the problem, its origin, cessation and path.<Cite source="samyutta-nikaya-sujato" locator="SN 56.11" /> The answer is consequently neither a vague calm feeling nor an additional happy state maintained by craving.

The limit on after-death predicates should also stay intact. MN 72 challenges descriptions of a liberated one through the same categories that sustain grasping.<Cite source="majjhima-nikaya-sujato" locator="MN 72" /> This is a substantive restraint on what can be asserted, not an invitation to invent an unknowable eternal person beyond all criticism. SN 22.85 prevents the opposite distortion of simple annihilation.<Cite source="samyutta-nikaya-sujato" locator="SN 22.85" />

#### Awakening and others

Tsongkhapa's aspiration for awakening includes beings caught in suffering and the intention to seek their good.<Cite source="tsongkhapa-three-principal-aspects" locator="verses 7–9" /> That emphasis changes the scale of the goal without being adequately summarized as postponing enlightenment forever. The question is how wisdom and compassion together characterize awakening.

Pure Land likewise distinguishes birth and completed awakening. The Contemplation Sutra's lowest-grade narrative describes a process that culminates in instruction and an aspiration for enlightenment; Shinran's report speaks of attaining Buddhahood and benefiting others.<Cite source="three-pure-land-sutras-inagaki" locator="Contemplation Sutra, lowest grade, pp. 84–85" /><Cite source="shinran-tannisho-cws" locator="chs. 4–5" /> These need not be forced into one universal timetable. Their shared relevance here is that the Pure Land is not simply a permanent refuge for an unchanged craving ego.
