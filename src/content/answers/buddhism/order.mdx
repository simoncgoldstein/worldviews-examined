## The view

Buddhism explains the order of the world without a creator or lawgiver. Its central idea is dependent origination: when this exists, that comes to be; when this ceases, that ceases. The Buddha described this pattern as something that holds regardless of anyone's awareness of it: "Whether Realized Ones arise or not, this law of nature persists, this regularity of natural principles, this surety of natural principles, specific conditionality." A Buddha awakens to it and teaches it; he does not make it.<Cite source="samyutta-nikaya-sujato" locator="SN 12.20" />

The standard teaching traces how suffering arises through a chain of conditions: ignorance conditions volitional activity, which conditions consciousness, and so on to birth, old age and death. Undo the conditions and suffering ceases.<Cite source="samyutta-nikaya-sujato" locator="SN 12.1" /> The same conditionality governs the physical and the moral world. Karma, the principle that intentional actions bear fruit, is part of this order, not a decree.

The Abhidharma philosopher Vasubandhu draws the contrast with theism explicitly. The world has no single cause such as God; beings and their worlds are produced by their own actions, in a beginningless series (our paraphrase of the Sanskrit).<Cite source="vasubandhu-akbh-gretil" locator="II.64d, Pradhan pp. 101–102" />

## What this explains well

The view explains regularity without postulating an unseen ruler. It ties moral consequence into the same order as physical cause, so goodness and harm have real effects. And it treats the order as discovered rather than imposed, which fits how we actually come to know it.

## Christian response

The Christian agrees that the world's order is real, discovered rather than invented, and that actions have real consequences. The disagreement is over whether that order needs a ground. For Buddhism the constancy of conditionality, the fact that it "persists" whether Buddhas arise or not, is simply how things are. The Christian holds that such constancy is better explained by the faithfulness of a wise Creator who upholds all things.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Hebrews 1:3" /> This is a question of explanatory adequacy. It assumes that a constant order calls for explanation, which Buddhists do not grant; it is not a charge of contradiction.

## Pressure questions

- What explains the fact that conditionality is constant, rather than haphazard?
- If nothing has a fixed nature of its own, what keeps fire hot and seeds true to their kind?

## Deep dive

#### Not "laws of physics"

Sujato's "law of nature… regularity of natural principles… surety of natural principles" translates three Pāli expressions: *dhammaṭṭhitatā*, *dhammaniyāmatā* and *idappaccayatā*, the last meaning "specific conditionality."<Cite source="samyutta-nikaya-sujato" locator="SN 12.20" /> The text concerns the conditioned arising of experience and suffering, not mathematical laws of matter. The sutta adds that this conditionality is "real, not unreal, not otherwise." The pattern is reliable without being a substance or a ruler.

#### No orderer behind the order

Vasubandhu's argument against a creator also targets an orderer. A single eternal cause would produce everything at once. Successive events need successive causes, and so the order of the world is the order of many conditions, not the will of one being.<Cite source="vasubandhu-akbh-gretil" locator="II.64d, Pradhan pp. 101–102" /> The argument is set out more fully in <QuestionLink question="something-rather-than-nothing" worldview="buddhism">why there is anything at all</QuestionLink>.

#### Emptiness and order

Madhyamaka teaches that nothing has a fixed nature of its own. That might seem to threaten order, but Nāgārjuna argues the opposite. Dependent arising is precisely what is meant by emptiness, and nothing exists that is not dependently arisen.<Cite source="nagarjuna-mmk" locator="24.18–19" /> A fixed nature produced by conditions would be a contradiction, so whatever arises from conditions lacks one.<Cite source="nagarjuna-mmk" locator="15.1–2" /> Emptiness therefore does not undermine order; it is another name for the conditionality that order consists in (our paraphrase of the Sanskrit).
