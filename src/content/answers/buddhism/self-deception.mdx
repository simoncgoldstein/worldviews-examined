## The view

The Aṅguttara Nikāya describes distortions in perception, thought, and view: taking what is impermanent as permanent, suffering as happiness, and not-self as self.<Cite source="anguttara-nikaya-sujato" locator="AN 4.49" /> These are not merely missing facts. They concern the way experience is apprehended. Buddhaghosa describes ignorance as confusing and concealing, while latent tendencies remain conditions for harmful states to recur.<Cite source="buddhaghosa-path-of-purification" locator="XVII.51; XXII.60" /> Correctly stating a doctrine therefore need not show that the habits organizing perception have changed.

## What this explains well

This explains self-misrecognition without requiring a fully conscious plan to deceive oneself. Yogācāra develops a related account in which afflicted mentation appropriates a self beneath ordinary awareness.<Cite source="vasubandhu-trimsika" locator="6" /> It adds a school-specific model rather than replacing the earlier discourse's account of distortion.

## Christian response

The Christian agrees that ignorance can be dispositional and desire can affect what seems evident. Augustine likewise describes avoiding scrutiny of a condition he already knew.<Cite source="augustine-confessions" locator="VIII.7.16–18" /> The disagreement concerns the ultimate object of recognition. For Buddhism the deepest misperception is taking what is not-self as self; for Christianity it is suppressing knowledge of the Creator, by created persons whose selves are real but whose self-understanding is corrupted by sin. Both traditions explain distorted belief well; describing the mechanism does not decide which account of personhood is true.

## Deep dive

#### Information, perception, and latent tendencies

The canonical list includes perception and thought as well as explicit views. A person may verbally acknowledge impermanence while continuing to approach things as if their availability or identity were secure. The distinction supplies a way of understanding why a correct sentence need not represent a transformed orientation.<Cite source="anguttara-nikaya-sujato" locator="AN 4.49" />

Buddhaghosa's account of tendencies describes conditions for recurrence, not a permanent substance hidden behind experience.<Cite source="buddhaghosa-path-of-purification" locator="XXII.60" /> Yogācāra's afflicted mentation gives a more specialized account of self-appropriation, belonging to that school rather than to every Buddhist tradition.<Cite source="vasubandhu-trimsika" locator="6" /> Neither description proves that every error is morally blameworthy, nor that another person's private awareness can be read off from a general doctrine.
