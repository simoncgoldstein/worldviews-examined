## The view

The Buddha taught that conditioned things arise in a regular way, by "specific conditionality." That order is "this law of nature," and it persists "whether Realized Ones arise or not." A Buddha discovers it; he does not make it (see <QuestionLink question="order" worldview="buddhism">order</QuestionLink>).<Cite source="samyutta-nikaya-sujato" locator="SN 12.20" />

Dharmakīrti turned this into a theory of inference. We may infer from one fact to another only when the two are really connected, by identity of nature or by cause and effect; without such dependence there is no necessary concomitance (see <QuestionLink question="logic-binding" worldview="buddhism">logic</QuestionLink>).<Cite source="stcherbatsky-buddhist-logic-2" locator="Nyāyabindu II.19–21" /> Mere repetition is not enough.

How are such relations known? Through experience, by observing that the effect appears when the cause is present and does not appear when it is absent, refined in later writers into a "fivefold examination."<Cite source="sep-dharmakirti" locator="§1.5" /> Dharmakīrti also held that a thing's causal powers belong to what it is. In SEP's words, fire, "being what it is, must cause smoke under the right conditions."<Cite source="sep-dharmakirti" locator="§1.5" />

Does this escape the problem of induction? Scholars disagree. Brendan Gillon has argued that Dharmakīrti tried to make the method watertight and, as SEP summarizes him, "ran up against a problem of induction that wouldn't go away." Tom Tillemans reads him more charitably: how we *find out* that two things are causally related is fallible and a posteriori, but what causation *is*, a connection rooted in the natures of things, is more than contingent.<Cite source="sep-dharmakirti" locator="§1.5" />

So it would be wrong to say Buddhism solved induction, and equally wrong to say it has no account of it.

## What this explains well

The account explains why generalizations resting on real dependence are trustworthy while those resting on coincidence are not. It connects logic to conditioned arising and is candid that discovery is fallible.

## Christian response

The Christian agrees that causal order is real and discovered. The pressure is twofold. First, Dharmakīrti's own nominalism makes the "natures" in things hard to place, since only momentary particulars are fully real<Cite source="sep-dharmakirti" locator="§1.1" /> (see <QuestionLink question="logic-binding" worldview="buddhism">logic</QuestionLink>). Second, the canonical order holds "whether Realized Ones arise or not," with no lawgiver behind it.<Cite source="samyutta-nikaya-sujato" locator="SN 12.20" /> That stability is treated as basic. The Christian holds that it calls for an explanation, which a faithful Creator supplies (see <QuestionLink question="induction" worldview="christianity">the Christian answer</QuestionLink>). This is a disagreement about where explanation stops, and an internal tension within Dharmakīrti, not a contradiction.

## Pressure questions

- If causal relations are discovered fallibly, what justifies treating a discovered relation as necessary in unobserved cases?
- If the order of conditioned arising has no ground beyond itself, why expect it to continue?

## Deep dive

#### Two passages, one theory?

SEP notes two strands in Dharmakīrti. In some passages, causal connection is "something stronger" than regularity: there are features "in" the causal relata themselves. In others, he establishes causality by observing presence and absence, like Hume's regularities and like J. S. Mill's method of agreement and difference.<Cite source="sep-dharmakirti" locator="§1.5" /> Later commentators such as Dharmottara added provisos to rule out misleading circumstances. Whether the strands fit together is exactly the Gillon–Tillemans question.

#### The Buddhist reply to the materialists

A fourteenth-century Hindu survey of philosophies reports the Buddhist answer to the Cārvāka in Dharmakīrti's terms: universal connection comes from identity and causation, "not through the mere observation of the desired result in similar cases," and causation is fixed by a fivefold test of observing and not observing cause and effect.<Cite source="cowell-gough-sarvadarsanasamgraha" locator="ch. II (pp. 12–13)" /> SEP compares Tillemans' reading to the view that water must be H₂O although we discovered it by fallible experiment.<Cite source="sep-dharmakirti" locator="§1.5" />

#### Not Hume's question

Dharmakīrti asked why some inferences carry certainty, not Hume's question of how any inference from past to future can be justified. The comparison is illuminating but should not be pressed. Buddhist epistemologists, like their Hindu opponents, answered the materialist challenge with a method whose discoveries can be mistaken, not with a proof that the future must resemble the past (see <QuestionLink question="induction" worldview="hinduism">the Hindu answer</QuestionLink>).

#### Momentariness

Dharmakīrti held that real things last only a moment. What we take to be enduring objects are "series of qualitatively similar moments," and nothing "remains numerically the same for more than one instant."<Cite source="sep-dharmakirti" locator="§1.2" /> "Fire" and "smoke" are therefore series of momentary events, and causation is a relation between moments. Inference relies on these streams continuing in regular ways, which the teaching of conditioned arising affirms.
