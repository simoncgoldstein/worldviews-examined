## The view

Buddhist accounts begin with harmful intention and action rather than a creator's judicial verdict. Intention is karma; the roots of unwholesome action include greed, hatred and delusion.<Cite source="anguttara-nikaya-sujato" locator="AN 6.63; 3.69" /> Remorse becomes useful when it brings clear recognition and changed conduct, rather than endlessly renewing self-preoccupation.

In the advice to Rāhula, actions are examined before, during and after performance. Harmful bodily or verbal acts are to be disclosed to the teacher or a sensible spiritual companion, followed by restraint in future.<Cite source="majjhima-nikaya-sujato" locator="MN 61" /> Recognition and amends are treated as signs of growth, not an excuse to deny the wrong.<Cite source="anguttara-nikaya-sujato" locator="AN 3.4" /> A permanent self is not required for conventional responsibility.<Cite source="buddhaghosa-path-of-purification" locator="XIX.19–20" />

Karmic results are also affected by cultivation. The salt simile contrasts the effect of a trivial bad deed in a narrowly developed person with its effect in one developed in ethics, mind and wisdom.<Cite source="anguttara-nikaya-sujato" locator="AN 3.100" /> It does not promise automatic erasure of every serious injury. Pure Land texts offer another form of release: the Contemplation Sutra describes even grave offenders receiving help at death and birth in Amitābha's land.<Cite source="three-pure-land-sutras-inagaki" locator="Contemplation Sutra, lowest grade, pp. 84–85" /> This is a significant alternative within Buddhism, not the universal Buddhist account of forgiveness.

## What this explains well

The practices direct attention from a fixed identity as a bad person to the conditions that produce harmful conduct. Confession and future restraint make regret actionable; the salt simile leaves room for transformation without pretending every deed has an unalterable result independent of later development.<Cite source="majjhima-nikaya-sujato" locator="MN 61" /><Cite source="anguttara-nikaya-sujato" locator="AN 3.100" /> Responsibility remains meaningful even when the self is not an eternal substance.

## Christian response

The Christian grants the value of disclosure, restraint and changed habits. He nevertheless claims that guilt includes answerability to God, and that Christ's atonement supplies pardon: Christ bore the judgment due to sin, so God forgives without treating the wrong as nothing (see <QuestionLink question="guilt" worldview="christianity">the Christian account of guilt</QuestionLink>).<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 3:23–26" /> The Buddhist rejects that diagnosis rather than offering an incomplete version of it. The comparison therefore has two sides: whether karmic transformation adequately addresses past moral injury, and whether a divine judgment should be believed to exist at all. The Christian argues that a wrong leaves something owed to the one wronged, which changed future conduct does not pay; the Buddhist account answers past harm through disclosure, amends and the transformation of the conditions that produced it.

## Deep dive

#### Consequences and cultivation

The salt analogy concerns a trivial bad action. It is a warning against treating karmic fruition as a fixed one-to-one repayment that makes liberation impossible, but its scope is limited.<Cite source="anguttara-nikaya-sujato" locator="AN 3.100" /> It does not make any atrocity insignificant once its perpetrator acquires some meditation skill. Ethical development belongs to the example itself, rather than being an unrelated technique for avoiding accountability.

Rāhula's instruction likewise joins insight with practice. A harmful bodily or verbal action is disclosed; the practitioner undertakes future restraint. Harmful mental acts receive their own reflective response.<Cite source="majjhima-nikaya-sujato" locator="MN 61" /> The teacher and spiritual companions are part of the prescribed means, so this is not private self-salvation.

#### Pure Land and the limits of a slogan

The Larger Sutra's eighteenth vow includes an exclusion concerning grave offenses and abuse of the Dharma, while the Contemplation Sutra's lowest grade describes help for grave offenders.<Cite source="three-pure-land-sutras-inagaki" locator="Larger Sutra, vow 18, p. 16; Contemplation Sutra, lowest grade, pp. 84–85" /> The texts raise interpretive questions, and Pure Land teaching does not reduce to the flat claim that karmic evil can never obstruct birth.

In the Tannishō's report of Shinran, reliance on Other Power is distinguished from calculating how each recitation cancels a quantity of karmic evil. The same work rejects purposely committing evil as a means to attain birth.<Cite source="shinran-tannisho-cws" locator="chs. 13–14" /> The remedy is trust in the vow, not deliberate moral deterioration. That position materially changes comparisons over effort and aid, while remaining distinct from a creator's satisfaction of justice.

The wider account is in <QuestionLink question="self-salvation" worldview="buddhism">the liberation anchor</QuestionLink>. <QuestionLink question="after-death" worldview="buddhism">After death</QuestionLink> addresses conditioned continuity and Pure Land birth; <QuestionLink question="final-end" worldview="buddhism">final destiny</QuestionLink> distinguishes nirvana and Buddhahood from the perpetuation of a guilty or innocent eternal ego.
