## The view

Death is followed by further conditioned birth while the causes of saṃsāra remain. Dependent origination connects ignorance, craving and grasping with continued becoming, birth, aging and death.<Cite source="samyutta-nikaya-sujato" locator="SN 12.1" /> This is not an eternal soul exchanging bodies. The Buddha rejects the claim that the very same consciousness wanders through successive lives; consciousness arises dependent on conditions.<Cite source="majjhima-nikaya-sujato" locator="MN 38" />

The continuity also should not be described as a wholly unrelated being receiving another's consequences. SN 12.17 rejects the opposed claims that the agent and experiencer are simply the same eternal subject or entirely different, and instead gives a conditioned account.<Cite source="samyutta-nikaya-sujato" locator="SN 12.17" /> Conventional responsibility survives the rejection of a permanent self.

Final release changes the question. MN 72 refuses the four alternatives that a liberated one exists after death, does not exist, both or neither. Its extinguished-fire image exposes a failure of the proposed categories, rather than locating an immortal ego in an unseen place.<Cite source="majjhima-nikaya-sujato" locator="MN 72" /> SN 22.85 likewise rejects annihilation as a correct description.<Cite source="samyutta-nikaya-sujato" locator="SN 22.85" />

Pure Land adds a distinct destination for awakening. Its canonical texts promise birth in Amitābha's land; Shinran's reported teaching stresses trust in Other Power and eventual Buddhahood.<Cite source="three-pure-land-sutras-inagaki" locator="Larger Sutra, vow 18, p. 16" /><Cite source="shinran-tannisho-cws" locator="chs. 1, 4–5" /> This does not make an eternal migrating soul the shared Buddhist answer.

## What this explains well

The account makes continuity causal rather than requiring a changeless bearer hidden behind all change. It also prevents liberation from being equated with the infinite extension of present craving and identity. The distinction between ordinary rebirth and a liberated one's after-death description is crucial: rejecting annihilation does not force the alternative of an everlasting substantial person.<Cite source="majjhima-nikaya-sujato" locator="MN 72" /><Cite source="samyutta-nikaya-sujato" locator="SN 22.85" />

## Christian response

The Christian instead affirms that an enduring creature is raised and judged, with ultimate blessedness retaining personal distinction.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="1 Corinthians 15:35–49; 2 Corinthians 5:10" /> The Buddhist account should be asked how conditioned continuity grounds responsibility, rather than accused of transferring punishment to a stranger without explanation. The dispute is over personhood and final good, with both traditions bearing the burden of warrant for their postmortem claims.

## Pressure questions

- What makes conditioned continuity sufficient for responsibility without a permanent self?
- Which descriptions of release preserve its meaning without converting it into either an eternal ego or annihilation?

## Deep dive

#### Neither a traveling soul nor an unrelated successor

MN 38 addresses a specific misunderstanding: the same consciousness is not said to transmigrate independently of conditions.<Cite source="majjhima-nikaya-sujato" locator="MN 38" /> Calling rebirth a consciousness-stream must therefore not covertly make that stream a soul-substance carrying itself from body to body. The continuity is dependent, and Buddhism's conventional agent is not the eternal ātman of Vedānta.

Buddhaghosa's account distinguishes conventional designations from a permanent being, while preserving the conditioned succession of events.<Cite source="buddhaghosa-path-of-purification" locator="XIX.19–20" /> That distinction answers part of the responsibility objection. A Christian can still dispute whether it is sufficient, but should engage the proposed causal relation rather than assume that no-self means no consequences or no agent in ordinary speech.

#### The extinguished-fire comparison

MN 72 asks in which direction an extinguished fire has gone. The question fails because the fire depended on fuel that is now exhausted.<Cite source="majjhima-nikaya-sujato" locator="MN 72" /> The comparison is not permission to conclude that a liberated person is a surviving entity who traveled to a fifth, unnamed location. Nor does it authorize the assertion of annihilation that SN 22.85 rejects. Its restraint is part of the teaching's challenge to the initial categories.

Pure Land birth offers another set of descriptions. The Larger Sutra's vow joins entrusting and aspiration for birth, while its stated grave-offense exclusion and the Contemplation Sutra's rescue narrative create interpretive questions.<Cite source="three-pure-land-sutras-inagaki" locator="Larger Sutra, vow 18, p. 16; Contemplation Sutra, lowest grade, pp. 84–85" /> Shinran's report connects settled trust with birth and Buddhahood rather than a merely pleasurable eternal self.<Cite source="shinran-tannisho-cws" locator="chs. 4–5, 9" />
