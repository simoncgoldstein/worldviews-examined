## The view

The Buddha did not try to explain why there is something rather than nothing. He taught that the process of birth and death has no discoverable starting point: "this transmigration has no known beginning. No first point is found."<Cite source="samyutta-nikaya-sujato" locator="SN 15.1" /> Speculation about the world is one of four things he called unthinkable: they should not be thought about, and anyone who tries will go mad or get frustrated.<Cite source="anguttara-nikaya-sujato" locator="AN 4.77" /> When asked whether the cosmos is eternal or not, he left the question undeclared, because answering it would not help anyone toward freedom from suffering.<Cite source="majjhima-nikaya-sujato" locator="MN 63" />

That does not mean anything goes. Two answers are explicitly rejected. One is that a creator God made beings. The discourses tell of a powerful deity who wrongly concludes that "these beings were created by me."<Cite source="digha-nikaya-sujato" locator="DN 1:2.5" /> Another discourse rejects the view that everything a person experiences is due to "God Almighty's creation," because it removes all reason for moral effort.<Cite source="anguttara-nikaya-sujato" locator="AN 3.61" /> The other rejected answer is that things arise without any cause.<Cite source="digha-nikaya-sujato" locator="DN 1:2.30–2.33" />

What remains is conditionality. Things arise from conditions, and those conditions from earlier ones, without beginning. The great Abhidharma thinker Vasubandhu draws the conclusion: there is no single cause of the world; beings' own actions (*karma*) produce them in each birth (our translation of the Sanskrit).<Cite source="vasubandhu-akbh-gretil" locator="II.64d, Pradhan pp. 101–102" />

## What this explains well

The view avoids both a first cause that itself needs explaining and the idea that something came from nothing. It ties each being's existence to intelligible causes, especially moral action, and keeps attention on the urgent question: how suffering arises and ends.

## Christian response

The Christian agrees that nothing comes into being without a cause, and that moral action has real consequences. The disagreement is over whether a beginningless series of conditions is a sufficient answer. Each link is explained by an earlier one, but the existence of the series itself is not explained. The Christian holds that it needs a ground outside the series: a self-existent God who created the world with a beginning.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Hebrews 11:3" /> Buddhists will reply that this demand repeats the speculation the Buddha set aside. The disagreement is about whether the question is legitimate, and the Christian's answer depends on theistic premises. Vasubandhu's critique of a creator is answered in <QuestionLink question="something-rather-than-nothing" worldview="christianity">the Christian answer</QuestionLink>.

## Pressure questions

- If every state is explained by an earlier one, without beginning, what explains that there is any such series at all?
- If questions about the world's origin do not lead to liberation, does that show they have no answer, or only that the Buddha chose not to answer them?

## Deep dive

#### Vasubandhu's argument against a creator

In his *Commentary on the Treasury of Abhidharma*, Vasubandhu argues that the world does not come from God (Īśvara) or any other single cause. His argument runs roughly as follows (our paraphrase of the Sanskrit).<Cite source="vasubandhu-akbh-gretil" locator="II.64d, Pradhan pp. 101–102" />

1. If one cause produced everything, everything would arise at once. But things arise in succession.
2. If succession is due to God's successive wishes, those wishes need their own causes. Then God is not the only cause, and the beginningless chain of causes the Buddhist teaches has been conceded.
3. God would gain nothing by creating, unless it were delight; and a God who needs delight is not fully lord.
4. A God who makes beings suffer for his delight would hardly deserve homage.

The argument targets a creator who acts by changing wishes. Whether it applies to a God whose single eternal decree orders all succession is the question the Christian answer takes up.

#### Brahmā's mistake

The *Brahmajāla Sutta* explains how belief in a creator arises. After a world-cycle dissolves, a being is reborn alone in an empty divine realm. He longs for company, others are reborn there, and he concludes that he made them. They agree, because he was there first.<Cite source="digha-nikaya-sujato" locator="DN 1:2.2–2.6" /> The deity is real but long-lived and mistaken, a being within the round of rebirth rather than its source.

#### No first beginning of ignorance

Buddhaghosa, the great Theravāda commentator, says that ignorance, the first link in the chain of conditions, has no first beginning, and that the chain is not the work of a creator or overlord.<Cite source="buddhaghosa-path-of-purification" locator="XVII.36–37, 50" /> Nāgārjuna generalizes the point: nothing arises from itself, from another, from both, or without a cause.<Cite source="nagarjuna-mmk" locator="1.1" /> The Buddhist answer is neither creation nor chance but dependent origination, treated in <QuestionLink question="order" worldview="buddhism">why reality is orderly</QuestionLink>.
