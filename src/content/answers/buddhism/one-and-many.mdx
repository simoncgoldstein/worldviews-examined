## The view

Buddhism refuses to make either the one or the many the final truth. The Buddha taught a middle way between "All exists" and "All does not exist," describing instead how things arise and cease through conditions.<Cite source="samyutta-nikaya-sujato" locator="SN 12.15" />

The world is plural because things arise from many conditions. It is connected because every thing depends on others. Neither the unity nor the plurality is a fixed substance. A "person" or a "chariot" is one thing only as a conventional label for many parts and processes.<Cite source="buddhaghosa-path-of-purification" locator="XVIII.25–28" />

Madhyamaka presses this further. Nothing, whether one or many, has a fixed nature of its own; dependent arising is emptiness.<Cite source="nagarjuna-mmk" locator="24.18–19" /> Nāgārjuna even says there is not the slightest difference between the limit of saṃsāra and the limit of nirvāṇa (our translation of the Sanskrit).<Cite source="nagarjuna-mmk" locator="25.19–20" /> This does not mean everything is secretly one substance. It means both are empty in the same way.

The Chinese Huayan school, systematized by Fazang (643–712), holds that "one is all, and all is one." Fazang's illustration is a rafter: it is a rafter only by its role in the building, and the building is not that building without it.<Cite source="sep-buddhism-huayan" locator="§1.3" /> Huayan claims more than mutual dependence. Each thing, Fazang argues, is at once existing, and so numerically distinct, and empty, with no nature of its own to close it off from others. So each includes, and is included in, all others and the whole.<Cite source="sep-buddhism-huayan" locator="§4.3" /> In his Essay on the Golden Lion, every hair of the lion holds lions without end, like the jewels of Indra's net. Yet the myriad things intermingle without confusion, and each keeps its own position (our paraphrase of the Chinese).<Cite source="fazang-golden-lion-cbeta" locator="§§6–8, T45:669b–670b" />

## What this explains well

The view explains how the world can be deeply interconnected without collapsing into a single substance, and diverse without breaking into isolated atoms. Huayan in particular explains how a thing's identity can depend on its place in a whole without being swallowed by the whole.

## Christian response

The Christian agrees that no creature is self-sufficient, and can recognize in Huayan a serious account of a whole that does not erase its parts. The disagreement is over what that web of dependence rests on. Christianity holds that unity and plurality are both real: the many are created goods,<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Genesis 1:31" /> and their unity rests on one Creator, distinct from the world, who is himself one God in three persons. Madhyamaka treats unity and plurality as empty designations. Fazang traces the whole realm of dependent arising to One Mind, its one source, which is neither an individual consciousness nor something standing apart from that realm.<Cite source="sep-buddhism-huayan" locator="§4.2" /> The Christian asks whether a source that is not distinct from the world can explain why the world exists and has this pattern rather than another. The Buddhist does not accept that explanation must end in a Creator. That is a disagreement about where explanation rests, grounded in different premises about the ultimate, not a contradiction in the Buddhist account.

## Pressure questions

- If a person, like a chariot, is a conventional designation for parts arranged together, is the arrangement that makes them one person itself only conventional?
- If saṃsāra and nirvāṇa share the same limit, what is it that liberation changes?

## Deep dive

#### The chariot

Buddhaghosa's chariot is the classic Theravāda analysis. There is no chariot apart from its parts arranged together. "Chariot" is a term of convenience, and so is "a being." In the ultimate sense there are only the momentary mental and material events that make it up.<Cite source="buddhaghosa-path-of-purification" locator="XVIII.25–28" /> Unity belongs to convention, plurality to the analysed events.

#### Not a hidden One

Nāgārjuna warns that emptiness itself must not be turned into a view or a thing.<Cite source="nagarjuna-mmk" locator="13.8" /> His statement that saṃsāra and nirvāṇa share one limit is sometimes read as monism. In context it says that both lack own-being, not that both are modes of one substance.

#### Tiantai's three truths

The Chinese Tiantai school teaches three truths: emptiness, provisional existence and the middle that holds both. On this view every determinate thing is what it is only through its relation to everything else, and so is "neither the same nor different" from the whole.<Cite source="sep-buddhism-tiantai" locator="§2.1" />

#### Huayan among the schools

Huayan, like Tiantai, is one school's teaching, not a doctrine all Buddhists share, and its ranking of other schools is its own. Fazang ranked the teachings in five grades. Lowest, in his judgment, is Abhidharma analysis, because it does not see that its elements are empty. Madhyamaka and Yogācāra are an "elementary" Mahāyāna that teaches everything is empty. Above them come the teaching of the pure Buddha-nature, the wordless "sudden" teaching, and finally his own "round" teaching, in which one is all and all is one.<Cite source="fazang-golden-lion-cbeta" locator="§6, T45:669a–b" /><Cite source="sep-buddhism-huayan" locator="§4.4" /> Its difference from Madhyamaka is one of emphasis and claim. Madhyamaka identifies dependent arising with emptiness and calls it the middle way: things lack a nature of their own, yet are not nothing.<Cite source="nagarjuna-mmk" locator="24.18–19" /> Huayan goes further and describes that dependence as a positive order in which each phenomenon (*shi*) and the principle (*li*) that pervades them do not obstruct one another.<Cite source="sep-buddhism-huayan" locator="§§1.3, 2" /> It also parts from Yogācāra. Fazang held that Yogācāra mistakes the store-consciousness, which contains defilements, for ultimate reality, whereas for him ultimate reality is pure.<Cite source="sep-buddhism-huayan" locator="§4.4" />
