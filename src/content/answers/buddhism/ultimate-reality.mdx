## The view

Where other traditions name a first cause or a basic substance, the Buddha points to a pattern: things arise in dependence on conditions and cease when their conditions cease. He calls this pattern a "law of nature" that persists "whether Realized Ones arise or not." A Buddha does not create it. He awakens to it and teaches it.<Cite source="samyutta-nikaya-sujato" locator="SN 12.20" /> Everything that arises this way is impermanent, conditioned and liable to end.

This middle way avoids two extremes. "'All exists': this is one extreme. 'All does not exist': this is the second extreme." The Buddha teaches between them by describing how things arise and cease.<Cite source="samyutta-nikaya-sujato" locator="SN 12.15" /> Things are not permanent substances, and they are not nothing either. They are dependent processes.

There is also the unconditioned, *nibbāna* (*nirvāṇa*). The Udāna says there is something unborn, unmade and unconditioned, and that without it there would be no escape from what is born, made and conditioned.<Cite source="udana-sujato" locator="Ud 8.3" /> This is not a creator or a ground from which the world comes. It is the cessation of, and release from, the conditioned process.

Later schools analyse this differently. Theravāda thought, as systematized by Buddhaghosa, holds that persons and chariots are conventional labels. What actually occurs is a flow of momentary mental and material events (*dhammas*).<Cite source="buddhaghosa-path-of-purification" locator="XVIII.25–28" /> The Madhyamaka of Nāgārjuna goes further: even those events lack any fixed nature of their own. Dependent arising simply *is* emptiness, and nothing exists that is not empty.<Cite source="nagarjuna-mmk" locator="24.18–19" />

## What this explains well

The view takes impermanence and interdependence with full seriousness, and these are pervasive features of experience. It explains why clinging to things as permanent leads to suffering. It avoids positing a hidden substance that no one observes. And it treats regularity in the world as real without needing a lawgiver.

## Christian response

The Christian agrees that everything in the world is changing, dependent and unable to account for itself. The disagreement is over what that dependence finally rests on. Buddhism treats conditionality as basic and needs no ground beneath it. Reformed Christianity holds that dependent things depend ultimately on a God who is not himself dependent.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Acts 17:24–25" />

The Christian's question is one of explanatory adequacy. The constancy of conditionality, the fact that it holds "whether Realized Ones arise or not," is itself something the Buddhist account takes as given. The Christian thinks it calls for explanation. This presupposes that order requires grounding, which Buddhists do not grant; it is a disagreement about where explanation may stop, not a contradiction.

## Pressure questions

- If conditionality holds whether Buddhas arise or not, what accounts for its constancy?
- If nirvāṇa is unconditioned but not a source, how is it related to the conditioned world it is freedom from?

## Deep dive

#### The analysis of "a being"

Buddhaghosa uses the old image of the chariot. Just as "chariot" is a name for parts arranged together, with no chariot over and above them, so "a being" is a term of common usage for mentality and materiality. In the ultimate sense there is no being to be found.<Cite source="buddhaghosa-path-of-purification" locator="XVIII.25–28" /> This is the Theravāda distinction between conventional truth (persons, things) and ultimate truth (the momentary *dhammas* that compose them). Buddhaghosa treats those *dhammas*, and the unconditioned *nibbāna*, as real in the ultimate sense.

#### Emptiness is not a substance

Nāgārjuna's *Fundamental Verses on the Middle Way* opens by denying that anything arises from itself, from something else, from both, or without a cause.<Cite source="nagarjuna-mmk" locator="1.1" /> Things cannot have a fixed nature of their own (*svabhāva*), because whatever arises from conditions is not self-standing.<Cite source="nagarjuna-mmk" locator="15.1–2" /> The Buddhas teach by two truths, conventional and ultimate, and the ultimate cannot be taught without relying on the conventional.<Cite source="nagarjuna-mmk" locator="24.8–10" /> "Ultimate truth" here is the emptiness of things, not another, deeper thing. Nāgārjuna warns that emptiness is the relinquishing of all views, and that those who make emptiness itself into a view are incurable (our paraphrase of the Sanskrit).<Cite source="nagarjuna-mmk" locator="13.8" />

#### Translating the unconditioned

The Pāli of Udāna 8.3 literally says "there is the unborn, unbecome, unmade, unconditioned." Bhikkhu Sujato deliberately renders it "freedom from rebirth, freedom from what has been produced, made, and conditioned," to avoid suggesting a hidden entity.<Cite source="udana-sujato" locator="Ud 8.3" /> Both renderings have defenders. Either way, the passage affirms that release is real without making nirvāṇa the source of the world.

#### Appearance-only

The Yogācāra school, systematized by Vasubandhu, holds that what we take to be external objects are appearances in consciousness.<Cite source="vasubandhu-trimsika" locator="17" /> It is a third major analysis, and like the others it denies any permanent self or creator.
