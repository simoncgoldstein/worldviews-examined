## The view

Buddhism sets history inside a process with no starting point: "No first point is found of sentient beings roaming and transmigrating, shrouded by ignorance and fettered by craving."<Cite source="samyutta-nikaya-sujato" locator="SN 15.1" /> The ages are almost unimaginable: a solid mountain stroked once a century with a fine cloth would be worn away before a single eon ended.<Cite source="samyutta-nikaya-sujato" locator="SN 15.5" />

World-systems themselves come and go: "this cosmos contracts," and later "this cosmos expands."<Cite source="digha-nikaya-sujato" locator="DN 27:10" /> One discourse pictures a world ending in fire as seven suns appear in turn, with the refrain: "So impermanent are conditions."<Cite source="anguttara-nikaya-sujato" locator="AN 7.66" /> The Theravāda commentator Buddhaghosa adds that these catastrophes have moral roots: "When greed is more conspicuous, it is destroyed by fire," and hate and delusion bring destruction by water and wind.<Cite source="buddhaghosa-path-of-purification" locator="XIII.64" />

Within the ages, human history rises and falls by moral cause. Neglect of the poor breeds theft and violence until people live ten years and do not even have "the word 'skillful'." After an "interregnum of swords" the survivors resolve, "We'd better do what's skillful," and life lengthens again. In that restored age "the Blessed One named Metteyya will arise in the world."<Cite source="digha-nikaya-sujato" locator="DN 26:14–25" /> Buddhism therefore has real hopes about the future.

The teaching itself declines too, not "like a ship that sinks all at once" but as counterfeits replace it.<Cite source="samyutta-nikaya-sujato" locator="SN 16.13" />

What Buddhism lacks is a final consummation of history. Buddhas come and go; worlds form and burn. The goal is to leave the round, as the discourse on the seven suns ends: craving for continued existence is cut off, and there will be no more future lives.<Cite source="anguttara-nikaya-sujato" locator="AN 7.66:14.5" /> On the personal goal, see <QuestionLink question="final-end" worldview="buddhism">final end</QuestionLink>.

## What this explains well

The view applies impermanence consistently, even to worlds and religions, and it explains social decline by moral causes rather than by fate.

## Christian response

The Christian agrees that history is morally serious and that no earthly order lasts, but asks two questions. First, "no first point is found" can mean that a beginning cannot be discovered or that there was none; the Christian claims that the world had a beginning in God's free act (see <QuestionLink question="something-rather-than-nothing" worldview="buddhism">why there is something</QuestionLink>). Second, Augustine argued against the ancient cycle theories that if a liberated being escapes misery forever, then something new and final happens in time, and he asked why the same should not be possible for the world.<Cite source="augustine-city-of-god" locator="XII.13" /> Buddhism affirms a final release for persons but not for the cosmos; the Christian holds that both will be renewed (see <QuestionLink question="history" worldview="christianity">the Christian answer</QuestionLink>).

## Pressure questions

- If every recovery leads to another decline, is anything finally gained for the world itself, or only for those who leave it?
- Does "no first point is found" claim only that a beginning cannot be known, or that there was none?

## Deep dive

#### The seven suns

Buddhaghosa divides each great eon into four "incalculables": the contraction, the period that follows it, the expansion and the period that follows that.<Cite source="buddhaghosa-path-of-purification" locator="XIII.29" /> The account in AN 7.66 is gradual. With a second sun the streams and pools dry up; with later suns the great rivers and then the ocean fail, until "there's not even enough water left in the great ocean for the tip of the toe." With the seventh, the earth and Mount Sineru blaze like ghee, leaving nothing behind.<Cite source="anguttara-nikaya-sujato" locator="AN 7.66" /> The point is not prediction but disenchantment: even the world is unworthy of craving.

#### Decline and recovery

DN 26 traces the decline from a single failure of a king to give to the needy, through poverty, theft and the making of swords, to killing, lying and the collapse of family respect.<Cite source="digha-nikaya-sujato" locator="DN 26:13–19" /> Recovery begins when the survivors of the age of swords choose to stop killing, and grows as they take up one good practice after another.<Cite source="digha-nikaya-sujato" locator="DN 26:21–22" /> The coming Buddha Metteyya is the next teacher in the cycle, not a final redeemer of the world.

#### How long the teaching lasts

One discourse gives the true teaching a span of only five hundred years, a figure it ties to the founding of the order of nuns.<Cite source="anguttara-nikaya-sujato" locator="AN 8.51:26.1–26.3" /> Later East Asian schools developed the idea of a degenerate "latter age" of the teaching, a development not treated here. Mahāyāna sūtras such as the Lotus add that the Buddha is never truly absent (see <QuestionLink question="revelation" worldview="buddhism">revelation</QuestionLink>). None of these traditions describes a last age after which history is fulfilled.
