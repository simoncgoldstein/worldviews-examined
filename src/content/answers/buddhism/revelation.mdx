## The view

Buddhism has no creator to reveal himself, so the Buddha is presented as a discoverer, not a messenger. At his awakening, he says, insight arose "regarding teachings not learned before from another." He compares himself to someone walking in a forest who finds "an ancient path, an ancient route," follows it to a ruined city and has the city rebuilt. "In the same way, I saw an ancient path, an ancient route traveled by fully awakened Buddhas in the past."<Cite source="samyutta-nikaya-sujato" locator="SN 12.65:3.8; 7.1–7.10" /> What he found was not invented: the pattern of dependent arising holds "whether Realized Ones arise or not."<Cite source="samyutta-nikaya-sujato" locator="SN 12.20:2.3" /> Buddhist "revelation" is disclosure of what already is.

How is genuine teaching recognized? Not by a sign from heaven. A claim to be the Buddha's word is compared with the discourses and the monastic training, and rejected if it does not fit (see <QuestionLink question="ultimate-authority" worldview="buddhism">ultimate authority</QuestionLink>). Above all, the teaching is tested in practice, to be seen and known for oneself.

A further question divides the traditions. The Mahāyāna sūtras appear in the record later than the early discourses: by traditional accounts, Mahāyāna doctrines arose "roughly in the first century BCE."<Cite source="sep-abhidharma" locator="§1" /> On what basis are later-appearing texts accepted as the Buddha's word? Mahāyāna gives several answers.

1. **Skilful means.** In the Lotus Sūtra all Buddhas teach "by means of only one vehicle, the Buddha-vehicle"; earlier talk of separate paths was adapted to the dispositions of hearers.<Cite source="kern-lotus-sutra" locator="II (pp. 40–41)" />
2. **The eternal Buddha.** The same sūtra has the Buddha declare that his awakening at Gayā was a show: "many hundred thousand myriads of koṭis of Æons ago I have arrived at supreme, perfect enlightenment."<Cite source="kern-lotus-sutra" locator="XV (p. 299)" /> The teachings he gives in every age "are true."<Cite source="kern-lotus-sutra" locator="XV (pp. 301–302)" />
3. **Parity.** Śāntideva replies to a critic that the critic's own scripture was not established for him at the start either; whatever grounds his trust in it should apply to the Mahāyāna too.<Cite source="shantideva-bodhicaryavatara" locator="9.42–43" note="Sanskrit; our paraphrase" />

No single theory covers every Mahāyāna canon, and Theravāda does not accept these sūtras as the Buddha's word.

## What this explains well

The account gives authority without appeal to power, ties the teaching to results a practitioner can test, and, through skilful means, explains how one tradition can hold differing teachings together.

## Christian response

The Christian agrees that claims should be tested, but presses two points. First, verification in practice is inward and varied, and conflicting experiences weaken one another as evidence.<Cite source="sep-religious-experience" locator="§3" /> What distinguishes insight into the truth from the conviction a long discipline produces? The Christian appeal to the Spirit's inner witness must meet the same test, and its reply is that the Spirit persuades of a text open to public examination.

Second, chronology is relevant to the Mahāyāna claim but not decisive. The sūtras are usually framed as the Buddha's own discourses, and as historical memory their late appearance counts against them. But their defenders' justifications do not rest on memory. They appeal to a Buddha whose teaching is not confined to one lifetime. Dating cannot refute that, but it raises the question every lane faces: what makes a self-authenticating source credible? Śāntideva's parity argument has the same shape as the Reformed claim that every final authority attests itself; the difference the Christian presses is the kind of evidence offered, a public event in history rather than a teaching confirmed in practice.

## Pressure questions

- If practice confirms the teaching, what distinguishes realization of truth from the conviction that disciplined practice produces?
- If later sūtras are accepted because the Buddha still teaches, what would keep any later text from claiming the same authority?

## Deep dive

#### Derived from the Buddha, or identical with his word?

In one discourse a monk says that "whatever is well spoken is spoken by the Blessed One," and explains with a simile: grain carried away by many people all comes from one great heap. In context this means that good teaching is drawn from the Buddha's store.<Cite source="anguttara-nikaya-sujato" locator="AN 8.8:6.1–6.8" /> Later Mahāyāna writers are reported to have used the formula as a test, so that whatever is well spoken counts as the Buddha's word. That later use was not examined for this page, and the difference between derivation and identity should be kept in view. Interpretation matters as well: one who explains "a discourse in need of interpretation as a discourse whose meaning is explicit," or the reverse, misrepresents the Buddha.<Cite source="anguttara-nikaya-sujato" locator="AN 2.24–25" />

#### How the early teaching was kept

The early discourses themselves were transmitted orally. According to SEP's account, the Buddha's discourses "were probably rehearsed shortly after his death," but the texts "were not written down until several centuries after his death," in languages other than his own, and they disagree in places, partly because of insertion and deletion in sectarian disputes.<Cite source="sep-buddha" locator="§1" /> The Sri Lankan chronicle reports that monks who had handed down the canon orally committed it to writing when they saw the teaching in decline.<Cite source="geiger-mahavamsa" locator="XXXIII.100–101 (p. 237)" /> The early canon, too, was handed down for centuries before it was written, so the difference from later sūtras is partly one of degree.

#### Śāntideva's parity argument

The argument runs in three steps. The critic's scripture is established, he says, because both sides accept it; but it was not established for him at the outset. Whatever reason produced his trust there should produce trust in the Mahāyāna as well, since agreement between parties cannot by itself make a text true, or the Vedas would be true too. And if the Mahāyāna is to be rejected because it is disputed, the critic must abandon his own scripture, which outsiders also dispute.<Cite source="shantideva-bodhicaryavatara" locator="9.42–44" note="Sanskrit; our paraphrase" /> Other classical defences of the Mahāyāna sūtras, such as those of the Yogācāra school, are not treated here.
