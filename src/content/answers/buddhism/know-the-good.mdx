## The view

The early discourses distinguish harmful actions and their unwholesome roots from wholesome alternatives. Greed, hatred, and delusion lead to injury; their absence supports different conduct.<Cite source="majjhima-nikaya-sujato" locator="MN 9.4–9.7" /><Cite source="anguttara-nikaya-sujato" locator="AN 3.65:5.1–15.1" /> Moral knowledge is practical discernment of these patterns, not simply possession of a command which automatically governs the mind.

The Kālāma discourse warns against relying on tradition, lineage, scripture, or reasoning alone. It joins personal recognition to the judgment of sensible people and to the harms which follow from undertaking a practice.<Cite source="anguttara-nikaya-sujato" locator="AN 3.65:4.1–4.3" /> It is not an endorsement of whatever seems right privately: the discourse goes on to the Buddha's teaching about the unwholesome roots, and its advice belongs within that practical framework.

## What this explains well

The account makes correction of judgment concrete. Reflection on whether an action harms oneself or others is applied before, during, and after acting.<Cite source="majjhima-nikaya-sujato" locator="MN 61" /> Shame and moral dread are honored as the guardians of the world.<Cite source="anguttara-nikaya-sujato" locator="AN 2.9" /> Knowing or seeking good involves training attention, examining consequences, and accepting correction. It need not wait for a full metaphysical argument before identifying killing, deception, or other harmful conduct.

## Christian response

The Christian agrees that harm, motive, and the reliability of judgment matter. His account likewise treats conscience as accusing and excusing rather than infallibly approving.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 2:14–15" /> The deeper disagreement concerns what a moral wrong finally is. On the Buddhist account it is action rooted in greed, hatred, and delusion, known by the suffering it produces. On the Christian account it is also an offense against the personal God whose law conscience registers, which is why wrongdoing leaves guilt and not only painful consequences (see <QuestionLink question="guilt" worldview="christianity">the Christian account of guilt</QuestionLink>). Buddhist discernment recognizes real moral distinctions and condemns injury without that premise. The disagreement is theological: whether obligation finally answers to a person or to the conditions of suffering.

## Pressure questions

- How are competing judgments about long-term harm adjudicated when sincere observers disagree?
- What makes an apparently beneficial practice wholesome when its motive is self-serving?

## Deep dive

#### Personal verification is not mere preference

The Kālāma discourse combines several tests. A practice may be recognized as harmful, blameworthy, and criticized by sensible people; the proposed recognition is neither bare deference nor unchecked private intuition. Removing the advice from its account of greed, hatred, and delusion would change the argument's character.<Cite source="anguttara-nikaya-sujato" locator="AN 3.65:4.1–15.1" />

Buddhaghosa further distinguishes conscience grounded in self-respect from moral dread grounded in regard for others.<Cite source="buddhaghosa-path-of-purification" locator="XIV.142" /> These contribute to restraint, but their existence does not mean that each particular judgment is reliable. The canonical framework requires reflection on action and harm, while other discourses identify distortions in perception and view.<Cite source="majjhima-nikaya-sujato" locator="MN 61" /><Cite source="anguttara-nikaya-sujato" locator="AN 4.49" /> This preserves the distinction between possessing a capacity and exercising it well.

Buddhist and Christian accounts of conscience therefore agree more than they first appear to. Neither requires moral awareness always to speak clearly, and both make formation necessary. They differ about the ultimate character of the agent and what moral obligation is finally related to; a different structure of authority is not by itself an inconsistency.
