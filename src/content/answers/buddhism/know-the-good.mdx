## The view

The early discourses distinguish harmful actions and their unwholesome roots from wholesome alternatives. Greed, hatred, and delusion lead to injury; their absence supports different conduct.<Cite source="majjhima-nikaya-sujato" locator="MN 9.4–9.7" /><Cite source="anguttara-nikaya-sujato" locator="AN 3.65:5.1–15.1" /> Moral knowledge is practical discernment of these patterns, not simply possession of a command which automatically governs the mind.

The Kālāma discourse warns against relying on tradition, lineage, scripture, or reasoning alone. It joins personal recognition to the judgment of sensible people and to the harms which follow from undertaking a practice.<Cite source="anguttara-nikaya-sujato" locator="AN 3.65:4.1–4.3" /> It therefore should not be read as unrestricted endorsement of whatever seems right privately. The discourse proceeds into the Buddha's teaching about the unwholesome roots; its advice belongs within that practical framework.

## What this explains well

The account makes correction of judgment concrete. Reflection on whether an action harms oneself or others is applied before, during, and after acting.<Cite source="majjhima-nikaya-sujato" locator="MN 61" /> Shame and moral dread are also described as guardians of the world, not as faculties Buddhism simply rejects.<Cite source="anguttara-nikaya-sujato" locator="AN 2.9" /> Knowing or seeking good involves training attention, examining consequences, and accepting correction. It need not wait for a full metaphysical argument before identifying killing, deception, or other harmful conduct.

## Christian response

The Christian agrees that harm, motive, and the reliability of judgment matter. His account likewise treats conscience as capable of accusation and excuse rather than infallible self-approval.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 2:14–15" /> The deeper disagreement concerns the final authority and scope of moral obligation. Christianity regards wrong as also answerable to a personal God; the Buddhist account identifies its harmful roots and its relation to suffering without that premise. This is a disputed theological difference, not a demonstration that Buddhist practical discernment cannot recognize real moral distinctions or needs Christianity before it can condemn injury.

## Pressure questions

- How are competing judgments about long-term harm adjudicated when sincere observers disagree?
- What makes an apparently beneficial practice wholesome when its motive is self-serving?

## Deep dive

#### Personal verification is not mere preference

The Kālāma discourse combines several tests. A practice may be recognized as harmful, blameworthy, and criticized by sensible people; the proposed recognition is neither bare deference nor unchecked private intuition. Removing the advice from its account of greed, hatred, and delusion would change the argument's character.<Cite source="anguttara-nikaya-sujato" locator="AN 3.65:4.1–15.1" />

Buddhaghosa further distinguishes conscience grounded in self-respect from moral dread grounded in regard for others.<Cite source="buddhaghosa-path-of-purification" locator="XIV.142" /> These contribute to restraint, but their existence does not mean that each particular judgment is reliable. The canonical framework requires reflection on action and harm, while other discourses identify distortions in perception and view.<Cite source="majjhima-nikaya-sujato" locator="MN 61" /><Cite source="anguttara-nikaya-sujato" locator="AN 4.49" /> This preserves the distinction between possessing a capacity and exercising it well.

The apparent disagreement with Christian conscience should therefore be stated carefully. Neither account requires that moral awareness always speak clearly; both make formation necessary. They differ about the ultimate character of the agent and what moral obligation is finally related to. This page does not infer an inconsistency simply because Buddhism's authority structure differs from a revelation-centred Christian one.
