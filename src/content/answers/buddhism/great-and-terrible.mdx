## The view

Buddhist traditions explain the human paradox as one mind under two conditions: beings are corrupted when the mind is corrupted, and purified when it is purified.<Cite source="samyutta-nikaya-sujato" locator="SN 22.100" /> Action is intention,<Cite source="anguttara-nikaya-sujato" locator="AN 6.63" /> and intentions grow from roots. Greed, hatred and delusion produce killing, deceit and the oppression practised by those who think "I'm powerful, I want power"; contentment, love and understanding produce the reverse.<Cite source="anguttara-nikaya-sujato" locator="AN 3.69" /> Beneath the roots lies ignorance, above all the misreading of an impermanent, dependently arisen process as a fixed self to be fed and defended. Śāntideva traces every calamity, fear and suffering in the world to grasping at a self.<Cite source="shantideva-bodhicaryavatara" locator="8.134" /> This is not the claim that persons do not exist: there is "no doer over and above the doing," yet the wise still speak of a doer as a matter of common usage.<Cite source="buddhaghosa-path-of-purification" locator="XIX.19–20" /> Because the corruptions are conditioned habits, not an essence, they are only "visiting"<Cite source="anguttara-nikaya-sujato" locator="AN 1.49–52" /> and can be uprooted. Knowing the good does not by itself cure us, though: latent tendencies outlast understanding until deeper insight and a better joy remove them.<Cite source="majjhima-nikaya-sujato" locator="MN 14.2–4" />

## What this explains well

The analysis gives one economical account of the whole range of conduct. The capacity that loves is the capacity that grasps, which is why Buddhaghosa calls greed the "near enemy" of loving-kindness.<Cite source="buddhaghosa-path-of-purification" locator="IX.98" /> It describes knowing wrongdoing precisely: Śāntideva asks how enemies without hands or feet have made him their slave.<Cite source="shantideva-bodhicaryavatara" locator="4.28" /> It links domination and self-destruction as one mistake about where happiness lies.<Cite source="shantideva-bodhicaryavatara" locator="1.28" /> And it explains why the disorder appears in everyone before they choose anything in this life: latent tendencies are present even in infants,<Cite source="majjhima-nikaya-sujato" locator="MN 64.3" /> traced to karmic impressions carried over from past action.<Cite source="vasubandhu-trimsika" locator="19" />

## Christian response

The Christian recognizes the analysis of craving and self-grasping as exact. Augustine found the same in himself: a perverse will hardened into habit, until "custom not resisted became necessity."<Cite source="augustine-confessions" locator="VIII.5.10" /> Nor does the Christian argue that Buddhism lacks real wrongs against others; its practice asks one to recognize a transgression, make amends and properly accept another's confession.<Cite source="anguttara-nikaya-sujato" locator="AN 3.4" />

The Christian addition is narrower: every wrong is also an offence against God. Scripture puts the root in refusal, people who "knew God" yet did not honour him,<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 1:21" /> and "Against you, you only, have I sinned"<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Psalm 51:4" /> names an answerability that, the Christian argues, no human party's forgiveness discharges. This is a claim of explanatory adequacy resting on a disputed ultimate premise: that a personal God exists to whom persons answer.

That shapes the remedy. If wrongdoing offends God as well as harming others, it needs God's forgiveness as well as cleansing. Shinran's reliance on Amida's vow, made above all for the evil person, stands close to grace.<Cite source="shinran-tannisho-cws" locator="3" /> The remaining disagreement is whether the one who saves is also the God who was wronged, so that deliverance is personal forgiveness and not only release. Christianity says he is, and that in Christ the wronged God himself bears the cost of forgiving (see <QuestionLink question="guilt" worldview="christianity">the Christian account of guilt</QuestionLink>).

## Pressure questions

- If someone has made amends and been forgiven by everyone they harmed, yet still feels a guilt no human party can lift, how should that remainder be understood?
- Does a person's worth rest on the rare opportunity of human birth, on a buddha-nature all beings share, or on something belonging to this particular person?
- If latent tendencies outlast understanding, what finally uproots them: insight, the power of practice, or another's vow?

## Deep dive

#### Roots, craving and latent tendencies

The early discourses call killing, stealing, false speech, ill will and wrong view "unskillful," rooted in greed, hate and delusion; their opposites are rooted in contentment, love and understanding.<Cite source="majjhima-nikaya-sujato" locator="MN 9.4–9.7" /> Craving is the origin of suffering,<Cite source="samyutta-nikaya-sujato" locator="SN 56.11" /> and for its sake "kings fight with kings" and parents with children.<Cite source="majjhima-nikaya-sujato" locator="MN 13.11" /> Ignorance stands at the head of dependent origination, yet is no causeless first principle: it has "no first beginning" but its own conditions.<Cite source="buddhaghosa-path-of-purification" locator="XVII.36–37" /> Disorder is also dispositional. Seven "inherent tendencies," from sensual greed and resentment to conceit and ignorance, inhere "as cause for the arising" of defilement "again and again."<Cite source="buddhaghosa-path-of-purification" locator="XXII.60" /> Vasubandhu's Yogācāra goes deeper still: beneath ordinary awareness a subtle mentation constantly takes the mind-stream to be a self, accompanied by self-view, self-conceit and self-love.<Cite source="vasubandhu-trimsika" locator="6" />

#### Knowing the good and doing otherwise

Mahānāma has long understood that greed, hate and delusion corrupt the mind, yet they still occupy his. The Buddha's diagnosis is that one who has not found a happiness "apart from sensual pleasures" can return to them even with right understanding.<Cite source="majjhima-nikaya-sujato" locator="MN 14.2–4" /> Buddhaghosa's image for ignorance is a tormented cow drinking scalding water that only harms her.<Cite source="buddhaghosa-path-of-purification" locator="XVII.39" /> Dōgen's *Shoaku makusa* retells an exchange in which a poet objects that the precept against evil is something a three-year-old could say, and the master replies that even an eighty-year-old cannot practise it. Refraining from evil, Dōgen insists, is not first contrived by the ordinary person's will; it appears as the power of practice.<Cite source="dogen-shobogenzo-ja" locator="Shoaku makusa" />

#### Agency, desire and conscience

The aggregates are not-self because they cannot be controlled and lead to affliction.<Cite source="samyutta-nikaya-sujato" locator="SN 22.59" /> Nāgārjuna turns the usual objection around: if things had a fixed intrinsic nature, no one could do right or wrong or progress toward awakening.<Cite source="nagarjuna-mmk" locator="24.32–33" /> Desire as such is not condemned; right effort means generating desire (*chanda*) for wholesome states.<Cite source="samyutta-nikaya-sujato" locator="SN 45.8" /> Conscience belongs to the same equipment: Buddhaghosa defines it as disgust at evil grounded in self-respect, and moral dread as fear of evil grounded in respect for others.<Cite source="buddhaghosa-path-of-purification" locator="XIV.142" /> Śāntideva puts the shared root in one verse: the world's suffering comes from wanting happiness for oneself, and its happiness from wanting it for others.<Cite source="shantideva-bodhicaryavatara" locator="8.129" />

#### Buddha-nature, dignity and the remedy

Here the schools diverge. In early and Theravāda sources, the value of human life lies chiefly in its rare opportunity, imaged as a blind turtle surfacing through a drifting yoke.<Cite source="samyutta-nikaya-sujato" locator="SN 56.47–48" /> Buddha-nature texts teach that the mind's nature is luminous and its defilements adventitious,<Cite source="ratnagotravibhaga-johnston" locator="I.63" /> and that moral aspiration itself is evidence of the buddha-element.<Cite source="ratnagotravibhaga-johnston" locator="I.40–41" /> Not all Mahāyāna treats defilement as merely adventitious: Tiantai held that evil is inherently included in buddhahood.<Cite source="sep-buddhism-tiantai" locator="§4.1" /> Dōgen rereads the line that all beings have buddha-nature to mean that the whole of being is buddha-nature, and rejects any reading that makes it a hidden self.<Cite source="dogen-shobogenzo-ja" locator="Busshō" /> Most strands hold the condition curable for all; Xuanzang's East Asian Yogācāra taught that one lineage of beings cannot attain nirvāṇa;<Cite source="sep-korean-philosophy" locator="§2.2" /> buddha-nature texts read such statements as warnings, not as permanent exclusion.<Cite source="ratnagotravibhaga-johnston" locator="I.41 vyākhyā" /> Where gradualist schools cure ignorance by cultivated knowledge,<Cite source="nagarjuna-mmk" locator="26.11" /> Shinran's Pure Land teaching relies on Amida's vow rather than self-power.

#### A Christian note: aspiration and dignity

The Christian reads moral aspiration not as a buddha-element but as the work of an ineradicable sense of deity,<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="I.3.1" /> and holds that the image of God belongs to each person, grounding honour owed even to the unworthy and the enemy.<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="III.7.6" /> With Tiantai the disagreement sharpens, because for Augustine evil is a privation of good, not a constituent of what is real.<Cite source="augustine-enchiridion" locator="11" /> These are theological disagreements, not demonstrations.
