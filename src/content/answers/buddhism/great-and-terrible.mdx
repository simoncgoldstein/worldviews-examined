## The view

Buddhist traditions explain the human paradox as one mind under two conditions. The same mind
that devised paintings has "for a long time ... been corrupted by greed, hate, and delusion".
Beings are corrupted when the mind is corrupted, and purified when it is purified.<Cite source="samyutta-nikaya-sujato" locator="SN 22.100" />
Human action is intention,<Cite source="anguttara-nikaya-sujato" locator="AN 6.63" /> and
intentions grow from roots. Greed, hatred and delusion produce killing, deceit and the
oppression practised by those who think "I'm powerful, I want power". Contentment, love and
understanding produce the reverse.<Cite source="anguttara-nikaya-sujato" locator="AN 3.69" />
Beneath the roots lies ignorance. Above all, it misreads an impermanent, dependently arisen
process as a fixed self that must be fed and defended. Śāntideva traces every calamity, fear and
suffering in the world to grasping at a self.<Cite source="shantideva-bodhicaryavatara" locator="8.134" />
Because this is conditioned habit, not an essence, the mind's corruptions are only "visiting".<Cite source="anguttara-nikaya-sujato" locator="AN 1.49–52" />
Buddha-nature texts teach that the mind's nature is luminous and its defilements adventitious.<Cite source="ratnagotravibhaga-johnston" locator="I.63" />
Human birth is rare, and to be born human while a Buddha's teaching is present is an
opportunity for awakening that should not be wasted.<Cite source="samyutta-nikaya-sujato" locator="SN 56.48" /> Knowing the
good does not by itself cure us. Latent tendencies outlast understanding until deeper insight
and a better joy uproot them.<Cite source="majjhima-nikaya-sujato" locator="MN 14.2–4" />

## What this explains well

Buddhist analysis gives one economical account of the whole range of human conduct. The
capacity that loves is the same capacity that grasps. That is why Buddhaghosa calls greed the
"near enemy" of loving-kindness.<Cite source="buddhaghosa-path-of-purification" locator="IX.98" />
It describes knowingly doing wrong precisely. A person can understand that greed corrupts the
mind and still be overtaken by it.<Cite source="majjhima-nikaya-sujato" locator="MN 14.2–4" />
Śāntideva asks how enemies without hands or feet have made him their slave.<Cite source="shantideva-bodhicaryavatara" locator="4.28" />
It also links domination and self-destruction as one mistake about where happiness lies. People
who seek happiness destroy their own happiness as if it were an enemy.<Cite source="shantideva-bodhicaryavatara" locator="1.28" />
Latent tendencies are present even in infants.<Cite source="majjhima-nikaya-sujato" locator="MN 64.3" />
This explains why the disorder shows up in everyone before they choose anything in this life.
The tradition traces it to karmic impressions from past action, which carry over to produce new lives.<Cite source="vasubandhu-trimsika" locator="19" />
Moral shame and moral dread, "two bright things [that] protect the world", give conscience a
native place in human nature.<Cite source="anguttara-nikaya-sujato" locator="AN 2.9" />

## Christian response

The Christian recognizes the analysis of craving and self-grasping as exact. Mahānāma understands that greed corrupts the mind and is still overtaken by it.<Cite source="majjhima-nikaya-sujato" locator="MN 14.2–4" /> Augustine found the same in himself: a perverse will hardened into habit, and "custom not resisted became necessity."<Cite source="augustine-confessions" locator="VIII.5.10" /> Śāntideva's teaching that the world's joy comes from wanting others to be happy<Cite source="shantideva-bodhicaryavatara" locator="8.129" /> stands close to Augustine's two loves.<Cite source="augustine-city-of-god" locator="XIV.28" />

The Christian does not argue that Buddhism lacks real wrongs against others. Its practice asks one to recognize a transgression, make amends and properly accept another's confession.<Cite source="anguttara-nikaya-sujato" locator="AN 3.4" /> A monk who refuses a fellow monk's confession is called a fool.<Cite source="samyutta-nikaya-sujato" locator="SN 11.24" /> Rāhula is told to confess a harmful deed to a teacher or companion and to restrain himself afterward.<Cite source="majjhima-nikaya-sujato" locator="MN 61.11" /> No-self, rightly understood, keeps a conventional doer who acts and reaps.<Cite source="buddhaghosa-path-of-purification" locator="XIX.19–20" /> Conscience rests on self-respect and respect for others.<Cite source="buddhaghosa-path-of-purification" locator="XIV.142" />

The Christian addition is narrower: every wrong is also an offence against God. Scripture puts the root in refusal: people "knew God" yet did not honour him.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 1:21" /> "Against you, you only, have I sinned"<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Psalm 51:4" /> names an answerability that, the Christian argues, no human party's forgiveness discharges. This is a claim of explanatory adequacy resting on a disputed ultimate premise: that a personal God exists to whom persons answer.

That shapes the remedy (redemptive adequacy). If wrongdoing offends God as well as harming others, it needs God's forgiveness as well as cleansing.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="1 John 1:9" /> Gradualist schools cure ignorance by cultivated knowledge.<Cite source="nagarjuna-mmk" locator="26.11" /> Shinran instead relies on Amida's vow, made for the evil person.<Cite source="shinran-tannisho-cws" locator="3" /> That is close to grace. The remaining disagreement is theological: whether the one who saves is also the God who was wronged, so that deliverance is personal forgiveness and not only release.

## Pressure questions

- Buddhist practice treats wrongs against others as real and answerable through confession and amends. If someone has made amends and been forgiven by everyone they harmed, yet still feels a guilt that no human party can lift, how should that remainder be understood?
- Does a person's worth rest on the rare opportunity of human birth, on a buddha-nature shared by all beings, or on something belonging to this particular person? Which best explains the honour owed to someone who squanders the opportunity?
- If latent tendencies outlast understanding, what finally uproots them: cultivated insight, the power of practice, or another's vow? What reason does each school give for thinking that power is sufficient?

## Deep dive

#### Roots, intention and latent tendencies

The early discourses analyse conduct by its roots. Killing, stealing, sexual misconduct, false
or harsh speech, covetousness, ill will and wrong view are "unskillful", and their root is
greed, hate and delusion. Their opposites have contentment, love and understanding as their
roots.<Cite source="majjhima-nikaya-sujato" locator="MN 9.4–9.7" /> Buddhaghosa gives the
wholesome roots positive content. Non-greed is like "a water drop on a lotus leaf"; non-hate is
like "a gentle friend"; non-delusion illuminates "like a lamp".<Cite source="buddhaghosa-path-of-purification" locator="XIV.143" />

Craving, whether for sense pleasure, for existence or for non-existence, is the origin of
suffering.<Cite source="samyutta-nikaya-sujato" locator="SN 56.11" /> For its sake "kings fight
with kings" and parents fight with their children.<Cite source="majjhima-nikaya-sujato" locator="MN 13.11" />
Ignorance stands at the head of dependent origination.<Cite source="samyutta-nikaya-sujato" locator="SN 12.1" />
It is not a causeless first principle, though. It has "no first beginning", yet it still has
its own conditions.<Cite source="buddhaghosa-path-of-purification" locator="XVII.36–37" />

Disorder is also a matter of disposition. Seven "inherent tendencies" inhere "as cause for the
arising" of defilement "again and again": sensual greed, resentment, conceit, views, doubt, greed
for becoming, and ignorance.<Cite source="buddhaghosa-path-of-purification" locator="XXII.60" />
Vasubandhu's Yogācāra takes the analysis deeper. Beneath ordinary awareness, a subtle mentation
constantly takes the mind-stream to be a self, accompanied by self-view, self-delusion,
self-conceit and self-love.<Cite source="vasubandhu-trimsika" locator="6" />

#### Knowing the good and doing otherwise

The canon faces weakness of will directly. Mahānāma has long understood that greed, hate and
delusion corrupt the mind, yet they still occupy his. The Buddha's diagnosis is that something
in him remains unabandoned. Even someone with right understanding can return to sensual
pleasures if they have not found a happiness "apart from sensual pleasures".<Cite source="majjhima-nikaya-sujato" locator="MN 14.2–4" />
Buddhaghosa describes ignorance as "manifested as concealing".<Cite source="buddhaghosa-path-of-purification" locator="XVII.51" />
His image for it is a tormented cow that drinks scalding water even though it only harms her.<Cite source="buddhaghosa-path-of-purification" locator="XVII.39" />
Śāntideva writes from the inside. Having understood all this, he still sinks back, confused as
if by a spell, not knowing what dwells within him.<Cite source="shantideva-bodhicaryavatara" locator="4.24–27" />

Dōgen's *Shoaku makusa* retells an exchange in which the poet Bai Juyi objects that the
precept against doing evil is something a three-year-old could say. The master Niaoke replies
that even an eighty-year-old cannot practise it. Dōgen insists that refraining from evil is not
first contrived by the ordinary person's will. It appears as the power of practice.<Cite source="dogen-shobogenzo-ja" locator="Shoaku makusa" />

#### One root of greatness and misery

Śāntideva states the shared explanation in a single verse. All the suffering in the world comes
from wanting happiness for oneself, and all its happiness from wanting happiness for others.<Cite source="shantideva-bodhicaryavatara" locator="8.129" />
Both rest on the same universal wish to be happy. And because suffering has no real owner, there
is no principled reason to care only about one's own.<Cite source="shantideva-bodhicaryavatara" locator="8.101–102" />

The tradition does not condemn desire as such. Right effort means generating desire (*chanda*)
for wholesome states.<Cite source="samyutta-nikaya-sujato" locator="SN 45.8" /> That desire fades
once its goal is reached.<Cite source="samyutta-nikaya-sujato" locator="SN 51.15" /> Conscience
belongs to the same equipment. Buddhaghosa defines it as disgust at evil grounded in
self-respect, and moral dread as fear of evil grounded in respect for others.<Cite source="buddhaghosa-path-of-purification" locator="XIV.142" />

#### Agency without a substantial self

No-self is not the claim that persons do not exist. The aggregates are not-self because they
cannot be controlled and they lead to affliction.<Cite source="samyutta-nikaya-sujato" locator="SN 22.59" />
In Buddhaghosa's words, there is "no doer over and above the doing", yet "the wise say 'doer'"
as a matter of common usage.<Cite source="buddhaghosa-path-of-purification" locator="XIX.19–20" />
Nāgārjuna holds that agent and action depend on one another.<Cite source="nagarjuna-mmk" locator="8.12" />
He then turns the usual objection around. If things had a fixed intrinsic nature, no one could
do right or wrong, and no one could progress toward awakening.<Cite source="nagarjuna-mmk" locator="24.32–33" />
In Tsongkhapa's Gelug presentation, the ethical subject is real at the conventional level,
through dependent origination.<Cite source="sep-tsongkhapa" locator="§5" />

#### Buddha-nature, aspiration and dignity

Here the schools diverge. In early and Theravāda sources, the value of human life lies in its
rare opportunity. The image is a blind turtle surfacing through the hole of a drifting yoke.<Cite source="samyutta-nikaya-sujato" locator="SN 56.47–48" />
One discourse says the people of this continent, Jambudīpa, surpass both the people of
Uttarakuru and the gods of the Thirty-Three in three respects: "Bravery, mindfulness, and the
spiritual life is lived here."<Cite source="anguttara-nikaya-sujato" locator="AN 9.21" />

The *Ratnagotravibhāga* goes further: all embodied beings always bear the buddha-element.<Cite source="ratnagotravibhaga-johnston" locator="I.28" />
Moral aspiration itself is evidence of that element. Without it there would be no revulsion at
suffering and no longing for nirvāṇa.<Cite source="ratnagotravibhaga-johnston" locator="I.40–41" />
Śāntideva uses the idea conditionally in an argument for patience. If harming others were the
nature of the foolish, anger at them would be like anger at fire for burning. If instead their
fault is adventitious and they are good by nature, anger would be like anger at the sky for
holding smoke.<Cite source="shantideva-bodhicaryavatara" locator="6.39–40" />

Not all Mahāyāna treats defilement as merely adventitious. The Chinese Tiantai school held that
evil is inherently included in buddhahood, so delusion and awakening are inseparable aspects of
one reality.<Cite source="sep-buddhism-tiantai" locator="§4.1" /> Dōgen rereads the sūtra line
that all sentient beings have buddha-nature to mean that the whole of being is buddha-nature,
and he rejects any reading that makes buddha-nature a hidden self.<Cite source="dogen-shobogenzo-ja" locator="Busshō" /> For him, practice and
realization are "not two": sitting is not a means to some later awakening.<Cite source="sep-japanese-zen" locator="§2" />

#### The remedy

Most strands hold the condition curable for all beings. For someone who has cultivated the
wholesome roots, the unwholesome are "cut off at the root, made like a palm stump". That person
is extinguished "in this very life".<Cite source="anguttara-nikaya-sujato" locator="AN 3.69" />
For Nāgārjuna, ignorance ceases through the cultivation of knowledge.<Cite source="nagarjuna-mmk" locator="26.11" />
Tsongkhapa summarises the Tibetan path. Renunciation needs bodhicitta, and both need the wisdom
that sees dependent origination and emptiness together.<Cite source="tsongkhapa-three-principal-aspects" locator="vv. 6–12" />

Universality is contested. Xuanzang's East Asian Yogācāra taught five lineages of beings, and
one of them, the icchantikas, cannot attain nirvāṇa.<Cite source="sep-korean-philosophy" locator="§2.2" />
Buddha-nature texts read such statements as warnings against hostility to the Mahāyāna, not as
permanent exclusion.<Cite source="ratnagotravibhaga-johnston" locator="I.41 vyākhyā" />
Shinran's Pure Land teaching locates the remedy in Amida's vow rather than in self-power. It
holds that the vow was made above all for the evil person.<Cite source="shinran-tannisho-cws" locator="3" />

#### A Christian note: aspiration, buddha-nature and dignity

The *Ratnagotravibhāga* reads moral aspiration as evidence of the buddha-element in all beings.<Cite source="ratnagotravibhaga-johnston" locator="I.40–41" /> The Christian offers a parallel reading: aspiration and conscience are remnants of the image of God and of an ineradicable sense of deity.<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="I.3.1" /> The two readings differ over the ground. Theravāda sources locate the value of human life chiefly in its rare opportunity.<Cite source="samyutta-nikaya-sujato" locator="SN 56.47–48" /> Buddha-nature texts locate it in a nature all beings share, which Dōgen refuses to treat as a self.<Cite source="dogen-shobogenzo-ja" locator="Busshō" /> The Christian argues that the image belongs to each person and grounds honour owed even to the unworthy and the enemy.<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="III.7.6" /> Tiantai holds that evil is inherently included in buddhahood.<Cite source="sep-buddhism-tiantai" locator="§4.1" /> Here the disagreement sharpens, because for Augustine evil is a privation of good, not a constituent of what is real.<Cite source="augustine-enchiridion" locator="11" /> These are Christian theological disagreements, not demonstrations.
