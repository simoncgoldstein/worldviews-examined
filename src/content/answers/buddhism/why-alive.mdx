## The view

Buddhist traditions do not trace human existence to a creator's purpose; craving and ignorance condition further births. The question is what this life should be used for.

The early discourses answer with two quests. The ignoble quest is when someone who is liable to be reborn, to grow old, fall sick and die seeks what is also liable to these things, and the Buddha lists "partners and children" among them, along with servants, livestock and gold. The noble quest is to understand the drawbacks of all this and to seek "that which is free of rebirth, the supreme sanctuary from the yoke, extinguishment."<Cite source="majjhima-nikaya-sujato" locator="MN 26:5–13" />

Human life is the best place to undertake it. A human birth is as rare as a blind turtle putting its head through a yoke drifting on the ocean, and it is precious because the teaching can now be heard.<Cite source="samyutta-nikaya-sujato" locator="SN 56.47–48" /> Humans surpass even the gods in bravery and mindfulness, and the spiritual life is lived here.<Cite source="anguttara-nikaya-sujato" locator="AN 9.21" /> The going-forth of monastics is the fullest form of the quest: "Life at home is cramped and dirty, life gone forth is wide open."<Cite source="digha-nikaya-sujato" locator="DN 2:41.4" />

Mahāyāna adds a second motive. Tsongkhapa urges practitioners to value the freedoms of a human life, to renounce saṃsāra, and then to generate the aspiration for complete awakening for the sake of all beings, who are bound as one is oneself.<Cite source="tsongkhapa-three-principal-aspects" locator="verses 2–9" />

## What this explains well

The account explains why ordinary goods, however good, do not finally satisfy: they share the impermanence of the one who seeks them. It gives life an urgent direction without depending on a creator. And in its Mahāyāna form it joins personal liberation to boundless compassion, so that purpose is never merely private.

## Christian response

The Christian agrees that impermanent goods cannot be the heart's final rest, and that human life is a precious and serious opportunity. Augustine's restless heart and the Buddha's ignoble quest describe a similar experience.<Cite source="augustine-confessions" locator="I.1.1" /> The difference is in the answer. Augustine's rest is in a personal God for whom the heart was made, while the noble quest seeks the unconditioned, which is not a person who loves. The disagreement is about ultimate reality, not a contradiction in the Buddhist account. The Christian also asks whether listing partners and children among things to be transcended values persons as ends; Buddhist teachers reply that the problem is attachment, not love.

## Pressure questions

- If no one assigns life a purpose, what makes the noble quest better than the ignoble one rather than merely more peaceful?
- If the human birth is valuable as an opportunity, what is the value of a life that never takes it?
- Does the bodhisattva's aspiration for all beings presuppose that persons matter in a way the not-self teaching qualifies?

## Deep dive

#### Two quests

MN 26 is autobiographical. Before his awakening the Buddha sought what was liable to birth, aging and death; he then saw the drawbacks and sought the unborn and undying, extinguishment.<Cite source="majjhima-nikaya-sujato" locator="MN 26:5–13" /> The list of things liable to rebirth includes partners and children. The point is not that they are bad, but that seeking final security in them is seeking it where it cannot be found. Lay life has a full ethic, described in <QuestionLink question="offspring-family" worldview="buddhism">the family question</QuestionLink>, without being the noble quest's highest form.

#### Precious human birth

The image of the turtle and the yoke stresses rarity. The human birth also has a positive excellence: courage, mindfulness and the possibility of spiritual life.<Cite source="anguttara-nikaya-sujato" locator="AN 9.21" /> Tsongkhapa's teaching on the freedoms and advantages of a human life asks the practitioner to reflect that they are hard to find and easily lost, so as to turn the mind from this life's concerns.<Cite source="tsongkhapa-three-principal-aspects" locator="verses 2–4" />

#### Arahant and bodhisattva

The early ideal is the arahant, who has ended craving and will not be reborn. Mahāyāna puts forward the bodhisattva, who aspires to full Buddhahood in order to free others; for Tsongkhapa, renunciation without that aspiration does not lead to complete awakening.<Cite source="tsongkhapa-three-principal-aspects" locator="verses 7–9" /> The two ideals differ in emphasis and scope, not in denying the value of compassion or of liberation. What those goals finally are is treated in <QuestionLink question="final-end" worldview="buddhism">the final-end question</QuestionLink>; the conditioned person who pursues them is described in <QuestionLink question="what-is-man" worldview="buddhism">the human-nature anchor</QuestionLink>.
