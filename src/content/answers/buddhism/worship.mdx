## The view

Buddhist traditions do not worship a creator God, and "worship" is used here only comparatively for reverence, refuge, offering and homage. Gods (*devas*) belong to the Buddhist cosmos, but they are beings within saṃsāra, not the refuge. The refuge is the Three Jewels: the Buddha, the Dhamma he taught and the Saṅgha of those who practise it.

The early texts nonetheless treat reverence as a human need. Shortly after his awakening the Buddha reflects that "one without respect and reverence lives in suffering," and asks whom he should honour and rely on. Finding no one more accomplished than himself, he resolves: "Why don't I honor and respect and rely on the same Dhamma to which I was awakened?"<Cite source="samyutta-nikaya-sujato" locator="SN 6.2" /> Reverence here is not submission to a ruler but orientation to the truth that liberates.

Honour to the Buddha is defined by practice. Near his death the Buddha says that whoever practises in line with the teaching honours him "with the highest honor."<Cite source="digha-nikaya-sujato" locator="DN 16:5.3.8–9" /> Yet devotion is not rejected. After his death the Mallas honour his relics for seven days "with dance and song and music and garlands and fragrances."<Cite source="digha-nikaya-sujato" locator="DN 16:6.23.10" />

In Pure Land Buddhism, devotion becomes entrusting oneself to Amitābha Buddha's vow, which Shinran taught as reliance on Other Power rather than one's own.<Cite source="shinran-tannisho-cws" locator="chs. 1, 3" />

## What this explains well

The account explains reverence without a creator: humans need something to honour and rely on, and the right object is the truth that frees them. It turns devotion toward practice and can redirect religious customs into ethics.

## Christian response

The Christian agrees that humans need to revere and rely on something beyond themselves, and that devotion without obedience is empty. Jesus also said that those who love him keep his commandments.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="John 14:15" />

The difference is the object. The Buddha honours the Dhamma, an impersonal truth; Christians worship a personal God who creates, speaks and loves. The Christian argues that the need for reverence the Buddha describes is best explained by a personal Creator for whom humans were made, and that an impersonal truth can be respected but not loved in return. That rests on a Christian premise about ultimate reality and is a theological disagreement, not a contradiction. With Pure Land, the question is whether Amitābha can be trusted as ultimately as the Creator.

## Pressure questions

- If reverence is a human need, why should its proper object be a truth rather than a person?
- In Pure Land devotion, is Amitābha relied on as a person who responds, or as the embodiment of the Dhamma?
- Can practice alone fully express gratitude to the one who taught it?

## Deep dive

#### Reverence and refuge

SN 6.2 shows reverence as part of the awakened life, not only a stage for beginners. The divinity Sahampati confirms the Buddha's resolve: all Buddhas of past and future honour and rely on the same Dhamma.<Cite source="samyutta-nikaya-sujato" locator="SN 6.2" /> Gods appear in the story, but as admirers of the Buddha's choice, not as its object. Taking refuge in the Three Jewels is therefore not worship of a deity. It is commitment to the teacher, the teaching and the community.

#### Honour by practice and offering

DN 16 holds the two together. The Buddha's highest honour is practice in line with the teaching,<Cite source="digha-nikaya-sujato" locator="DN 16:5.3.8–9" /> while relic veneration with music, garlands and fragrance is recorded without disapproval.<Cite source="digha-nikaya-sujato" locator="DN 16:6.23.10" /> Later Buddhist cultures developed rich traditions of stūpa veneration, image halls and chanting from these roots. Those traditions are not surveyed here.

#### Ritual reinterpreted

The Buddha also reinterprets ritual worship. Finding the young Sigālaka revering the six directions with wet clothes and raised palms, he teaches that the directions should be "revered" as relationships: parents, teachers, spouse and children, friends, workers and renunciants.<Cite source="digha-nikaya-sujato" locator="DN 31:1–2, 27" /> Reverence is turned into ethical obligation.

#### Other Power

In Pure Land teaching the eighteenth vow promises birth in the Pure Land to beings who sincerely entrust themselves and call Amitābha's name, with stated exceptions for the gravest offences.<Cite source="three-pure-land-sutras-inagaki" locator="Larger Sutra, vow 18, p. 16" /> Shinran turned this into a teaching of complete reliance, in which even the saying of the name is the working of the vow, not a meritorious act.<Cite source="shinran-tannisho-cws" locator="chs. 1, 3" /> That form of devotion is discussed further in <QuestionLink question="self-salvation" worldview="buddhism">the liberation anchor</QuestionLink>.
