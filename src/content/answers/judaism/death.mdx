## The view

Death is the appointed limit of a creaturely life. Psalm 90 counts human years as few and asks for wisdom in numbering them, and Genesis places the return to dust within the account of Adam's disobedience.<Cite source="tanakh-jps-gender-sensitive" locator="Genesis 3:19; Psalm 90:10–12" /> Mortality therefore makes life an occasion for moral attention: Avot reminds each person that he will die and give account before God.<Cite source="mishnah-kulp" locator="Avot 4:22" /> Nor is death the last word. The Mishnah excludes from the World to Come anyone who says the resurrection of the dead is not taught in the Torah (see <QuestionLink question="after-death" worldview="judaism">after death</QuestionLink>).<Cite source="mishnah-kulp" locator="Sanhedrin 10:1" />

Rabbinic discussion complicates any simple equation between personal sin and death. Shabbat debates the claim that death always results from sin and concludes that death can occur without the deceased person's own sin; its account of four exemplary people connects their mortality with Adam's encounter with the serpent.<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Shabbat 55a–b" /> That does not establish inherited personal guilt. Genesis Rabbah preserves still another strand: Rabbi Meir interprets creation's goodness as including death, while another interpretation connects mortality with the danger of human self-deification.<Cite source="midrash-rabbah-sefaria" locator="9:5" /> These are distinct voices, and the tradition keeps them so.

## What this explains well

Mortality can be understood as a limit on pride and a reason to live wisely, without interpreting each bereavement as a readable verdict on the victim. Avot emphasizes unavoidable death and subsequent accountability, while another saying values an hour of repentance and good deeds in this world.<Cite source="mishnah-kulp" locator="Avot 4:22; 4:17" /> Finite life becomes morally urgent. Its authoritative discussions also keep their disagreements rather than reducing them to a single maxim.

## Christian response

The Christian shares the connection between mortality, Adam and accountability.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 5:12; 1 Corinthians 15:26" /><Cite source="wcf" locator="6.6" /> The Talmud's connection with Adam is not the Reformed doctrine of original guilt: Shabbat ties death to Adam's story while denying that each death proves personal sin. Rabbinic Judaism also awaits death's defeat: the Mishnah excludes from the World to Come anyone who says the resurrection of the dead is not taught in the Torah.<Cite source="mishnah-kulp" locator="Sanhedrin 10:1" /> The Christian difference is that death is unambiguously an enemy and a misery of the fallen estate, never good in itself, and that its defeat has already begun in Christ's resurrection (see <QuestionLink question="death" worldview="christianity">the Christian answer</QuestionLink>). The question the Christian puts is how death can be good in one rabbinic strand while remaining bound up with judgment and loss in others. The rabbinic reply is that the strands address different questions. The disagreement is theological.

## Pressure questions

- In what sense can death be called good without making particular losses desirable?
- How should Adam's legacy be distinguished from the personal guilt of those who die?

## Deep dive

#### A debate, not a diagnosis of every death

The Shabbat discussion tests a proposed universal connection. Its counterexamples matter because they prevent the death of a particular person from proving that person's sinfulness.<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Shabbat 55a–b" /> The reference to Adam still offers a story about the human condition, but an ancestral connection is not personal blame.

Genesis Rabbah's interpretation of death as good appears within a collection of interpretations of creation's goodness. It is not a claim that Judaism celebrates death or that mourners misunderstand their loss.<Cite source="midrash-rabbah-sefaria" locator="9:5" /> The alternative reading, about human self-deification, explains a moral purpose served by limits on human life. Both strands belong to the tradition.

Maimonides provides a related distinction when his account of evils separates those associated with material existence from injuries people inflict and harms generated by their own conduct.<Cite source="maimonides-guide-friedlander" locator="III.12" /> The categories help distinguish vulnerability from culpable action; they do not make all deaths identical in cause or moral significance.

Psalm 90 and Avot then direct attention to the use of the time available. Wisdom, repentance and good deeds concern living under mortality rather than an explanation of each person's lifespan. The plurality resembles that preserved in <QuestionLink question="suffering" worldview="judaism">the suffering answer</QuestionLink>. Resurrection and the World to Come are treated in <QuestionLink question="after-death" worldview="judaism">after death</QuestionLink>.
