## The view

Genesis presents return to dust within the account of Adam's disobedience. Psalm 90 asks for wisdom in numbering finite days.<Cite source="tanakh-jps-gender-sensitive" locator="Genesis 3:19; Psalm 90:10–12" /> These are not simply competing descriptions of a physical process: mortality also makes life an occasion for moral attention.

Rabbinic discussion complicates any simple equation between personal sin and death. Shabbat debates the claim that death always results from sin and concludes that death can occur without the deceased person's own sin; its account of four exemplary people connects their mortality with Adam's encounter with the serpent.<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Shabbat 55a–b" /> That does not establish inherited personal guilt. Genesis Rabbah preserves still another strand: Rabbi Meir interprets creation's goodness as including death, while another interpretation connects mortality with the danger of human self-deification.<Cite source="midrash-rabbah-sefaria" locator="9:5" /> These voices must remain distinguishable.

## What this explains well

Mortality can be understood as a limit on pride and a reason to live wisely, without interpreting each bereavement as a readable verdict on the victim. Avot emphasizes unavoidable death and subsequent accountability, while another saying values an hour of repentance and good deeds in this world.<Cite source="mishnah-kulp" locator="Avot 4:22; 4:17" /> Finite life becomes morally urgent. This account also preserves the fact that authoritative rabbinic discussion contains disagreement, rather than constructing a single maxim and attributing it to all Judaism.

## Christian response

The Christian shares the connection between mortality, Adam and accountability, but gives death a more unified confessional status as an enemy and a misery of the fallen estate.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 5:12; 1 Corinthians 15:26" /><Cite source="wcf" locator="6.6" /> That comparison should not turn the Talmud's connection with Adam into the Reformed doctrine of original guilt. The strongest issue is how death can be good in one rabbinic strand while remaining connected to judgment and loss in others.

## Pressure questions

- In what sense can death be called good without making particular losses desirable?
- How should Adam's legacy be distinguished from the personal guilt of those who die?

## Deep dive

#### A debate, not a diagnosis of every death

The Shabbat discussion tests a proposed universal connection. Its counterexamples matter because they prevent the death of a particular person from proving that person's sinfulness.<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Shabbat 55a–b" /> The reference to Adam still offers a story about the human condition, but the two levels must not collapse: an ancestral connection is not the same assertion as personal blame.

Genesis Rabbah's interpretation of death as good also needs careful scope. It appears within a collection of interpretations of creation's goodness. It should not be expanded into the claim that Judaism universally celebrates death, or that mourners misunderstand their loss.<Cite source="midrash-rabbah-sefaria" locator="9:5" /> The alternative about human self-deification instead explains a moral purpose served by limits on human life. Preserving both strands makes the question harder but the representation more faithful.

Maimonides provides a related distinction when his account of evils separates those associated with material existence from injuries people inflict and harms generated by their own conduct.<Cite source="maimonides-guide-friedlander" locator="III.12" /> The categories help distinguish vulnerability from culpable action; they do not make all deaths identical in cause or moral significance.

Psalm 90 and Avot then direct attention to the use of the time available. Wisdom, repentance and good deeds concern living under mortality rather than an explanation of each person's lifespan. The plurality resembles that preserved in <QuestionLink question="suffering" worldview="judaism">the suffering answer</QuestionLink>. Questions about resurrection and the World to Come belong to <QuestionLink question="after-death" worldview="judaism">after death</QuestionLink>; the present scope is mortality's meaning and its disputed relation to sin.
