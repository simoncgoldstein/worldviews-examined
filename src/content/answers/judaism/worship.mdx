## The view

The Torah's demand is exclusive. The God who brought Israel out of Egypt forbids other gods beside him and any sculptured image made for worship.<Cite source="tanakh-jps-gender-sensitive" locator="Exodus 20:2–6" /> The Shema joins confession of God's oneness to love of God with all one's heart, soul and might.<Cite source="tanakh-jps-gender-sensitive" locator="Deuteronomy 6:4–5" /> The Mishnah names worship (*avodah*, Temple service) as one of the three pillars on which the world stands, beside Torah and acts of kindness.<Cite source="mishnah-kulp" locator="Avot 1:2" />

When the Temple fell, prayer took up that service. The Talmud reads the command to serve God "with all your heart" and asks, "Which is the service of God that is performed in the heart? You must say that this is referring to prayer."<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Ta'anit 2a" /> Love of God is total. The Mishnah teaches that one must love God with both inclinations, the good and the evil.<Cite source="mishnah-kulp" locator="Berakhot 9:5" />

Maimonides also explains why humans worship wrongly. In the generation of Enosh, people reasoned that God had made the stars and spheres his servants and that it was fitting to honour them. They built temples to the stars, intending to honour God through them, until in time the Creator was forgotten and only the images remained. Abraham rediscovered the one God by reflection while still a child.<Cite source="maimonides-mishneh-torah-touger" locator="Avodah Zarah 1:1–3" /> Idolatry, on this account, begins as misdirected honour, not as atheism.

## What this explains well

The account explains why worship is so persistent: humans are moved by the world to reverence. It explains how idolatry can grow from good intentions, so that the line between honour and worship must be guarded. And its shift from sacrifice to prayer shows how worship survived the loss of its institutions.

## Christian response

The Christian shares the whole framework: one God, worship due to him alone, prayer as the service of the heart, idolatry as honour transferred to creatures. Jesus cites the Shema as the first commandment.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Mark 12:29–30" /> The deep disagreement is over Jesus. Christians worship Jesus as God incarnate, and from the Jewish side that looks like exactly the transfer of honour Maimonides describes. The Christian answer is that the Son is not a creature but the one God revealed in the flesh, so that worship of him is not idolatry. That is a revelatory and doctrinal disagreement, and it cannot be settled by the shared principle alone.

## Pressure questions

- If honour given to God's servants can slide into idolatry, how should one regard reverence for sages, holy places or the Torah itself?
- Does prayer replace sacrifice fully, or does the absence of the Temple leave worship incomplete?
- If love of God must include the evil inclination, what does such love look like in practice?

## Deep dive

#### From Temple to prayer

The Hebrew *avodah* covers the sacrificial service of the Temple and, by extension, all service of God. The Talmud's reading of Deuteronomy 11:13 is the classic warrant for treating prayer as *avodah*: serving God with the heart means prayer.<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Ta'anit 2a" /> Avot's "Temple service" thus remained a pillar after the Temple's destruction, now carried by prayer, study and deeds. Rabbinic texts link all three, so that study and kindness are also forms of worship.<Cite source="mishnah-kulp" locator="Avot 1:2" />

#### Idolatry's origin

Maimonides' history of idolatry is a philosophical reconstruction. Touger's edition adds notes drawing on midrashic narratives about Abraham, which should be kept distinct from Maimonides' own text.<Cite source="maimonides-mishneh-torah-touger" locator="Avodah Zarah 1:1–3" /> The key claim is that idolatry starts from a true premise, that the stars serve God, and a false inference, that they deserve honour. The commandment therefore forbids not only worship of other gods but also worship of God through created intermediaries.

#### Love and awe

Maimonides explains how love of God arises: contemplating God's works and seeing his wisdom in them, a person is moved to love, praise and long to know him, and then to awe at his own smallness.<Cite source="maimonides-mishneh-torah-touger" locator="Yesodei ha-Torah 2:1–2" /> His intellectual emphasis is one strand. The Mishnah's demand to love God with both inclinations, with all one's resources and in every circumstance, ties worship to the whole of life.<Cite source="mishnah-kulp" locator="Berakhot 9:5" /> The connection between worship and the purpose of human life is drawn in <QuestionLink question="why-alive" worldview="judaism">why are we alive?</QuestionLink>, and the creature who worships is described in <QuestionLink question="what-is-man" worldview="judaism">the human-nature anchor</QuestionLink>.
