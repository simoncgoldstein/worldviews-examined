## The view

In rabbinic Judaism the final court of appeal is God's public revelation at Mount Sinai. The whole people heard God's voice, and the Torah concludes: "It has been clearly demonstrated to you that the ETERNAL alone is God."<Cite source="tanakh-jps-gender-sensitive" locator="Deuteronomy 4:32–35" /> Maimonides makes Sinai, not miracles, the reason Israel trusts Moses, since wonders can be faked by sorcery: "Our eyes saw, and not a stranger's. Our ears heard, and not another's."<Cite source="maimonides-mishneh-torah-touger" locator="Foundations of the Torah 8:1" />

What was given at Sinai, on the rabbinic account, is the Torah in two forms. The Written Torah is the Five Books of Moses. The Oral Torah is the teaching that explains and applies it, received with it and handed down in an unbroken chain: "Moses received the Torah at Sinai and transmitted it to Joshua, Joshua to the elders, and the elders to the prophets, and the prophets to the Men of the Great Assembly."<Cite source="mishnah-kulp" locator="Avot 1:1" /> In the tradition's own understanding, the Oral Torah is not a later layer laid over Scripture but the transmission of Sinai itself.

Interpretive authority belongs to the authorized court. Deuteronomy sends cases "too baffling for you to decide" to the judges in the place God will choose, and commands: "you must not deviate from the verdict that they announce to you either to the right or to the left."<Cite source="tanakh-jps-gender-sensitive" locator="Deuteronomy 17:8–11" /> Nachmanides presses the point: one must follow the court even when convinced it is wrong, because God "gave me the Torah as taught by them, even if they were to err."<Cite source="ramban-torah-chavel" locator="on Deuteronomy 17:11" /> The Talmud's most famous story makes the same point from the other side. Rabbi Eliezer supports his ruling with miracles and finally a voice from heaven, but Rabbi Yehoshua replies with Scripture: "It is not in heaven." The Torah has been given, and the majority of the sages decides.<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Bava Metzia 59b" />

Reason has a real but defined place (on reason as a source of knowledge, see <QuestionLink question="knowledge-possible" worldview="judaism">why we can know anything</QuestionLink>). It interprets the Torah, and it tests anyone who claims to speak for God. Saadia says that a prophet is first heard out; only if his message is reasonable do we ask for signs, "for there is no sign for the impossible."<Cite source="saadia-emunot-ibn-tibbon" locator="III.8" note="Hebrew; our translation of the phrase" /> No prophet may add to the Torah or change it, whatever wonders he performs.<Cite source="maimonides-mishneh-torah-touger" locator="Foundations of the Torah 9:1" />

## What this explains well

The structure joins a fixed revelation to a living practice of interpretation. It stops private visions from overriding the law and makes reading Scripture a shared, reasoned discipline across generations.

## Christian response

The Christian shares the Written Torah and its account of Sinai. The disagreement concerns where final interpretive authority lies. The Reformed lane holds that "the infallible rule of interpretation of Scripture is the Scripture itself" and that the supreme judge is "the Holy Spirit speaking in the Scripture," so no human court, however learned, is beyond correction.<Cite source="wcf" locator="1.9–10" /> The oven of Akhnai is the sharpest contrast: there the majority is final even against a heavenly voice. This is a theological disagreement about the status of the Oral Torah and the court, not a charge of incoherence. Whether Sinai and its transmission happened as the tradition says is a historical question for the Revelation & History domain.

## Pressure questions

- If the court's ruling binds even when it errs, what corrects the court?
- If no heavenly voice can overrule the majority, how would the community recognize a genuinely new word from God?

## Deep dive

#### The oven of Akhnai

The story in Bava Metzia concerns whether a certain oven can become ritually impure. Rabbi Eliezer brings every argument, then asks a carob tree, a stream and the walls of the study hall to prove him right, and each one does. Finally a heavenly voice declares that the law follows him. Rabbi Yehoshua stands and quotes "It is not in heaven." Rabbi Yirmeya explains: the Torah was already given at Sinai, and it says to follow the majority. Later, the prophet Elijah reports that God smiled and said, "My children have triumphed over Me."<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Bava Metzia 59b" /> The point is not that God is defeated. It is that God has entrusted the interpretation of his Torah to the sages' reasoned decision.

#### Following the court

Nachmanides quotes Rashi's formula, that one obeys the court "even if he tells you of the right that it is the left." He explains it with the story of Rabbi Yehoshua, who submitted to Rabban Gamaliel's ruling on the calendar although he thought it wrong. Without such obedience, Nachmanides argues, the Torah would become many Torahs.<Cite source="ramban-torah-chavel" locator="on Deuteronomy 17:11" />

#### Reason, demonstration and tradition

The medieval thinkers weigh reason differently within this structure. Maimonides holds that a passage of Scripture must be read figuratively when its literal sense is refuted by demonstration, as with passages that seem to give God a body. The eternity of the world has not been demonstrated, so the literal teaching of creation stands.<Cite source="maimonides-guide-friedlander" locator="II.25" /> Judah Halevi gives tradition the priority. Public events witnessed by a whole people and passed on without a break are firmer than philosophers' arguments.<Cite source="halevi-kuzari-hirschfeld" locator="I.25, I.83–86" />

#### Outside the rabbinic lane

The Karaites, a medieval movement, rejected the Oral Torah and its authority. They are mentioned here only as context. This page represents rabbinic Judaism, the mainstream tradition.
