## The view

The Jewish claim is that God revealed himself to a whole people at once. At Sinai he did not speak to Moses alone: the entire nation saw and heard. God came down "in the sight of all the people,"<Cite source="tanakh-jps-gender-sensitive" locator="Exodus 19:9–11" /> and afterwards told them, "You yourselves saw that I spoke to you from the very heavens"<Cite source="tanakh-jps-gender-sensitive" locator="Exodus 20:15–19" />, a meeting Moses later calls "face to face."<Cite source="tanakh-jps-gender-sensitive" locator="Deuteronomy 5:4" />

Why believe it? Maimonides answers that Israel did not believe in Moses because of his wonders, which magic can imitate, but because of Sinai, where the people saw and heard for themselves. Moses and the people were then witnesses to one another, "like two witnesses who observed the same event together."<Cite source="maimonides-mishneh-torah-touger" locator="Foundations of the Torah 8:1–2" />

Judah Halevi made this the centre of his defence of Judaism. In his dialogue the *Kuzari*, a king seeking the true religion says he could believe that God speaks to human beings only on the strength of a miracle seen "in the presence of great multitudes, who saw it distinctly, and did not learn it from reports," and examined carefully and repeatedly.<Cite source="halevi-kuzari-hirschfeld" locator="I.8" /> The rabbi answers with Sinai, where the whole people "distinctly heard the Ten Commandments."<Cite source="halevi-kuzari-hirschfeld" locator="I.87" /> Later generations know it through "uninterrupted tradition, which is equal to" experience.<Cite source="halevi-kuzari-hirschfeld" locator="I.25" />

How the Torah then governs interpretation is treated under <QuestionLink question="ultimate-authority" worldview="judaism">ultimate authority</QuestionLink>; the question here is why Sinai should be believed at all. Sinai then becomes the test for every later claim. A prophet who urges the worship of other gods is false "even if the sign or portent named to you comes true,"<Cite source="tanakh-jps-gender-sensitive" locator="Deuteronomy 13:2–4" /> and one whose oracle fails was not sent by God.<Cite source="tanakh-jps-gender-sensitive" locator="Deuteronomy 18:21–22" /> Maimonides adds that a later prophet is tested by his predictions, "many times," and may not change the Torah.<Cite source="maimonides-mishneh-torah-touger" locator="Foundations of the Torah 9:1; 10:1–2" /> Saadia argues that the Torah cannot be abrogated, which is the Jewish answer to later revelations claiming to supersede it.<Cite source="saadia-emunot-ibn-tibbon" locator="III.7" note="Hebrew; our paraphrase" />

## What this explains well

The account does not rest revelation on one person's private experience or on marvels that could be faked, and it gives clear tests for later claimants.

## Christian response

The Christian shares Sinai and the Hebrew Scriptures, and Calvin defended Moses with the same argument from public events,<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="I.8.5" /> so one standard must apply to both. The argument's strength is its form: a revelation claimed to be public and national. Its difficulty is evidential. We know the event through the Torah and the people's tradition, not through independent records from the time. Critical scholars, among them the rabbi and biblical scholar Zev Farber, find no external evidence for the exodus as narrated, judge its numbers implausible and read the Torah as a composite text.<Cite source="farber-torahs-exodus" locator="§§1–2" /> Critics of the modern "Kuzari principle" argue that a story of ancestral witness can arise many generations after the event and be received as old.<Cite source="gottlieb-sinai-argument" locator="Tal's critique" />

This page does not settle when the Torah was written, and tradition rejects the critical dating. The Christian point is narrower. The New Testament's central claim comes with letters written within about twenty-five years and naming witnesses the writer had met (see <QuestionLink question="jesus" worldview="judaism">Jesus</QuestionLink>); for Sinai, the distance between event and text is itself the disputed question. And the Christian argues that the God who spoke at Sinai has since acted publicly again, in the resurrection.

## Pressure questions

- If the Sinai argument depends on the tradition reaching back to the generation it describes, what evidence shows that it does?
- If God could make himself known publicly once, would Deuteronomy's tests exclude a later public act that called Israel to no other god?

## Deep dive

#### Wonders and witnesses

Maimonides treats Moses' wonders as practical acts: the sea was split to save Israel, manna was given because they needed food. They were not credentials. Later prophets are heeded because Moses commanded it, in the way a court follows two witnesses whose truthfulness it cannot see into.<Cite source="maimonides-mishneh-torah-touger" locator="Foundations of the Torah 8:1–2" /> This explains an apparent tension in his writing. He says a prophet sent to others is given a sign so that people will know God sent him,<Cite source="maimonides-mishneh-torah-touger" locator="Foundations of the Torah 7:7" /> yet also that belief resting on wonders is defective. Signs serve within the Torah's authority; they do not found it.

#### Halevi's argument from public history

In the *Kuzari* the king first hears a Christian and a Muslim scholar. Both appeal to the history of Israel, so he turns to the Jews as "the evidence for the divine law on earth."<Cite source="halevi-kuzari-hirschfeld" locator="I.10" /> When the Muslim scholar says the Qur'an is a miracle because no one can produce its like, the king objects that a non-Arab "cannot perceive its miraculous character."<Cite source="halevi-kuzari-hirschfeld" locator="I.5–6" /> After the rabbi describes Sinai, the king concedes that a spectacle "seen by thousands, cannot be denied."<Cite source="halevi-kuzari-hirschfeld" locator="I.88" />

#### The Kuzari principle and its critics

Dovid Gottlieb holds that the argument could be refuted by a single example of a public national event that is widely believed yet known not to have happened, and that none exists. Shlomi Tal proposes counter-cases, including the sun standing still in Joshua 10, and argues that the Sinai story rests on a single source. Gottlieb replies that the counter-cases do not meet the principle's conditions, chiefly that the event was accepted by the whole people as witnessed by their own ancestors at a time when records existed.<Cite source="gottlieb-sinai-argument" locator="Tal's critique; Gottlieb's replies" />

#### What critical scholarship says

Farber notes that no pharaoh of the period drowned and that an Israelite population of nearly three million could not have left no trace. He also argues that the stories mix myth, folklore and historiography and contain contradictory versions. A small escape of slaves might lie behind the story, but that, he says, "is not the exodus story anymore."<Cite source="farber-torahs-exodus" locator="§§1–2" /> Farber notes Joshua Berman's opposing case for a historical exodus, which could not be consulted for this page.
