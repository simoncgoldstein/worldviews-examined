## The view

In classical rabbinic Judaism the human being is one creature made from two realms. Every person is made in God's image and loved for it,<Cite source="mishnah-kulp" locator="Avot 3:14" /> standing, speaking and understanding like the angels, yet eating, procreating and dying like animals.<Cite source="midrash-rabbah-sefaria" locator="Bereshit Rabbah 8:11" /> The evil inclination belongs to creation's "very good": without it no one would build a house, marry, have children or trade.<Cite source="midrash-rabbah-sefaria" locator="Bereshit Rabbah 9:7" /> In this aggadic strand, greatness and wickedness spring largely from one created energy, governed well or badly; Maimonides instead traces human merits to the intellect and vices to matter.<Cite source="maimonides-guide-friedlander" locator="III.8" /> Humans are free: "everything is foreseen yet freedom of choice is granted."<Cite source="mishnah-kulp" locator="Avot 3:15" /> Wrongdoing grows by choice and habit, and it can be undone. God gave Torah as the inclination's "antidote,"<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Kiddushin 30b" /> and opened the way of *teshuvah*, return, by which the penitent is again "beloved and desirable" before God.<Cite source="maimonides-mishneh-torah-touger" locator="Teshuvah 7:4" />

## What this explains well

The account explains why the drives that build homes, families, markets and cities are the same drives behind envy, lust and domination, and why removing them would leave not a saint but a barren world: in one Talmudic story, imprisoning the inclination leaves not a single fresh egg in the land.<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Yoma 69b" /> It expects that the greater the person, the greater the inclination.<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Sukkah 52a" /> It names knowing wrongdoing exactly: "Our will is to perform Your will," yet the "yeast in the dough" resists.<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Berakhot 17a" /> And it makes moral change concrete: repentance is complete when one meets the same temptation, with the same strength, and refrains.<Cite source="maimonides-mishneh-torah-touger" locator="Teshuvah 2:1" />

## Christian response

Christianity shares much here: one Creator, the image of God in every person, the Hebrew Scriptures, real responsibility, covenant, and a God who forgives those who return to him. The disagreements concern how the heart is renewed and on what ground sin is forgiven. Both are Christian theological disagreements, not demonstrated deficiencies.

Both traditions affirm real change now and a further change to come. Nachmanides reads the promised circumcision of the heart as messianic; until then choice remains, and God assists whoever "comes to purify himself."<Cite source="ramban-torah-chavel" locator="on Deuteronomy 30:6" /> Reformed theology likewise expects freedom from remaining corruption only in glory, but holds that the new heart is given now, in regeneration, before which a person is "altogether passive."<Cite source="wcf" locator="10.2" /> The difference is who begins the change.

On pardon, the shared Scriptures do not tie every forgiveness to a slain animal: a poor person's flour offering brings forgiveness,<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Leviticus 5:11–13" /> Solomon asks God to forgive repentant exiles who pray toward the Temple from afar,<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="1 Kings 8:46–50" /> and the Mishnah teaches that repentance and Yom Kippur atone.<Cite source="mishnah-kulp" locator="Yoma 8:8" /> The Christian agrees that God forgave penitent Israel, but argues that every such pardon rests on one sacrifice, that of the servant whose soul makes "an offering for guilt."<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Isaiah 53:10" /> Rashi reads that servant as Israel.<Cite source="rashi-tanakh-judaica-press" locator="on Isaiah 53:3" /> Identifying him with Jesus rests on the New Testament's witness, not on the Tanakh alone.

## Pressure questions

- What will the promised circumcision of the heart give that complete *teshuvah* does not already give?
- Is the evil inclination a created good to be ruled, or a disorder that God will finally remove?
- Since the Temple's destruction, were the sacrifices one means of a mercy that never depended on them alone, or does their meaning await fulfilment or restoration?

## Deep dive

#### The yetzer: one energy, two outcomes

Scripture says the devisings of the human heart are "evil from youth,"<Cite source="tanakh-jps-gender-sensitive" locator="Genesis 8:21" /> and the Talmud adds that the inclination dominates from birth.<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Sanhedrin 91b" /> Yet the midrash refuses to call it simply bad. When the angels debate whether humans should be created, Truth and Peace object that man is "full of lies" and "full of discord"; God creates him regardless.<Cite source="midrash-rabbah-sefaria" locator="Bereshit Rabbah 8:5" /> The ideal is mastery, not elimination: "Who is mighty? He who subdues his inclination."<Cite source="mishnah-kulp" locator="Avot 4:1" /> Left unmastered, the inclination begins as a passing traveller, becomes a guest, and finally becomes master of the house.<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Sukkah 52b" /> At the end, God will slaughter it: the righteous will weep that they overcame a mountain, and the wicked that they failed before a hair.<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Sukkah 52a" />

#### Eden and Adam's legacy

Rabbinic readings of Genesis 3 vary. The Talmud concludes that some died only "due to Adam's sin with the serpent," though themselves without sin: there is "death without sin."<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Shabbat 55b" /> On this view Adam's legacy is mortality, and each person answers for his own wrongdoing.<Cite source="tanakh-jps-gender-sensitive" locator="Ezekiel 18:20" /> Medieval readers diverge. In the *Guide*, Maimonides argues that Adam first knew true and false by intellect and, after following appetite and imagination, became absorbed in the lesser register of the proper and improper.<Cite source="maimonides-guide-friedlander" locator="I.2" /> Nachmanides holds that humans originally acted "naturally," like the heavens, and that the fruit gave rise to "will and desire": choice is "a godlike attribute," yet for humans "bad."<Cite source="ramban-torah-chavel" locator="on Genesis 2:9" /> The same endowment explains both grandeur and danger.

#### Philosophers and pietists

Saadia Gaon argued that God's gift of wisdom lifts humans above all creatures, letting them farm, build, govern kingdoms and chart the stars, and makes the human being the world's "axis," and therefore the one who is commanded, warned and judged.<Cite source="saadia-emunot-ibn-tibbon" locator="IV.1" /> Maimonides identified the divine image with the intellect, not bodily shape,<Cite source="maimonides-mishneh-torah-touger" locator="Yesodei ha-Torah 4:8" /> and held that temperament may make a virtue easier or harder but never necessary.<Cite source="maimonides-eight-chapters" locator="ch. 8" /> Habad Hasidism gave the struggle a modern form: the *Tanya*'s ideal "intermediate" person never sins in deed, speech or thought, yet feels the evil reawaken after prayer, and restrains it because the mind by its created nature rules the heart.<Cite source="tanya-hebrew-sefaria" locator="Likkutei Amarim 12" />

#### Dignity: universal and particular

Rabbi Tanhuma warned that whoever demeans another should know whose likeness he disgraces.<Cite source="midrash-rabbah-sefaria" locator="Bereshit Rabbah 24:7" /> The texts also layer covenantal particularity onto this universal worth. The printed Mishnah speaks of one who "has caused a single life to perish from Israel,"<Cite source="mishnah-kulp" locator="Sanhedrin 4:5" /> while the Mishnah as given in the Jerusalem Talmud reads simply "a single life."<Cite source="yerushalmi-guggenheimer" locator="Sanhedrin 4:9" /> Judah Halevi's view that the divine influence rests on Israel in a way it does not on others is contested within Jewish thought.<Cite source="halevi-kuzari-hirschfeld" locator="I.95" /> The anthropology is broadly shared across modern streams; Reform Judaism's 1999 principles affirm that "every human being is created b'tzelem Elohim, in the image of God."<Cite source="ccar-principles-1999" locator="Statement of Principles" /> What differs is the authority of the means.
