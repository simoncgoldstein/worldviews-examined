## The view

In classical rabbinic Judaism, the human being is a single creature made from two realms. Every person is made in God's image and loved for it.<Cite source="mishnah-kulp" locator="Avot 3:14" /> Humans stand, speak and understand like angels, yet eat, procreate and die like animals.<Cite source="midrash-rabbah-sefaria" locator="Bereshit Rabbah 8:11" /> They are to love God "with all your heart", that is, with both the good and the evil impulse.<Cite source="mishnah-kulp" locator="Berakhot 9:5" /> The evil inclination belongs to creation's "very good": without it no one would build a house, marry, have children or trade.<Cite source="midrash-rabbah-sefaria" locator="Bereshit Rabbah 9:7" /> In this aggadic strand, greatness and wickedness spring largely from one created energy, governed well or badly. Maimonides instead traces human merits to the intellect and human vices to matter.<Cite source="maimonides-guide-friedlander" locator="III.8" /> Humans are free: "everything is foreseen yet freedom of choice is granted".<Cite source="mishnah-kulp" locator="Avot 3:15" /> Wrongdoing grows by choice and habit, and it can be undone. God gave Torah as the "antidote" to the evil inclination,<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Kiddushin 30b" /> and opened the way of *teshuvah*, return, by which the penitent is again "beloved and desirable" before God.<Cite source="maimonides-mishneh-torah-touger" locator="Teshuvah 7:4" />

## What this explains well

This account explains why the drives that build homes, families, markets and cities are the same drives behind envy, lust and domination. It also explains why removing those drives would not leave a saint but a barren world. In one Talmudic story, imprisoning the inclination leaves not a single fresh egg in the land.<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Yoma 69b" /> It expects that "anyone who is greater than another, his evil inclination is greater".<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Sukkah 52a" /> It describes knowing wrongdoing precisely. "Our will is to perform Your will", yet the "yeast in the dough" resists.<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Berakhot 17a" /> It also makes moral change concrete. Repentance is complete when a person meets the same temptation, with the same strength and desire, and refrains.<Cite source="maimonides-mishneh-torah-touger" locator="Teshuvah 2:1" />

## Christian response

Christianity shares much here: one good Creator, the image of God in every person, the Hebrew Scriptures, real responsibility, covenant, and a God who forgives those who return to him. The disagreement concerns how deep the heart's problem goes, and how God remedies it.

The first point concerns revelatory and redemptive adequacy, argued from the shared Tanakh. Scripture calls the heart "deceitful above all things".<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Jeremiah 17:9" /> Moses says that "to this day" the LORD has not given Israel "a heart to understand",<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Deuteronomy 29:4" /> and promises that God will circumcise its heart.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Deuteronomy 30:6" /> Nachmanides reads that promise as messianic. He links it to Jeremiah's new covenant and Ezekiel's "new heart", and holds that until then a heart evil from youth needs instruction.<Cite source="ramban-torah-chavel" locator="on Deuteronomy 30:6" /> The Christian argues that this concedes the diagnosis. Torah, *teshuvah* and God's present help govern the heart.<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Sukkah 52b" /> On the Tanakh's own terms, its cure is God's remaking of the heart. In Calvin's words, the law by itself "neither changes nor amends that depravity of heart".<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="II.11.7" /> The question is when, and through whom, God keeps it.

The second point is a Christian theological disagreement about atonement. The Mishnah teaches that repentance, Yom Kippur and death atone,<Cite source="mishnah-kulp" locator="Yoma 8:8" /> and that God himself purifies Israel.<Cite source="mishnah-kulp" locator="Yoma 8:9" /> Reformed theology holds that believing Israel was truly forgiven.<Cite source="wcf" locator="7.5" /> But Jeremiah joins the new heart to the forgiving of iniquity,<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Jeremiah 31:33–34" /> and Isaiah speaks of a servant whose soul makes "an offering for guilt".<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Isaiah 53:10" /> Rashi reads that servant as Israel.<Cite source="rashi-tanakh-judaica-press" locator="on Isaiah 53:3" /> The Christian claim that these promises meet in Christ's sacrifice<Cite source="wcf" locator="8.5" /> rests on the New Testament's witness, not on the Tanakh alone.

## Pressure questions

- Nachmanides places the "circumcision of the heart", when good becomes natural, in the messianic future. Until then, is the heart's condition something Torah and *teshuvah* are meant to heal, or to govern well? What would count as that promise beginning to be fulfilled?
- Within the tradition's own sources, the evil inclination belongs to creation's "very good", yet "even its Creator calls it evil", and God will one day slaughter it. Is it best understood as a created good to be ruled, or as a disorder to be removed?
- Leviticus ties atonement to a life given on the altar. On a point where Jews and Christians read the same texts differently: since the Temple's destruction, how should that link be understood? Has it been replaced, fulfilled in another form, or does it await restoration?

## Deep dive

#### The yetzer: one energy, two outcomes

The rabbinic tradition's most characteristic contribution to this question is its account of the *yetzer ha-ra*, the "evil inclination". Scripture says the devisings of the human heart are "evil from youth".<Cite source="tanakh-jps-gender-sensitive" locator="Genesis 8:21" /> The Talmud adds that the inclination dominates from birth<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Sanhedrin 91b" /> and renews itself daily.<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Kiddushin 30b" />

Yet the midrash refuses to treat the inclination as simply bad. It is part of creation's "very good", the source of building, marriage and commerce.<Cite source="midrash-rabbah-sefaria" locator="Bereshit Rabbah 9:7" /> A midrash also reads the verb "formed" (*va-yitzer*) as a sign of the two inclinations.<Cite source="midrash-rabbah-sefaria" locator="Bereshit Rabbah 14:4" /> The Talmud debates that derivation and prefers to hear in it the human predicament: "woe unto me from my Creator and woe unto me from my inclination".<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Berakhot 61a" />

When the angels debate whether humans should be created at all, Kindness and Righteousness argue in favour. Truth and Peace argue against, since man is "full of lies" and "full of discord". God creates him regardless.<Cite source="midrash-rabbah-sefaria" locator="Bereshit Rabbah 8:5" /> The ideal is mastery, not elimination: "Who is mighty? He who subdues his inclination."<Cite source="mishnah-kulp" locator="Avot 4:1" />

Left unmastered, the inclination begins as a passing traveller, becomes a guest, and finally becomes the master of the house.<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Sukkah 52b" /> The Talmud names two forces that keep a will set on God's will from acting on it: the inner "yeast in the dough" and outward subjugation to empires.<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Berakhot 17a" />

#### Eden and Adam's legacy

Rabbinic readings of Genesis 3 vary. One midrash has God create humans from both the upper and lower realms so that "if he sins he will die".<Cite source="midrash-rabbah-sefaria" locator="Bereshit Rabbah 8:11" /> The Talmud concludes that some people died only "due to Adam's sin with the serpent", though they themselves were without sin, so there is "death without sin".<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Shabbat 55b" /> On this view Adam's legacy is mortality, and each person answers for his own wrongdoing.<Cite source="tanakh-jps-gender-sensitive" locator="Ezekiel 18:20" /> One aggadah speaks of a moral contamination from the serpent that ceased for Israel at Sinai. The same passage records a dissenting view.<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Shabbat 146a" />

Medieval readers diverge sharply. In his code, Maimonides reads "knowing good and evil" as the human power to choose freely.<Cite source="maimonides-mishneh-torah-touger" locator="Teshuvah 5:1" /> In the *Guide*, he argues that Adam first knew true and false by intellect. After following appetite and imagination, Adam became absorbed in the lesser register of the proper and the improper.<Cite source="maimonides-guide-friedlander" locator="I.2" /> Nachmanides holds instead that humans originally acted "naturally", like the heavens, and that the fruit gave rise to "will and desire". Choice is "a godlike attribute", but for humans it is a burden.<Cite source="ramban-torah-chavel" locator="on Genesis 2:9" /> On this reading, the same endowment explains both human grandeur and human danger.

#### The philosophers: wisdom, matter and choice

Saadia Gaon argued that God's gift of wisdom is what lifts humans above all creatures. That wisdom lets them remember the past, farm, build, govern kingdoms and chart the stars. It makes the human being the "axis" of the world, and therefore the one who is commanded, warned and judged.<Cite source="saadia-emunot-ibn-tibbon" locator="IV.1" /> He denied that God compels either obedience or rebellion.<Cite source="saadia-emunot-ibn-tibbon" locator="IV.4" /> He located much disorder in letting one love tyrannize the others, when God implanted many loves to be weighed together.<Cite source="saadia-emunot-ibn-tibbon" locator="X.1" />

Maimonides identified the divine image with the intellect, not with bodily shape.<Cite source="maimonides-mishneh-torah-touger" locator="Yesodei ha-Torah 4:8" /> He held that "all corruption, destruction, or defect comes from matter", while human merits come from form.<Cite source="maimonides-guide-friedlander" locator="III.8" /> He observed that the largest class of evils is the one people inflict on themselves.<Cite source="maimonides-guide-friedlander" locator="III.12" /> Temperament, he held, may make a virtue easier or harder, but never necessary.<Cite source="maimonides-eight-chapters" locator="ch. 8" /> God willed human freedom just as He willed the natures of the elements.<Cite source="maimonides-mishneh-torah-touger" locator="Teshuvah 5:4" />

Maimonides also reconciled the rabbinic praise of the one who desires wrong yet refrains with the philosophers' praise of the one who does not desire it at all. In his view, the rabbinic dictum concerns ritual prohibitions. A noble soul should not even crave bloodshed or theft.<Cite source="maimonides-eight-chapters" locator="ch. 6" />

#### Hasidic psychology: the intermediate person

Habad Hasidism gave this struggle an influential modern form. Rabbi Shneur Zalman of Liadi's *Tanya* teaches that within every Jew there are two souls, an animal soul and a divine soul.<Cite source="tanya-kehot" locator="Likkutei Amarim 1" /> Its ideal, the *beinoni* or "intermediate" person, never sins in deed, speech or thought. Yet after prayer "he begins to feel a desire for the lusts of the world". He restrains it because, by created nature, "the brain rules over the heart".<Cite source="tanya-kehot" locator="Likkutei Amarim 12" /> The Musar tradition develops related themes, which are not treated here.

#### Majesty and covenant

Joseph B. Soloveitchik reads the two creation narratives as "a real contradiction in the nature of man".<Cite source="soloveitchik-lonely-man-of-faith" locator="p. 10" /> The majestic Adam I is commanded to subdue nature. For him, "there is no dignity without responsibility".<Cite source="soloveitchik-lonely-man-of-faith" locator="pp. 13–14" /> The covenantal Adam II seeks the God who addresses him.<Cite source="soloveitchik-lonely-man-of-faith" locator="p. 17" /> For Soloveitchik, the Halakhah treats the two as complementary within a single world. That world can plunge into "ugliness and hatefulness" or be roused to "redeeming activity".<Cite source="soloveitchik-lonely-man-of-faith" locator="p. 51" />

#### Dignity: universal and particular

Ben Azzai called "This is the book of the descendants of Adam" the central principle of the Torah. Rabbi Tanhuma warned that whoever demeans another should know whose likeness he disgraces.<Cite source="midrash-rabbah-sefaria" locator="Bereshit Rabbah 24:7" /> The texts also layer covenantal particularity onto this universal worth. Avot moves from "beloved is man" to "beloved are Israel".<Cite source="mishnah-kulp" locator="Avot 3:14" /> The printed Mishnah reads that one who destroys "a single soul from Israel" destroys a world.<Cite source="mishnah-kulp" locator="Sanhedrin 4:5" /> The Mishnah as given in the Jerusalem Talmud reads simply "a single life".<Cite source="yerushalmi-guggenheimer" locator="Sanhedrin 4:9" /> Judah Halevi holds that the divine influence rests on Israel as a chosen people in a way it does not on others, a view contested within Jewish thought.<Cite source="halevi-kuzari-hirschfeld" locator="I.95" />

#### Remedy: Torah, teshuvah and hope

The rabbinic remedy has already been given: "I created an evil inclination … and I created Torah as its antidote".<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Kiddushin 30b" /> God also helps the one who struggles.<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Sukkah 52b" /> *Teshuvah* means abandoning the sin, regretting it, resolving before God never to repeat it, and confessing it.<Cite source="maimonides-mishneh-torah-touger" locator="Teshuvah 2:2" /> In one Talmudic view, repentance from love turns intentional sins into merits.<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Yoma 86b" /> Maimonides also warns that persistent, wilful wickedness can lead to the withholding of repentance itself.<Cite source="maimonides-mishneh-torah-touger" locator="Teshuvah 6:3" />

In the end, God will slaughter the evil inclination. The righteous will weep that they overcame a mountain, and the wicked that they failed before a hair.<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Sukkah 52a" /> Nachmanides envisions a messianic "circumcision of the heart", when doing good will again be natural, as it was before Adam's sin.<Cite source="ramban-torah-chavel" locator="on Deuteronomy 30:6" />

Reform Judaism's 1999 principles affirm that "every human being is created in the image of God". They speak of the mitzvot "that address us" and of "transgression and repentance".<Cite source="ccar-principles-1999" locator="Statement of Principles" /> The anthropology is broadly shared across the modern streams. What differs is the authority of the means.

#### A Christian note: shared text, rabbinic reading, Christian claim

Three layers should be kept apart. The Hebrew Bible is shared ground. Its verdict on the heart (Gen 6:5; Jer 17:9) and its promise of a new heart (Deut 30:6; Ezek 36:26) belong to Jews and Christians alike. Rabbinic Judaism developed its own reading of those texts after the Second Temple: Torah study, *teshuvah* and Yom Kippur in the present, and the circumcised heart in the messianic age. The specifically Christian claim is that the new covenant has already begun in Jesus. It rests on the New Testament's testimony to his death and resurrection. Reformed theology holds that the covenant of grace is "one and the same" across both testaments.<Cite source="wcf" locator="7.6" /> On that view the Christian is not claiming that Israel's faith was graceless. The claim is that its promises have been kept. Note that Jeremiah 31:31–34 in English Bibles is 31:30–33 in Jewish editions.
