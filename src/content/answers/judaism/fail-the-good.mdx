## The view

The Torah presents sin as a threat to be mastered, and commands Israel to choose life.<Cite source="tanakh-jps-gender-sensitive" locator="Genesis 4:7; Deuteronomy 30:11–19" /> Rabbinic texts name the inner conflict. A prayer in Berakhot affirms the desire to do God's will while identifying the evil inclination, alongside foreign domination, as an obstruction.<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Berakhot 17a" /> Sukkah portrays that inclination growing from a visitor into the master of the house: repeated indulgence strengthens its hold.<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Sukkah 52a–b" /> Moral knowledge therefore needs formation and resistance, not simply more information.

## What this explains well

It takes sincere intention and persistent temptation seriously without making them identical. Maimonides distinguishes the person who desires wrong yet restrains himself from the person whose desires are themselves well ordered.<Cite source="maimonides-eight-chapters" locator="ch. 6" /> The distinction makes sense of doing the good with effort as well as failing to do it.

## Christian response

Both accounts recognize disordered desire, responsible action, and dependence on God. Rabbinic teaching explicitly says God's help is needed to overcome the inclination; Torah is its antidote.<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Kiddushin 30b; Sukkah 52b" /> So the question is not whether Judaism admits divine assistance but where change begins. Maimonides affirms the person's freedom to turn toward righteousness.<Cite source="maimonides-mishneh-torah-touger" locator="Teshuvah 5:1–4" /> Reformed theology holds that the first turn is itself God's work: he makes alive those who were dead in their trespasses, and the Westminster Confession calls the person "altogether passive" at the beginning of that renewal.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Ephesians 2:1–5" /><Cite source="wcf" locator="10.2" /> These are different theological accounts of how change begins; moral failure, which both describe, does not by itself decide between them.

## Deep dive

#### Restraining desire and changing character

The rabbinic model does not make the disappearance of temptation the measure of every good act. Avot identifies the mighty person as one who masters his inclination.<Cite source="mishnah-kulp" locator="Avot 4:1" /> That leaves space for obedience which costs a struggle, not only obedience which has become easy.

Maimonides complicates the comparison between self-restraint and virtue. In *Eight Chapters* 6 he distinguishes commandments whose wrongness is intelligible to reason from specifically revealed prohibitions. For the former, desiring the wrong itself indicates defective character; for the latter, refraining because God commands it can be commendable even while desire remains.<Cite source="maimonides-eight-chapters" locator="ch. 6" /> For Maimonides, then, struggle is not always superior to settled virtue, and not every remaining desire is equally blameworthy.

His account also distinguishes temperament from necessity: predispositions can make a course easier without compelling it.<Cite source="maimonides-eight-chapters" locator="ch. 8" /> Jewish moral discipline is therefore not an attempt to earn salvation without God's help: it joins effort to divine help and a realistic account of temperament. The Christian question about renewal concerns where that change begins.
