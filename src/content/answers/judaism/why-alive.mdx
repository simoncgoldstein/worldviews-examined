## The view

The Tanakh presents human life as given by God and for God. God forms and makes those called by his name for his glory.<Cite source="tanakh-jps-gender-sensitive" locator="Isaiah 43:7" /> He set the first human in the garden to till and tend it.<Cite source="tanakh-jps-gender-sensitive" locator="Genesis 2:15" />

The rabbinic tradition describes that purpose as service. Simeon the Righteous taught that "the world stands upon three things: the Torah, the Temple service, and the practice of acts of piety."<Cite source="mishnah-kulp" locator="Avot 1:2" /> Rabbi Yose extended service to everything: "let all your actions be for [the sake of] the name of heaven."<Cite source="mishnah-kulp" locator="Avot 2:12" /> Rabbi Simeon ben Elazar contrasted animals, which were created to serve him, with himself: "I was created to serve my master."<Cite source="mishnah-kulp" locator="Kiddushin 4:14" /> The service is not only ritual. The Talmud reads "walk after the Lord your God" as imitating God's attributes: as God clothed the naked, visited the sick, comforted mourners and buried the dead, so should we.<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Sotah 14a" />

The philosophers gave the purpose a theoretical form. Saadia Gaon argued that the human being is the purpose of creation, its axis, because humans alone receive wisdom and commandments.<Cite source="saadia-emunot-ibn-tibbon" locator="IV.1" /> Maimonides directs every activity, eating, sleeping and work included, toward knowing God,<Cite source="maimonides-mishneh-torah-touger" locator="De'ot 3:2–3" /> and calls intellectual perfection the true perfection of man, completed by imitating God's loving-kindness, judgment and righteousness.<Cite source="maimonides-guide-friedlander" locator="III.54" />

## What this explains well

The account gives purpose to the whole of ordinary life, not only to religious moments. It joins love of God to concrete care for others. It explains why purpose comes as command as well as gift. And it combines universal and particular. All humans are made in God's image and owe him service, while Israel's Torah gives that service a specific covenantal form.

## Christian response

The Christian shares almost all of this. Paul's instruction to do everything for God's glory echoes Rabbi Yose.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="1 Corinthians 10:31" /> Imitating God's mercy is also a Christian calling. The difference concerns how the purpose is fulfilled. Christians hold that the service for which humans were made has been perfectly rendered by one person, Jesus, and that others are restored to that purpose through him rather than by their own faithful practice alone. That is a revelatory disagreement about Jesus, not a criticism of the Jewish account's coherence. Jewish teaching also has its own account of divine help and return, treated in <QuestionLink question="self-salvation" worldview="judaism">the salvation anchor</QuestionLink>.

## Pressure questions

- Does Maimonides' ranking of intellectual perfection fit the rabbinic emphasis on deeds of kindness?
- What purpose does Torah assign to the non-Jewish majority of humanity?

## Deep dive

#### Service of heaven in ordinary life

The rabbinic phrase "for the sake of heaven" turns purpose into a way of doing things rather than a separate activity. Avot sets this beside Torah study and care for another's property.<Cite source="mishnah-kulp" locator="Avot 2:12" /> Rabbi Simeon ben Elazar's saying comes in a discussion of trades and livelihood; the point is that a human life is service even when it is spent earning a living.<Cite source="mishnah-kulp" locator="Kiddushin 4:14" /> The imitation of God's attributes in Sotah makes that service social, since God's own acts of clothing, visiting and burying become the model for human ones.<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Sotah 14a" />

#### Maimonides' ranking

In the closing chapter of the *Guide*, Maimonides ranks four perfections: possessions, bodily health, moral virtue and intellectual perfection. Only the last belongs to a person truly, and it consists in knowing God as far as humans can. He ends, though, by saying that the one who has attained it will seek to imitate God's loving-kindness, judgment and righteousness on earth.<Cite source="maimonides-guide-friedlander" locator="III.54" /> The ranking is Maimonides', not a rabbinic consensus. It is one major strand alongside more praxis-centred accounts.

#### Universal and covenantal purpose

The Genesis mandate to fill and tend the earth addresses all humanity, and the image belongs to every person. Israel's purpose is given a covenantal shape in Torah and commandments. Classical sources describe the rest of humanity as bound by more general obligations, often called the Noahide laws. The final good toward which this service leads, the World to Come, is treated in <QuestionLink question="final-end" worldview="judaism">the final-end question</QuestionLink>, and the creature who serves is described in <QuestionLink question="what-is-man" worldview="judaism">the human-nature anchor</QuestionLink>.
