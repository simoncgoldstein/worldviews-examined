## The view

The Jewish sources affirm more than a person's continued influence among the living. Daniel depicts sleepers in the dust awakening to different outcomes.<Cite source="tanakh-jps-gender-sensitive" locator="Daniel 12:2" /> Rabbinic discussion likewise treats resurrection and accountability seriously: Sanhedrin's parable of the blind and lame guards explains why body and soul can be judged together rather than each escaping blame by accusing the other.<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Sanhedrin 91b" /> Avot warns of death and a reckoning before the divine King.<Cite source="mishnah-kulp" locator="Avot 4:22" />

Resurrection is a doctrine of prayer and creed, not only of commentary. The Mishnah excludes from the World to Come anyone who says the resurrection of the dead is not taught in the Torah, and it already names the second blessing of the daily Amidah by that theme.<Cite source="mishnah-kulp" locator="Sanhedrin 10:1; Berakhot 5:2" /> In that blessing the worshipper praises God, who "revives the dead" and "keeps faith with those who sleep in the dust".<Cite source="siddur-metsudah-hebrew" locator="weekday Amidah, second blessing" note="Hebrew; our translation" /> Maimonides calls the resurrection "one of the cardinal doctrines of the Law of Moses" and makes it the last of his thirteen principles.<Cite source="maimonides-helek-abelson" locator="pp. 42, 56" />

The interpreters then divide over what the resurrection is for. Maimonides held that the soul really returns to the body, and that denying this is heresy. Yet the people raised will eat, marry and eventually die again; the life with no death after it is the World to Come, where the righteous exist without bodies and delight in knowing God.<Cite source="maimonides-treatise-resurrection-hebrew" locator="reply to Yemen; the life of those raised" note="Hebrew; our paraphrase" /><Cite source="maimonides-mishneh-torah-touger" locator="Teshuvah 8:1–2" /> Maimonides does not deny bodily resurrection: he denies that it is the final state.

Saadia Gaon and Nachmanides give the resurrection a permanent place. Saadia argues that the God who created the world from nothing can certainly restore the dead, and that the prophets' promises must be read as they stand: the one who wounded is the one who heals, and the one raised will know that he is the person who lived and died.<Cite source="saadia-emunot-ibn-tibbon" locator="VII.1, VII.4, VII.6" note="Hebrew; our paraphrase" /> Reward and punishment belong to body and soul together, he concludes, because they acted as one.<Cite source="saadia-emunot-ibn-tibbon" locator="IX.5" note="Hebrew; our paraphrase" /> Nachmanides holds that souls receive their reward or punishment immediately after death. Later, after the messianic age, come the judgment and the resurrection, which he calls the reward that includes body and soul and the great hope of all who trust in God. The people raised then live forever.<Cite source="ramban-shaar-ha-gemul-hebrew" locator="¶¶64, 78, 87" note="Hebrew; our paraphrase" /> All three affirm the resurrection; what they dispute is whether the body shares in final blessedness, which is explained under <QuestionLink question="final-end" worldview="judaism">final destiny</QuestionLink>.

## What this explains well

The body-and-soul parable makes responsibility concern the acting person rather than a convenient division of agents, and Saadia draws the conclusion that the whole person is recompensed. Daily prayer keeps the hope concrete: the same blessing that praises God for sustaining the living and healing the sick praises him for keeping faith with the dead. Maimonides' account, for its part, keeps the final good centred on knowing God rather than on restored comforts. Avot's emphasis on repentance and good deeds in this world gives present action weight without making speculation about unseen states the whole religious task.<Cite source="mishnah-kulp" locator="Avot 4:17" />

## Christian response

The Christian shares the bodily resurrection and the final judgment, and the Reformed sequence is close to Nachmanides': the souls of the righteous are received at death, and at the last day the same bodies are raised and reunited with them forever.<Cite source="wcf" locator="32.1–3" /> Against Maimonides the Reformed account holds that final blessedness is embodied; against Saadia and Nachmanides there is no such difference to press. The real disagreement lies elsewhere. The Christian claims that the resurrection has already begun: Christ was raised as the firstfruits, his people's resurrection follows his, and he is the judge at whose voice the dead will come out.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="John 5:28–29; 1 Corinthians 15:20–23" /> That claim stands or falls with the historical case weighed in <QuestionLink question="jesus" worldview="christianity">who Jesus is</QuestionLink>, and the Jewish denial that Jesus is the Messiah is set out in <QuestionLink question="jesus" worldview="judaism">the Jewish answer</QuestionLink>. The dispute is historical and revelatory, not a contest between bodily and bodiless hope.

## Pressure questions

- If body and soul are judged together because they acted together, what justifies Maimonides' view that the body has no share in the final reward?
- What preserves the accountability of the person when body and soul are distinguished?

## Deep dive

#### Resurrection and the agent who acted

Sanhedrin's guards have different capacities: one cannot move to the fruit and the other cannot see it, yet together they commit the offense. The owner judges them together, answering their separate attempts to excuse themselves.<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Sanhedrin 91b" /> Applied to body and soul, the illustration rejects the strategy of assigning responsibility to a partner one can then disown. It is an argument about accountability within resurrection, not a scientific account of the soul's separation or return.

#### Saadia's case for the resurrection

Saadia tests the belief against nature, reason, Scripture and tradition. Those who believe that God created from nothing and worked the wonders of the exodus cannot call resurrection impossible, since restoring is easier than creating; he answers the objection that a body's matter is later absorbed into other bodies by arguing that nothing created is annihilated and that God preserves each body's parts.<Cite source="saadia-emunot-ibn-tibbon" locator="VII.1" note="Hebrew; our paraphrase" /> Scripture is read in its plain sense unless the senses, reason, another text or tradition compel otherwise; turning the resurrection prophecies into metaphors for national revival would license allegorizing the commandments and the creation story too.<Cite source="saadia-emunot-ibn-tibbon" locator="VII.2, VII.5" note="Hebrew; our paraphrase" />

Saadia places this resurrection at the redemption, in this world: Ezekiel's opened graves lead to the land of Israel. Those raised are the righteous and the penitent of Israel; they will not die again but will pass from the days of the Messiah into the World to Come.<Cite source="saadia-emunot-ibn-tibbon" locator="VII.4, VII.8" note="Hebrew; our paraphrase" /> Saadia wrote in Arabic, and this treatise survives in two versions; the text used here is Judah ibn Tibbon's Hebrew translation.

#### Maimonides' own distinction

Maimonides wrote his *Treatise on Resurrection* because readers in Damascus and Yemen were citing his account of the bodiless World to Come to deny the resurrection. He answered that the resurrection is a pillar of the Torah, the return of the soul to the body, which may not be explained away; Daniel 12:2 admits no other reading.<Cite source="maimonides-treatise-resurrection-hebrew" locator="reply to Yemen; the life of those raised" note="Hebrew; our paraphrase" /> In the commentary on the Mishnah he had already said that resurrection is "the reward of the righteous only", and his thirteenth principle reads simply: "The resurrection of the dead. We have already explained this."<Cite source="maimonides-helek-abelson" locator="pp. 42, 56" /> The *Ani ma'amin* printed in many prayer books ("I believe with perfect faith that the dead will be revived when the Creator so wills") is a separate liturgical formula, not his wording.<Cite source="siddur-metsudah-hebrew" locator="Thirteen Principles, no. 13" note="Hebrew; our translation" />

#### Modern movements

The classical texts do not settle how modern movements use the doctrine. The Reform Pittsburgh Platform of 1885 rejected bodily resurrection in favour of the soul's immortality.<Cite source="ccar-pittsburgh-platform-1885" locator="art. 7" /> The Conservative statement *Emet Ve-Emunah* affirms both resurrection and the soul's survival, and records that some members hold them as literal truths while others understand them figuratively.<Cite source="emet-ve-emunah-1988" locator="pp. 29–30" />
