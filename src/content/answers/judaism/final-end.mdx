## The view

Jewish final hope concerns more than continued existence. Rabbinic Avot values repentance and good deeds in this world while also speaking of the surpassing satisfaction of the World to Come.<Cite source="mishnah-kulp" locator="Avot 4:17" /> The good pursued here and the reward beyond this life are related, but they are not interchangeable.

Maimonides calls the life of the World to Come the final good without death or evil. In his interpretation, its delight is incorporeal knowledge of God, rather than eating, drinking or the extension of ordinary bodily pleasures.<Cite source="maimonides-mishneh-torah-touger" locator="Teshuvah 8:1–2" /> He affirms the resurrection of the dead, but those raised will die again; only the bodiless life of the soul has no end (see <QuestionLink question="after-death" worldview="judaism">after death</QuestionLink>).

Saadia Gaon and Nachmanides describe the same final good with the body included. For Saadia, those rewarded are human beings, bodies and souls, so God will create a new heaven and earth as their dwelling. There the righteous need no food, sustained instead by a light God creates, as Moses lived forty days on the mountain without eating.<Cite source="saadia-emunot-ibn-tibbon" locator="IX.5–6" note="Hebrew; our paraphrase" /> For Nachmanides, the World to Come is the world of the resurrection, which follows the messianic age and lasts forever. There the body is sustained like the soul, by the radiance of the divine presence, and the soul cleaves to the knowledge of God more fully even than it did in Gan Eden.<Cite source="ramban-shaar-ha-gemul-hebrew" locator="¶¶75, 78" note="Hebrew; our paraphrase" /> He reads the rabbinic saying that the World to Come has no eating or drinking, only the righteous enjoying the radiance of the Shekhinah, as a description of raised bodies, not of souls alone.<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Berakhot 17a" /><Cite source="ramban-shaar-ha-gemul-hebrew" locator="¶75" note="Hebrew; our paraphrase" />

The three therefore agree that the final good is nearness to God and knowledge of him, not restored pleasures or political power. They disagree about whether the body has a part in it. Nachmanides himself praises Maimonides' account of the soul's delight in knowing God; what he rejects is the claim that the raised body falls away.<Cite source="ramban-shaar-ha-gemul-hebrew" locator="¶¶80, 87" note="Hebrew; our paraphrase" />

All three also distinguish the final good from the messianic era. Maimonides says that peace and freedom from oppression allow fuller attention to Torah, wisdom and knowledge of God, and that the final reward exceeds the political and material conditions that facilitate it.<Cite source="maimonides-mishneh-torah-touger" locator="Teshuvah 9:2; Kings 12:1–5" /> Another strand stresses God's transformation of the heart: Nachmanides reads Deuteronomy's circumcision of the heart in connection with a future restoration in which evil desire is no longer natural.<Cite source="ramban-torah-chavel" locator="Deuteronomy 30:6" /> Divine aid and moral renewal belong within final hope; it is not a hope of self-earned righteousness.

## What this explains well

The distinction between peace and the final good keeps material security from exhausting the religious aim. Knowledge of God and a changed heart address the person and community, not only external circumstances, and the embodied account of Saadia and Nachmanides keeps the whole person who acted within the promise. Maimonides also makes the desired peace serve Torah and wisdom rather than conquest.<Cite source="maimonides-mishneh-torah-touger" locator="Kings 12:4–5" /> Present repentance retains urgency even while fulfillment is hoped for.

## Christian response

The Christian shares the hope for knowledge of God, moral healing and peace, and with Saadia and Nachmanides holds that final blessedness is embodied: the righteous are raised and glorified in a renewed creation.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 8:29–30; Revelation 21:1–4" /> Against Maimonides' incorporeal reward this is a real difference; against the resurrection-centred Jewish account it is not. The decisive question is who brings the fulfillment and on what ground. The Christian claims that the promised completion has a name, Jesus the Messiah, already raised as the firstfruits, through whom the heart is renewed and the final good secured.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="1 Corinthians 15:20–23" /> The Jewish tradition denies that he is the Messiah (see <QuestionLink question="jesus" worldview="judaism">the Jewish answer on Jesus</QuestionLink>). The disagreement is revelatory and historical. Neither side reduces the other's hope to worldly prosperity.

## Pressure questions

- If the final good is knowing God, what does the raised body add on Nachmanides' account, and what is lost if it falls away on Maimonides'?
- What completes the relation between knowing God and having the heart transformed?

## Deep dive

#### The goal beyond a better political condition

Maimonides explicitly says that prophets and sages did not seek the messianic era in order to dominate other peoples or indulge in endless festivities. They sought freedom to pursue Torah and wisdom without obstruction.<Cite source="maimonides-mishneh-torah-touger" locator="Kings 12:4–5" /> The account has a communal setting, but it does not reduce the religious good to political power. Teshuvah makes the hierarchy equally explicit: the messianic era remains life in this world, whereas the final reward is the World to Come.<Cite source="maimonides-mishneh-torah-touger" locator="Teshuvah 9:2" />

#### Three sequences, one term

The interpreters do not use "World to Come" in exactly the same sense. Nachmanides distinguishes two rewards. The first is Gan Eden, which the soul enters at death. The second is the World to Come, which he finds in the Talmud's own reasoning: whoever denied the resurrection loses a share in it, "measure for measure", so the World to Come must be the world of the resurrection.<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Sanhedrin 90a" /><Cite source="ramban-shaar-ha-gemul-hebrew" locator="¶¶64, 69, 73" note="Hebrew; our paraphrase" /> Saadia places the resurrection at the redemption, in this world, and has those raised pass without dying again into the World to Come.<Cite source="saadia-emunot-ibn-tibbon" locator="VII.8" note="Hebrew; our paraphrase" /> For Maimonides the resurrection belongs to a long but finite life, and the World to Come is the soul's lasting state. Nachmanides notes that some Spanish authors who call the world of souls the World to Come differ from him partly in names; with Maimonides the difference is substantive, because Maimonides limits the life of those raised.<Cite source="ramban-shaar-ha-gemul-hebrew" locator="¶¶84, 87" note="Hebrew; our paraphrase" />

#### Knowledge, holiness and the body

In Maimonides' explanation, the righteous delight in what they comprehend of God. The bodily images of sitting with crowns are interpreted metaphorically, and the final good does not depend on the functions of a body.<Cite source="maimonides-mishneh-torah-touger" locator="Teshuvah 8:2" /> Nachmanides takes up the objection that a body without eating or reproduction would exist for nothing: the Creator, he answers, wills to keep what he formed, and the body can be sustained as the soul is, by its union with the knowledge of God, as Moses' face shone on the mountain.<Cite source="ramban-shaar-ha-gemul-hebrew" locator="¶77" note="Hebrew; our paraphrase" /> Saadia similarly speaks of the reward as light and the punishment as fire, two aspects of one created reality that shines on the righteous and burns the wicked.<Cite source="saadia-emunot-ibn-tibbon" locator="IX.5" note="Hebrew; our paraphrase" />

Whether Christ is the promised completion is a question the Christian has to argue; that Jewish interpreters differ settles nothing either way.
