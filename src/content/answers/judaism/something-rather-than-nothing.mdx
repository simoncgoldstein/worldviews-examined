## The view

The Torah opens with God creating heaven and earth by his word: "God said, 'Let there be light,' and there was light."<Cite source="tanakh-jps-gender-sensitive" locator="Genesis 1:1–3" /> The psalmist sums it up: God "commanded that they be created."<Cite source="tanakh-jps-gender-sensitive" locator="Psalms 148:5" /> The world exists because God willed it, not because it had to.

Did God create from nothing, or shape something already there? The opening verse alone does not settle it. Rashi read its grammar as "at the beginning of God's creating," <Cite source="rashi-tanakh-judaica-press" locator="on Genesis 1:1" /> and the JPS translation renders it "When God began to create."<Cite source="tanakh-jps-gender-sensitive" locator="Genesis 1:1" /> The wider tradition supplies the answer. When a philosopher told Rabban Gamliel that God had found good materials ready to hand, the rabbi replied that Scripture speaks of each of them as created.<Cite source="midrash-rabbah-sefaria" locator="Bereshit Rabbah 1:9" /> Nachmanides calls creation the root of faith: God brought all things forth "from absolute non-existence," beginning with a formless first matter from which everything else was shaped.<Cite source="ramban-torah-chavel" locator="on Genesis 1:1" />

The medieval philosophers argued the point. Saadia offered proofs that the world had a beginning, and argued that if God had made it from something, that something would be a second eternal reality beside him.<Cite source="saadia-emunot-ibn-tibbon" locator="I.1–2" /> Maimonides held that creation from nothing, with time itself created, is the teaching of Moses. He judged that Aristotle had not demonstrated the world's eternity, so there was no reason to depart from the plain sense of Scripture.<Cite source="maimonides-guide-friedlander" locator="II.13, II.25" />

## What this explains well

The view makes the world wholly dependent on God without making it part of him, real yet unnecessary. Denying an eternal matter leaves nothing beside God as a second ultimate. And, as Maimonides saw, a created world leaves room for God to act within it, which an eternal and necessary order would not.

## Christian response

Here the Christian lane largely agrees, reading the same Scriptures to the same conclusion: creation by God's will and word, from nothing, with time. The Christian adds that God created through his Word, whom the New Testament identifies as the Son.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="John 1:1–3" /> That is a disagreement about God's inner life, grounded in a claim of revelation, and it is treated elsewhere.

The two lanes also share a debate: is creation proved by argument, as Saadia held, or known by prophetic tradition, as Halevi held?

## Pressure questions

- If the arguments for creation and for an eternal world are evenly balanced, as Halevi judged, what makes prophetic tradition decisive?
- If Maimonides could have accepted an eternal matter had it been proved, is creation from nothing a fixed doctrine or the best available reading?

## Deep dive

#### Restraint and proof

The Mishnah warns that whoever speculates about "what is above, what is beneath, what came before, and what came after" would have been better off not coming into the world.<Cite source="mishnah-kulp" locator="Chagigah 2:1" /> The medieval philosophers read this as a limit on public exposition, not a ban on argument.

#### Saadia's proofs

Saadia opens *Beliefs and Opinions* with four arguments that the world had a beginning: from its finitude, from its being composed of parts, from the accidents that inhere in it, and from time. The last runs roughly as follows (our paraphrase of the Hebrew). If past time were infinite, it could never have been traversed to reach the present.<Cite source="saadia-emunot-ibn-tibbon" locator="I.1" /> He then argues that things cannot make themselves, and that creation from a pre-existing matter would make that matter co-eternal with God and lead to a regress.<Cite source="saadia-emunot-ibn-tibbon" locator="I.2" />

#### Maimonides on eternity

Maimonides distinguishes three views: creation from nothing (the Law of Moses); Plato's view, in which God formed an eternal matter; and Aristotle's, in which the world is eternal and necessary. He argues that Aristotle's view would abolish the possibility of miracles and so the Law itself. Plato's could in principle be reconciled with the Torah, but it has not been demonstrated, so it is not adopted.<Cite source="maimonides-guide-friedlander" locator="II.13, II.25" />

#### Halevi's concession

Halevi's rabbi in the *Kuzari* says the question of eternity and creation is obscure and "the arguments are evenly balanced." The prophetic tradition from Adam, Noah and Moses tips the scale. Even if a believer were compelled to admit an eternal matter, that would not impair his belief that this world was created at a certain epoch.<Cite source="halevi-kuzari-hirschfeld" locator="I.67" /> Halevi grounds creation in tradition rather than demonstration, and the difference matters to <QuestionLink question="ultimate-personal" worldview="judaism">how God is known</QuestionLink>.

#### Nachmanides' first matter

Nachmanides reads *bara*, "created," as the one Hebrew word for bringing something forth from nothing. God first created a very thin substance with the potential to take form, called *tohu* (the Greek *hylē*). Everything else was formed from it.<Cite source="ramban-torah-chavel" locator="on Genesis 1:1" /> This keeps creation from absolute nothing while explaining the "formless and void" of Genesis 1:2.
