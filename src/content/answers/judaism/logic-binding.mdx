## The view

Rabbinic Judaism is a tradition of argument, and the rabbis developed rules for interpreting Scripture. But there is no rabbinic doctrine of *why* logic binds. The question was taken up by medieval philosophers, whose answers are developments within the tradition rather than its official teaching.

Saadia Gaon addressed the question through God's power. God is not praised for being able to make five greater than ten without adding to the five, to fit the world through a ring's hole without changing either, or to bring back yesterday, "for all this is absurd." If an unbeliever asks whether God can do such things, the answer is that God can do everything, and what he "cannot" do is not anything at all: the absurd is nothing.<Cite source="saadia-emunot-ibn-tibbon" locator="II.13" note="Hebrew; our translation of the phrases" />

Maimonides states the principle more sharply. "That which is impossible has a permanent and constant property, which is not the result of some agent, and cannot in any way change, and consequently we do not ascribe to God the power of doing what is impossible." Only those who "have no idea of Logic" deny this.<Cite source="maimonides-guide-friedlander" locator="III.15" />

Maimonides does not say that logic exists in God's mind or flows from his nature. He says that the impossible has a fixed character that no agent produces. The Torah's picture of "a faithful God, never false" stands in the background,<Cite source="tanakh-jps-gender-sensitive" locator="Deuteronomy 32:4" /> but the philosophical claim is about the impossible itself.

## What this explains well

The account explains why logic does not bend to power, even infinite power, without making God less than omnipotent. It keeps a clear line between what is beyond our understanding and what is simply incoherent.

## Christian response

The Christian agrees that God does not do the self-contradictory and that this is no defect in his power. Reformed theology says something very close (see <QuestionLink question="logic-binding" worldview="christianity">the Christian answer</QuestionLink>). The difference lies in the grounding. Augustine places the validity of reasoning in "the reason of things" with its "origin with God."<Cite source="augustine-christian-doctrine" locator="II.32.50" /> Maimonides places the impossible in a fixed character "not the result of some agent." The Christian asks what that fixed character is, if it is neither God nor made by God. Maimonides could answer, with Saadia, that the impossible is nothing, so nothing needs grounding. This is a theological disagreement about what logical necessity rests on, not an incoherence.

## Pressure questions

- If the character of the impossible is produced by no agent, is there anything necessary besides God?
- Since imagination cannot be trusted to mark the impossible, what tells the intellect where the boundary lies?

## Deep dive

#### Intellect, not imagination

Maimonides' answer to the second question comes in his critique of the Muslim theologians of the *kalām*. They held that whatever can be imagined is possible. Maimonides objects that this lets imagination, which animals also have, decide what is necessary, possible and impossible. Some things that cannot be pictured are nonetheless proved, such as people standing upright on opposite sides of the globe. "In man exists a certain faculty which is entirely distinct from imagination, and by which the necessary, the possible, and the impossible can be distinguished from each other."<Cite source="maimonides-guide-friedlander" locator="I.73, tenth proposition" />

#### Agreement and disagreement about the impossible

Maimonides notes that philosophers disagree about whether particular things are impossible, but agree that impossible things "remain impossible, and do not depend on the action of an agent."<Cite source="maimonides-guide-friedlander" locator="III.15" /> Earlier in the same section Saadia lists the impossibility of joining existence and non-existence among the things the intellect knows without the senses, as a model for how God, whom Jeremiah calls truth, is known without being perceived.<Cite source="saadia-emunot-ibn-tibbon" locator="II.13" note="Hebrew; paraphrased" />

#### Two formulations, one view

Saadia calls the impossible "nothing"; Maimonides calls it a fixed character produced by no agent. The two are compatible. Both deny that God's power includes the absurd, and both deny that this limits God. Neither identifies logic with God, and later Jewish thinkers who do relate wisdom to God's own being, in Kabbalah for example, were not researched for this answer.
