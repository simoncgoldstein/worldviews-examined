## The view

Jewish tradition treats knowledge as a gift that God gives to the creature he made. "The fear of GOD is the beginning of knowledge,"<Cite source="tanakh-jps-gender-sensitive" locator="Proverbs 1:7" /> and "GOD grants wisdom."<Cite source="tanakh-jps-gender-sensitive" locator="Proverbs 2:6" /> That does not make ordinary knowing mysterious. The prophet expects people to use their minds: "Have you not discerned how the earth was founded?"<Cite source="tanakh-jps-gender-sensitive" locator="Isaiah 40:21" /> The senses and reason genuinely work, because the God who made the world also made the human beings who study it.

In the tenth century Saadia Gaon gave the classic account. Knowledge has three roots: what the senses perceive; what the mind knows directly, such as that truthfulness is good and lying shameful; and what must follow from these, as fire is inferred from smoke. Believers add a fourth root, reliable tradition, which is built on the other three.<Cite source="saadia-emunot-ibn-tibbon" locator="Introduction 5" note="Hebrew; paraphrased" /> Each root can mislead: people have mistaken a reflection in a mirror for a real figure, and dreams for waking events. Each also has tests for catching the error.<Cite source="saadia-emunot-ibn-tibbon" locator="Introduction 5" note="Hebrew; paraphrased" /> Knowledge is not infallible, but it can be corrected.

The fourth root matters especially in Judaism, because its central claims concern events. Israel is told not to forget "the things that you saw with your own eyes" at Horeb, and to make them known to children and grandchildren.<Cite source="tanakh-jps-gender-sensitive" locator="Deuteronomy 4:9–10" /> Judah Halevi says Israel knew these things "first from personal experience, and afterwards through uninterrupted tradition, which is equal to the former."<Cite source="halevi-kuzari-hirschfeld" locator="I.25" />

The great medieval thinkers weigh the sources differently. Saadia is confident that careful reasoning will reach the truths that prophecy gives.<Cite source="saadia-emunot-ibn-tibbon" locator="Introduction 6" note="Hebrew; paraphrased" /> Halevi distrusts religion built on speculation: the philosophers' systems are "open to many doubts," and the philosophers do not agree.<Cite source="halevi-kuzari-hirschfeld" locator="I.13" /> Maimonides makes knowing that God exists "the foundation of all foundations,"<Cite source="maimonides-mishneh-torah-touger" locator="Foundations of the Torah 1:1" /> yet he also insists that "a boundary is undoubtedly set to the human mind which it cannot pass."<Cite source="maimonides-guide-friedlander" locator="I.31" /> Scripture had said as much: "Concealed acts concern the ETERNAL our God."<Cite source="tanakh-jps-gender-sensitive" locator="Deuteronomy 29:28" /> The tradition is neither pure rationalism nor pure traditionalism.

## What this explains well

The account explains why knowledge is reliable yet fallible, and how its errors are caught. It gives testimony its due, since most of what anyone knows comes from others, and it holds confidence in reason together with modesty about its limits.

## Christian response

The Christian shares almost all of this. Both lanes stand on the same Hebrew Scriptures about creation, wisdom and the human mind, and Saadia's self-correcting roots of knowledge and Maimonides' boundary on the intellect have close Reformed parallels. The disagreement is not about whether knowledge is possible. It concerns what God has made known, and through whom. The Christian holds that the wisdom the Tanakh calls God's gift is finally personal and revealed in Christ, "in whom are hidden all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge."<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Colossians 2:3" /> The two lanes share an account of how knowing works and differ theologically over its fulfilment; neither is charged with incoherence. Which revelation is genuine is a historical question for a later domain.

## Deep dive

#### Saadia's tests and his reason for revelation

Saadia does not leave the roots of knowledge unchecked. A necessary inference, he says, must be guarded against five faults. It must not contradict what the senses perceive or what reason knows. It must not overturn some other truth, it must not contradict itself, and it must not escape one difficulty by falling into a worse one. Add care with sense and reason themselves, and patience to finish the inquiry, and the truth will emerge.<Cite source="saadia-emunot-ibn-tibbon" locator="Introduction 5" note="Hebrew; paraphrased" /> If reason can reach the truths of faith, why did God reveal them? Saadia answers that inquiry is slow, many people cannot complete it, and doubt can disturb it. So God gave the truths at once through prophets confirmed by public signs. Women, children and those unable to reason are not left without knowledge of God.<Cite source="saadia-emunot-ibn-tibbon" locator="Introduction 6" note="Hebrew; paraphrased" />

#### Halevi on testimony

Halevi's Rabbi introduces his faith to the Khazar king not as belief in the Creator of heaven and earth but as belief in the God of Abraham, Isaac and Jacob who brought Israel out of Egypt.<Cite source="halevi-kuzari-hirschfeld" locator="I.25" /> He thinks that Aristotle reasoned his way to an eternal world because "he had no tradition from any reliable source at his disposal."<Cite source="halevi-kuzari-hirschfeld" locator="I.65" /> For Halevi, public events witnessed by a whole people are a firmer foundation than arguments that philosophers dispute.<Cite source="halevi-kuzari-hirschfeld" locator="I.83–86" />

#### Maimonides on the limits of the mind

Maimonides compares the intellect to the senses: both perceive what lies within their range and cannot perceive what lies beyond it. He lists the causes that keep people from the truth: arrogance, the difficulty of the subject, lack of ability, and the habits of one's upbringing.<Cite source="maimonides-guide-friedlander" locator="I.31" /> Knowledge of God himself is mainly knowledge of what God is not and of what he does, as <QuestionLink question="ultimate-reality" worldview="judaism">the account of ultimate reality</QuestionLink> explains.

#### A real tension

Saadia's confidence that reason will reach everything the prophets taught sits uneasily with Maimonides' boundary. Maimonides himself admits that the creation of the world has not been demonstrated.<Cite source="maimonides-guide-friedlander" locator="II.25" /> The disagreement is about how far reason reaches, not whether it is trustworthy.
