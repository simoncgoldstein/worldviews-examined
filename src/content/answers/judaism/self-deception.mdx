## The view

Proverbs contrasts people's approval of their own ways with God's assessment of their motives; Jeremiah likewise warns about the heart's deceptiveness.<Cite source="tanakh-jps-gender-sensitive" locator="Proverbs 16:2; Jeremiah 17:9" /> Rabbinic teaching describes a transgression repeated until it appears permissible to its doer.<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Yoma 86b" /> The issue is not simply ignorance of a commandment. Familiarity with one's conduct can change how one judges it, while the inclination supplies reasons for continuing.

## What this explains well

This explains the slide from doing what one admits is wrong to regarding it as justified. Luzzatto distinguishes darkness which conceals obstacles from darkness which makes evil appear good, and recommends deliberate self-reckoning.<Cite source="luzzatto-mesillat-yesharim-sebag" locator="ch. 3" /> Self-deception can generate persuasive arguments, not only suppress uncomfortable thoughts.

## Christian response

The Christian agrees that conscience needs scrutiny rather than unconditional trust in one's sincerity. Both traditions appeal to the deceitful heart and God's knowledge of it. Reformed theology likewise acknowledges that believers need their hearts' deceitfulness exposed.<Cite source="wcf" locator="5.5" /> Jewish practice plainly recognizes rationalization: Maimonides calls for awakening, inspecting one's deeds, and repentance, a substantive account of moral correction.<Cite source="maimonides-mishneh-torah-touger" locator="Teshuvah 3:4" /> The traditions differ over renewal. The Jewish answer is self-reckoning and teshuvah, which a person undertakes with God's help; the Christian answer is that a heart able to deceive itself even in self-examination needs a new birth that only God's Spirit gives (see <QuestionLink question="self-salvation" worldview="christianity">the Christian account of salvation</QuestionLink>). That is the theological disagreement about renewal that runs through moral failure and salvation.

## Deep dive

#### More than an inward good intention

A person can sincerely approve his own ways and still need correction. That is why the Tanakh's contrast between self-approval and divine scrutiny matters: confidence is evidence of one's present assessment, not a guarantee of its truth. Rabbinic accounts of repeated sin explain how the assessment itself can change, making the wrong seem less threatening with each repetition.<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Yoma 86b" />

Luzzatto adds a practical obstacle. The inclination keeps people occupied so that they do not stop to consider their direction; his response is scheduled examination, not merely waiting for remorse.<Cite source="luzzatto-mesillat-yesharim-sebag" locator="ch. 2–3" /> Such practices can expose rationalization without making the examiner infallible. They work against the habit of treating one's familiar conduct as its own justification.

The account of the inclination's growth and the role of Torah is developed in <QuestionLink question="great-and-terrible" worldview="judaism">the flagship anthropology</QuestionLink>. <QuestionLink question="fail-the-good" worldview="judaism">Moral failure</QuestionLink> distinguishes temptation from action against better judgment. Self-deception adds a further danger: repeated action can reshape the very judgment used to excuse it.
