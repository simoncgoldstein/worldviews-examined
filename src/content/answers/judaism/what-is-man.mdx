## The view

Jewish anthropology begins with two accounts in Genesis. God creates humankind in the divine image, male and female, blesses them to be fertile and to rule the earth;<Cite source="tanakh-jps-gender-sensitive" locator="Genesis 1:26–28" /> and God forms the Human from the soil and blows into his nostrils the breath of life, so that he becomes a living being.<Cite source="tanakh-jps-gender-sensitive" locator="Genesis 2:7" /> The rabbis read these together as a composite. In some ways humans are like the angels: they stand upright, speak, understand and see. In others they are like animals: they eat and drink, procreate and die.<Cite source="midrash-rabbah-sefaria" locator="Bereshit Rabbah 8:11" />

The image is the ground of human worth. "Beloved is man for he was created in the image [of God]," says the Mishnah, and especially beloved because that was made known to him.<Cite source="mishnah-kulp" locator="Avot 3:14" /> Each person is also unique. A human ruler stamps many coins from one seal and they are all alike, but God stamped every person with the seal of the first human and no two are the same; therefore everyone must say that the world was created for his sake.<Cite source="mishnah-kulp" locator="Sanhedrin 4:5" />

Humans are free and responsible. "Everything is foreseen yet freedom of choice is granted."<Cite source="mishnah-kulp" locator="Avot 3:15" /> The rabbis describe two inclinations within each person, one toward good and one toward evil,<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Berakhot 61a" /> both part of the created human being and both to be directed to God's service.

The medieval philosophers gave this a theory. Saadia Gaon held that God's gift of wisdom lifts humans above every other creature, making the human being the axis of the world and therefore the one who is commanded and judged.<Cite source="saadia-emunot-ibn-tibbon" locator="IV.1" /> Maimonides read the image not as bodily shape but as the human form of knowledge, the intellect by which humans grasp what is not material.<Cite source="maimonides-mishneh-torah-touger" locator="Yesodei ha-Torah 4:8" />

Joseph Soloveitchik, a modern Orthodox thinker, returns to the two Genesis accounts and reads them as two types within humanity. "Adam the first", made in the image and commanded to fill and subdue the earth, expresses the image as creative power. "Adam the second", formed from dust and given God's breath, seeks an intimate relation with God.<Cite source="soloveitchik-lonely-man-of-faith" locator="pp. 10–11, 17" /> They are not two kinds of people. The person of faith moves between majesty and covenant, and halakha aims at one life in which a person is "both the creative, free agent, and the obedient servant of God". Soloveitchik offers this as his own interpretation, not a definitive halakhic philosophy.<Cite source="soloveitchik-lonely-man-of-faith" locator="pp. 10, 51" />

## What this explains well

The account holds together what humans most obviously are: animals that eat and die, and beings that speak, understand and worship. It grounds the worth of each person in the image rather than in achievement, and it values individuality, not only shared nature. Because both inclinations are created, it treats bodily appetite and ambition as raw material for holiness rather than as evils to be destroyed. Its texts have long supported a strong ethic of human dignity, which Reform Judaism's principles affirm for "every human being."<Cite source="ccar-principles-1999" locator="Statement of Principles" />

## Christian response

The Christian shares this foundation almost entirely: the same Genesis texts, the same image, the same composite of dust and breath. The difference is not over what humans are made to be but over two further claims. Christians hold that the image is fully displayed in one human being, Jesus, who is "the image of the invisible God"<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Colossians 1:15" /> and in whom the defaced image is restored.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Colossians 3:10" /> And they read the evil inclination as the sign of a corruption deeper than an energy awaiting direction. The first is a revelatory disagreement about Jesus; the second is argued in <QuestionLink question="great-and-terrible" worldview="judaism">the flagship question</QuestionLink>.

The rabbinic composite and Maimonides' intellectualism also pull in different directions on where the image lies; that is an internal debate, not a contradiction, and Reformed theology has faced it too.

## Pressure questions

- If the image is the intellect, as Maimonides held, what is the standing of a person whose intellect never develops?
- What does the image require of humans that a composite of angelic and animal traits would not?
- If God stamps every person from Adam's seal, is that uniqueness given at creation or achieved through Torah?

## Deep dive

#### Image, intellect and uniqueness

Rabbinic texts use the image in at least three ways. It grounds dignity: Rabbi Tanhuma warned that whoever demeans another should know whose likeness he disgraces.<Cite source="midrash-rabbah-sefaria" locator="Bereshit Rabbah 24:7" /> It grounds uniqueness, in the Mishnah's coin parable.<Cite source="mishnah-kulp" locator="Sanhedrin 4:5" /> And in the philosophers it names the intellect. Maimonides opens the *Guide* by arguing that "image" in Genesis refers to the specific form of a thing, and that the human form is intellectual apprehension, not physical shape.<Cite source="maimonides-guide-friedlander" locator="I.1" /> On his reading, the body is not the bearer of the image, though it is the necessary instrument of a human life. The rabbinic strand is more embodied. It ties the image to procreation itself: one who refrains from fathering children is said to diminish the divine image.<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Yevamot 63b" /> That link is developed in <QuestionLink question="offspring-family" worldview="judaism">the family question</QuestionLink>.

#### The composite and the inclinations

The angelic and animal traits are not two persons in one body. They are the range of one creature. The midrash even calls the evil inclination part of creation's "very good," since without it no one would build a house, marry, have children or trade.<Cite source="midrash-rabbah-sefaria" locator="Bereshit Rabbah 9:7" /> How that inclination becomes wrongdoing, and how Torah and repentance answer it, is the subject of <QuestionLink question="great-and-terrible" worldview="judaism">the flagship</QuestionLink>. For the ontology, the important claim is that human nature is created mixed and good, and that its unity comes from service of God rather than from the elimination of desire.

#### Soul, body and what persists

Classical sources speak of a soul that God gives and that survives the body, and of a future resurrection of the dead.<Cite source="tanakh-jps-gender-sensitive" locator="Daniel 12:2" /> They do not agree on how the two relate. Maimonides affirms the resurrection but describes the final life of the World to Come as souls without bodies enjoying knowledge of God.<Cite source="maimonides-mishneh-torah-touger" locator="Teshuvah 8:2" /> Saadia and Nachmanides hold that reward belongs to body and soul together and that the raised body shares in the final state.<Cite source="saadia-emunot-ibn-tibbon" locator="IX.5" note="Hebrew; our paraphrase" /><Cite source="ramban-shaar-ha-gemul-hebrew" locator="¶78" note="Hebrew; our paraphrase" /> The disagreement is real and long-standing, and the fuller account belongs to <QuestionLink question="after-death" worldview="judaism">the after-death question</QuestionLink>.

#### Universal and particular

The image belongs to every human being. One ancestor was created so that no one could say, "My father was greater than your father."<Cite source="mishnah-kulp" locator="Sanhedrin 4:5" /> Covenantal particularity is layered onto that universal worth; the same Mishnah that calls every human beloved adds that Israel is especially beloved as God's children,<Cite source="mishnah-kulp" locator="Avot 3:14" /> and Israel receives the Torah and its commandments, while the rest of humanity is bound by more general obligations. Judah Halevi's view that the divine influence rests on Israel in a special way is one strand, and it is contested within Jewish thought.<Cite source="halevi-kuzari-hirschfeld" locator="I.95" /> Modern streams share the anthropology while differing over the authority of halakha.
