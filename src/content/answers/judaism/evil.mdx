## The view

Isaiah attributes both favourable and disastrous conditions to the one Creator. The JPS rendering distinguishes weal and woe; this must not simply be restated as God committing moral wickedness.<Cite source="tanakh-jps-gender-sensitive" locator="Isaiah 45:7" /> Ecclesiastes contrasts humanity's created condition with the complications humans devise, and Ezekiel maintains personal responsibility for sin.<Cite source="tanakh-jps-gender-sensitive" locator="Ecclesiastes 7:29; Ezekiel 18:20" /> The account has no need for an independent evil creator opposed to God.

Rabbinic interpretation also refuses to treat all the energy associated with wrongdoing as intrinsically useless. Genesis Rabbah includes the evil inclination within creation's very goodness, connecting its energy with building, marriage and economic activity.<Cite source="midrash-rabbah-sefaria" locator="9:7" /> This does not make every act arising from it good. The challenge is governance of desire. Other passages describe how the inclination acquires power through repeated wrongdoing, gradually becoming master where it was once a visitor.<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Sukkah 52a–b" />

Maimonides supplies a different conceptual account. He distinguishes evils associated with material existence, injuries humans inflict on one another and evils people bring upon themselves through their conduct. He regards the last as the largest class.<Cite source="maimonides-guide-friedlander" locator="III.12" /> This is a philosophical taxonomy and assessment, not a statistical finding about every society or a warrant to blame every sufferer.

The classification of harms also prevents the word evil from doing too much work. Isaiah's woe, the misdirection of an inclination and an act of oppression are not simply synonyms. One concerns divine government of conditions; another concerns motivation; another concerns a human action and its victim. Their relations are part of the explanation, but the assertion of one Creator should not be allowed to erase the difference between causing or governing a calamity and committing a moral wrong.

## What this explains well

The inclination account explains how capacities sustaining ordinary life can become destructive without requiring a second human nature. The Maimonidean taxonomy then prevents all adversity from being assigned one cause. Embodied vulnerability, aggression and self-destructive conduct raise different questions. Saadia's account of many loves adds that disorder can result from letting one attachment rule the others, rather than from having no good capacities at all.<Cite source="saadia-emunot-ibn-tibbon" locator="X.1" /> These accounts make evil intelligible while leaving human conduct morally significant.

## Christian response

The Christian agrees that evil uses good capacities and that personal responsibility matters. Augustine describes evil as privation rather than an independent substance, while the Reformed confession places human corruption within Adam's history.<Cite source="augustine-enchiridion" locator="11, 13" /><Cite source="wcf" locator="6.2–4" /> The resemblance to rabbinic disordered inclination must not conceal that difference. Nor should Maimonides' matter/form explanation be treated as the same doctrine as Christian fall and corruption. The comparison concerns which account best explains both wrongdoing and suffering without making evil a competing ultimate power.

## Pressure questions

- How can the inclination be part of creation's goodness while acts arising from it deserve blame?
- What justifies the distinction between unavoidable vulnerability and harm traceable to one's own conduct?

## Deep dive

#### Created energy and cultivated disorder

Genesis Rabbah's claim concerns the role of an inclination within human life. Its examples connect motivation with activities through which households and society continue.<Cite source="midrash-rabbah-sefaria" locator="9:7" /> An inclination that can contribute to such activities still needs limits. Treating the passage as approval of cruelty would confuse a capacity's contribution with a verdict on every possible exercise of it.

The Talmud's images of growing strength and the guest who becomes master supply a temporal account of disorder. Repetition changes the relation between the person and the inclination.<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Sukkah 52a–b" /> That connects evil with habit rather than making it an unchangeable essence of the wrongdoer. <QuestionLink question="fail-the-good" worldview="judaism">Failure to do the good</QuestionLink> examines mastery and restraint; <QuestionLink question="self-deception" worldview="judaism">self-deception</QuestionLink> considers the way repeated wrongdoing can acquire the appearance of permission.

Saadia's account of many loves offers another way of understanding this relation. The problem is not simply possession of desire, but the ascendancy of one attachment at the expense of a properly ordered life.<Cite source="saadia-emunot-ibn-tibbon" locator="X.1" /> It can illuminate moral imbalance without claiming that every rabbinic text uses his philosophical framework.

#### Maimonides and the limits of a taxonomy

Maimonides links defects and vices with matter while tracing merits to form, especially the human intellectual capacity.<Cite source="maimonides-guide-friedlander" locator="III.8" /> His threefold classification of evils develops the difference between the limitations of material existence and harms produced through action. Human domination falls into one class; conduct through which a person damages himself falls into another.<Cite source="maimonides-guide-friedlander" locator="III.12" /> The account therefore contains both vulnerability and agency.

Calling self-inflicted evils the largest class expresses Maimonides' argument. It is not a permission to assume that an injured person's suffering was self-caused, or to minimize oppression by counting it against other categories. The actual cause of an injury still matters. His philosophical account should also remain distinguishable from Genesis Rabbah's interpretation of the inclination: the two explain overlapping phenomena with different conceptual structures.

Avot acknowledges limits in explaining the security of the wicked and the afflictions of the righteous.<Cite source="mishnah-kulp" locator="Avot 4:15" /> That humility belongs within the comparison rather than outside it. <QuestionLink question="suffering" worldview="judaism">Suffering</QuestionLink> preserves both moral reflection and resistance to a simplistic diagnosis of victims; <QuestionLink question="death" worldview="judaism">death</QuestionLink> shows the disagreement about mortality's relation to sin. Neither question supports reducing Jewish thought to one complete explanation of every evil.

Ezekiel's emphasis on personal responsibility further constrains the comparison.<Cite source="tanakh-jps-gender-sensitive" locator="Ezekiel 18:20" /> A discussion of the common human condition cannot silently become a transfer of a parent's personal wrongdoing to a child. Nor does the text by itself settle every philosophical account of inherited vulnerability. The pertinent distinction is between responsibility for an action and the conditions under which later people live. Keeping it explicit prevents a Christian category from being imported as though the rabbinic inclination account already affirmed it.
