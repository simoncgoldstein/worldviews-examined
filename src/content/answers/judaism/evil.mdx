## The view

In Jewish teaching, moral evil is human wrongdoing, and the rabbis explain it through the evil inclination (*yetzer ha-ra*). That inclination is not a corrupt second nature: the energy that can drive wrongdoing belongs to good creation. Genesis Rabbah includes the evil inclination within creation's very goodness, connecting its energy with building, marriage and economic activity.<Cite source="midrash-rabbah-sefaria" locator="9:7" /> Not every act that arises from it is good; the task is to govern desire, not to eliminate it. The inclination acquires power through repeated wrongdoing, gradually becoming master where it was once a visitor, and the sages say that no one would overcome it without God's help.<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Sukkah 52a–b" /> Yielding to it is the person's own act: Ezekiel holds each person responsible for his own sin, not for a parent's.<Cite source="tanakh-jps-gender-sensitive" locator="Ezekiel 18:20" />

Scripture places all this within one Creator's world. Isaiah attributes both well-being and calamity to the one Creator; the text speaks of conditions, weal and woe, not of God committing moral wickedness.<Cite source="tanakh-jps-gender-sensitive" locator="Isaiah 45:7" /> Ecclesiastes contrasts humanity's created condition with the complications humans devise.<Cite source="tanakh-jps-gender-sensitive" locator="Ecclesiastes 7:29" /> The account has no need for an independent evil creator opposed to God.

Maimonides supplies a different conceptual account. He distinguishes evils associated with material existence, injuries humans inflict on one another and evils people bring upon themselves through their conduct. He regards the last as the largest class.<Cite source="maimonides-guide-friedlander" locator="III.12" /> This is a philosophical judgment, not a statistic and not a warrant to blame sufferers.

The word evil therefore covers different things. Isaiah's woe concerns God's government of conditions; the inclination concerns motivation; oppression is a human act with a victim. Belief in one Creator does not erase the difference between governing a calamity and committing a moral wrong.

## What this explains well

The inclination account explains how capacities sustaining ordinary life can become destructive without requiring a second human nature. The Maimonidean taxonomy then prevents all adversity from being assigned one cause. Embodied vulnerability, aggression and self-destructive conduct raise different questions. Saadia's account of many loves adds that disorder can result from letting one attachment rule the others, rather than from having no good capacities at all.<Cite source="saadia-emunot-ibn-tibbon" locator="X.1" /> These accounts make evil intelligible while leaving human conduct morally significant.

## Christian response

The Christian agrees that evil uses good capacities and that personal responsibility matters. Augustine's account of evil as the privation of a good nature, not an independent substance, has a clear parallel in an inclination that is good in its place.<Cite source="augustine-enchiridion" locator="11, 13" /> The real difference concerns how deep the disorder runs and where it began. The rabbinic account does not make wrongdoing occasional. It calls the inclination evil from youth, describes it attacking a person every day, and says that no one would overcome it without God's help.<Cite source="tanakh-jps-gender-sensitive" locator="Genesis 8:21" /><Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Sukkah 52a–b" /> Yet the inclination remains part of created human nature, it gains its mastery as a person yields to it, and Torah and God's help are the means of governing it. In the Reformed account humanity was created upright, and corruption entered through Adam's fall, so that every person's nature is disordered before any habit forms.<Cite source="wcf" locator="6.2–4" /> Both accounts therefore explain why everyone sins. The Christian argues that his better explains what kind of disorder this is: an inclination that is evil from youth and prevails wherever God does not help looks less like a good capacity awaiting governance than like a nature turned from God, and Ecclesiastes says that God made humanity upright before humans devised their schemes.<Cite source="tanakh-jps-gender-sensitive" locator="Ecclesiastes 7:29" /> The rabbinic reply is that the inclination is good in its place, the energy behind building and family, and that yielding to it belongs to the person, not to creation; Ecclesiastes describes human scheming, not a fall of the race. Maimonides' matter-and-form explanation is a third account, distinct from both. The disagreement is theological, about human nature and its history, and both sides deny that evil is a competing ultimate power.

## Pressure questions

- How can the inclination be part of creation's goodness while acts arising from it deserve blame?
- What justifies the distinction between unavoidable vulnerability and harm traceable to one's own conduct?

## Deep dive

#### Created energy and cultivated disorder

Genesis Rabbah's claim concerns the role of an inclination within human life. Its examples connect motivation with activities through which households and society continue.<Cite source="midrash-rabbah-sefaria" locator="9:7" /> An inclination that can contribute to such activities still needs limits: the passage approves the capacity, not every exercise of it.

The Talmud's images of growing strength and the guest who becomes master supply a temporal account of disorder. Repetition changes the relation between the person and the inclination.<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Sukkah 52a–b" /> That connects evil with habit rather than making it an unchangeable essence of the wrongdoer. <QuestionLink question="fail-the-good" worldview="judaism">Failure to do the good</QuestionLink> examines mastery and restraint; <QuestionLink question="self-deception" worldview="judaism">self-deception</QuestionLink> considers the way repeated wrongdoing can acquire the appearance of permission.

Saadia's account of many loves offers another way of understanding this relation. The problem is not simply possession of desire, but the ascendancy of one attachment at the expense of a properly ordered life.<Cite source="saadia-emunot-ibn-tibbon" locator="X.1" /> This is Saadia's philosophical framework, not that of every rabbinic text, but it illuminates the same imbalance.

#### Maimonides and the limits of a taxonomy

Maimonides links defects and vices with matter while tracing merits to form, especially the human intellectual capacity.<Cite source="maimonides-guide-friedlander" locator="III.8" /> His threefold classification of evils develops the difference between the limitations of material existence and harms produced through action. Human domination falls into one class; conduct through which a person damages himself falls into another.<Cite source="maimonides-guide-friedlander" locator="III.12" /> The account therefore contains both vulnerability and agency.

Calling self-inflicted evils the largest class is Maimonides' philosophical judgment. It gives no license to assume that an injured person's suffering was self-caused, or to minimize oppression by counting it against other categories; the actual cause of an injury still matters. His account and Genesis Rabbah's interpretation of the inclination explain overlapping phenomena with different conceptual structures.

Avot acknowledges limits in explaining the security of the wicked and the afflictions of the righteous.<Cite source="mishnah-kulp" locator="Avot 4:15" /> That humility is part of the Jewish account itself. <QuestionLink question="suffering" worldview="judaism">Suffering</QuestionLink> preserves both moral reflection and resistance to a simplistic diagnosis of victims; <QuestionLink question="death" worldview="judaism">death</QuestionLink> shows the disagreement about mortality's relation to sin. Jewish thought offers no single explanation of every evil.

Ezekiel's emphasis on personal responsibility marks a real difference from the Christian doctrine of original sin: a parent's wrongdoing is not charged to the child.<Cite source="tanakh-jps-gender-sensitive" locator="Ezekiel 18:20" /> The text distinguishes responsibility for an action from the conditions under which later people live. It does not settle every account of inherited vulnerability, but the rabbinic inclination account contains no inherited guilt.
