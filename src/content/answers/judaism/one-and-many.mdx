## The view

Judaism has no formal doctrine of "the one and the many." What it has is a sharp line between the oneness of God and the many things he made.

God's oneness is the center of Jewish faith: "the ETERNAL alone is God; there is none else."<Cite source="tanakh-jps-gender-sensitive" locator="Deuteronomy 4:35" /> Maimonides explains that this oneness is unlike any other. God is not one as a species is one, containing many members, nor as a body is one, made of many parts. "There exists no unity similar to His in this world."<Cite source="maimonides-mishneh-torah-touger" locator="Yesodei ha-Torah 1:7" />

Plurality belongs to creation. The world contains countless distinct things, and they are real. Yet Maimonides also sees the created world as a single whole: the universe is "one individual being," whose many parts are related like the organs of a human body.<Cite source="maimonides-guide-friedlander" locator="I.72" /> Unity of a lesser kind belongs to the world because one God made and governs it.

## What this explains well

The view explains why the world is both diverse and coherent: many creatures, one Creator, one ordered whole. It keeps God's oneness distinct from the world's multiplicity without making that multiplicity an illusion.

## Christian response

The Christian shares the distinction between the one Creator and the many creatures. The difference concerns God's own unity. Maimonides' unity excludes all inner distinction. Christians confess one God in three persons, so distinction as well as unity has its source in God.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Matthew 28:19" /> This is a theological disagreement grounded in revelation.

## Pressure questions

- If God's unity contains no distinction whatever, does the world's real plurality have any source in God's own nature?
- If creatures are "as nothing" before God, as some Ḥasidic teachers say, in what sense are the many real?

## Deep dive

#### Saadia on attributes

Saadia faced the question of plurality within God through the attributes. God is living, powerful and wise, but these are not three things in God. The mind grasps them in a single insight, and language forces us to use three words.<Cite source="saadia-emunot-ibn-tibbon" locator="II.4" /> Divine unity is protected by treating multiplicity as a feature of our speech, not of God.

#### Ḥabad's variant

Ḥabad Ḥasidism presses the relation between God and the world further. Every creature is continually brought into being from nothing by God's creative word. Measured against that creative power, creatures are as nothing, and their apparent separate existence comes from our not perceiving the divine vitality within them (our paraphrase of the Hebrew).<Cite source="tanya-hebrew-sefaria" locator="Sha'ar ha-Yiḥud ve-ha-Emunah 3" /> This leans toward denying the world independent reality while still affirming creation. Kabbalistic teaching on the divine emanations (*sefirot*), another major way Jewish thought relates the one God to multiplicity, is not researched here. Neither strand is presented as the rabbinic position.
