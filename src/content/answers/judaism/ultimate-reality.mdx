## The view

Judaism's answer begins with the Shema: the LORD is one.<Cite source="tanakh-jps-gender-sensitive" locator="Deuteronomy 6:4" /> Israel is told that "the ETERNAL alone is God; there is none else,"<Cite source="tanakh-jps-gender-sensitive" locator="Deuteronomy 4:35, 39" /> and that the same God forms light and creates darkness. No rival principle shares his rule.<Cite source="tanakh-jps-gender-sensitive" locator="Isaiah 45:7, 18" /> What ultimately exists, then, is one God, and alongside him only what he has made.

The rabbis drew out what this means for creation. The world was created with ten divine utterances.<Cite source="mishnah-kulp" locator="Avot 5:1" /> When a philosopher suggested that God had worked with ready-made materials, Rabban Gamliel answered that Scripture speaks of each of those materials as itself created.<Cite source="midrash-rabbah-sefaria" locator="Bereshit Rabbah 1:9" /> The world is real and good, but it is not a second eternal thing beside God.

The medieval philosophers gave this a precise form. Maimonides opens his code of law with it: "the foundation of all foundations" is to know that a Primary Being brought all existence into being. If everything else ceased to exist, God would remain, because everything requires him and he requires nothing.<Cite source="maimonides-mishneh-torah-touger" locator="Yesodei ha-Torah 1:1–3" /> His oneness is unlike any oneness in the world. It is not the unity of a class with many members, nor of a body with many parts.<Cite source="maimonides-mishneh-torah-touger" locator="Yesodei ha-Torah 1:7" /> This systematizes biblical monotheism rather than replacing it; how far philosophy should shape the picture is itself debated, as the Deep dive shows.

## What this explains well

The view holds together two things that are easy to separate: a world that is genuinely real and good, and a God who is not part of it. It rules out both idolatry, which treats something created as divine, and dualism, in which good and evil are rival eternal powers. It makes the whole world dependent on one source, so reality is one creation rather than a contest. And it guards God's transcendence without forgetting that this God speaks to Israel.

## Christian response

The Christian lane shares this foundation, because it reads the same Scriptures. Both affirm one Creator, a real and dependent creation, and the sharp distinction between them. The difference concerns God's inner life. Maimonides defines God's unity so that it excludes any distinction within God.<Cite source="maimonides-mishneh-torah-touger" locator="Yesodei ha-Torah 1:7" /> Christians confess one God in three persons. They read the Shema as excluding other gods, not as excluding the Son and the Spirit, on the basis of New Testament revelation.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="John 1:1–3" /> This is a theological disagreement that turns on revelation, not a contradiction in Jewish thought.

The Christian also notes a debate internal to Judaism. If, as Maimonides holds, God's essence can be described only by negation, it is unclear how the Torah's personal language tells us what God is truly like. Halevi, writing earlier, also treated many divine names as descriptions of God's acts, but he insisted that God is truly known through his address to Israel, not through philosophy alone.

## Pressure questions

- Does the Shema's "one" exclude every distinction within God, or the existence of other gods?
- If God's essence can be described only by negations, in what sense do the prophets' words about his love and anger tell the truth about him?

## Deep dive

#### Maimonides' necessary existence

The first chapter of *Mishneh Torah* reasons in a philosophical register. God's existence is of a different kind from everything else's: "the truth of His [being] does not resemble the truth of any of their [beings]."<Cite source="maimonides-mishneh-torah-touger" locator="Yesodei ha-Torah 1:1–3" /> The chapter also uses medieval sphere-cosmology to describe God moving the heavens. That framework is dated, and the core claim does not depend on it. The same chapter argues from unity to incorporeality: many gods would have to be distinguished by body and form, and a God with body and form would be limited.<Cite source="maimonides-mishneh-torah-touger" locator="Yesodei ha-Torah 1:7" /> In the *Guide*, Maimonides carries this into a negative theology, in which nothing positive can be said of God's essence. That is treated in <QuestionLink question="ultimate-personal" worldview="judaism">whether ultimate reality is personal</QuestionLink>.

#### No second power

The rabbis read even the creation of a single first human as a lesson in monotheism: one ancestor was created so that heretics could not say there are "many ruling powers in heaven."<Cite source="mishnah-kulp" locator="Sanhedrin 4:5" /> Isaiah's claim that God forms light and creates darkness served the same purpose against dualism. Rabban Gamliel's answer to the philosopher cites it to show that even darkness and disorder are created.<Cite source="midrash-rabbah-sefaria" locator="Bereshit Rabbah 1:9" />

#### The divine name

When Moses asks God's name, the answer is *Ehyeh-Asher-Ehyeh*. The JPS translators note that the meaning of the Hebrew is uncertain: "I Am That I Am," "I Am Who I Am" or "I Will Be What I Will Be."<Cite source="tanakh-jps-gender-sensitive" locator="Exodus 3:14" /> On any of these readings the name speaks of who God is, not of anything in the world.

#### Halevi's counterweight

Judah Halevi warned that philosophy alone reaches only a remote first cause. The God of Israel is known through his address to a people, as <QuestionLink question="ultimate-personal" worldview="judaism">whether ultimate reality is personal</QuestionLink> explains.<Cite source="halevi-kuzari-hirschfeld" locator="IV.3" />

#### A mystical variant

Ḥasidic thought, especially Ḥabad, stresses that creatures are continually brought into being from nothing by God's word. Measured against that creative power, they are as nothing.<Cite source="tanya-hebrew-sefaria" locator="Sha'ar ha-Yiḥud ve-ha-Emunah 3" /> This leans toward denying the world independent reality, while still affirming creation. Kabbalistic teaching on the divine emanations (*sefirot*) is a further strand not treated here. Neither is the standard rabbinic-philosophical account, but both are real Jewish options. The relation of divine unity to created plurality is taken up in <QuestionLink question="one-and-many" worldview="judaism">the one and the many</QuestionLink>.
