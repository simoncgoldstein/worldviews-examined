## The view

Guilt concerns wrongs against God's commands and injuries within human relationships. The Tanakh connects forgiveness with concrete practices of return: Leviticus provides offerings even for those unable to afford the usual animals, while Solomon's prayer asks God to forgive a people who acknowledge sin and turn toward him.<Cite source="tanakh-jps-gender-sensitive" locator="Leviticus 5:11–13; 1 Kings 8:46–50" /> This is a world of commanded atonement and mercy, not a claim that remorse makes every wrong disappear automatically.

Rabbinic teshuvah means return. Maimonides describes leaving the sin, regretting it, resolving not to repeat it and confessing; full repentance is tested when a similar opportunity returns and the person refrains.<Cite source="maimonides-mishneh-torah-touger" locator="Teshuvah 2:1–2" /> The changed deed matters because the return concerns the person, not merely an altered mood.

The Mishnah distinguishes wrongs against God from wrongs against a neighbor. Yom Kippur does not atone for the latter until the injured person is appeased; deliberate reliance on future repentance or the holy day also receives a warning.<Cite source="mishnah-kulp" locator="Yoma 8:8–9" /> Divine forgiveness and interpersonal reconciliation therefore cannot simply substitute for one another. Nor is it a scheme in which a sinner buys acceptance with a pile of meritorious acts: atonement comes through commanded return and God's mercy.

## What this explains well

This account makes repentance morally concrete and guards the victim's place. A ritual calendar does not allow a wrongdoer to ignore the person injured. At the same time, past sin need not define the returning person forever: Maimonides describes the penitent as again beloved and near to God.<Cite source="maimonides-mishneh-torah-touger" locator="Teshuvah 7:4–6" /> Accountability and hope for restored relationship belong together.

## Christian response

The Reformed Christian shares the demands for confession, changed conduct and repair. He asks a further question: how a just God forgives guilt that remains real after the sinner has turned. His answer is that God himself provided the sacrifice, Christ bearing that guilt, so that God remains just in forgiving; repentance is necessary but is not the ground of pardon (see <QuestionLink question="guilt" worldview="christianity">the Christian account of guilt</QuestionLink>).<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 3:24–26" /><Cite source="wcf" locator="15.3" /> The Jewish reply can appeal to God's authority and promise to forgive those who return, which needs no further satisfaction. The dispute is over the ground and fulfillment of atonement, not whether Jews know mercy or whether repentance is only external compliance.

## Pressure questions

- What makes a return genuine when the original damage cannot be repaired?
- How do God's forgiveness and the injured person's outstanding claim relate?

## Deep dive

#### Sacrifice, repentance and the holy day

The biblical sacrificial provisions belong within the wider account of confessed wrongdoing and divine forgiveness. Leviticus's provision for poverty is particularly useful: access to the prescribed offering is not measured simply by purchasing power.<Cite source="tanakh-jps-gender-sensitive" locator="Leviticus 5:11–13" /> The passage establishes a commanded means in its biblical setting. It does not imply that later Jewish practice requires the continued performance of those sacrifices.

Yoma also gives atonement more than one step. The Mishnah associates atonement with repentance and differentiates kinds of transgression and means of atonement.<Cite source="mishnah-kulp" locator="Yoma 8:8–9" /> It warns against planning to sin on the assumption that repentance will later cancel the consequences. Return cannot coherently be a device for maintaining the same wrongdoing. The neighbor's place imposes a further limit: worship does not erase obligations owed to another person.

#### A changed relationship, not an erased history

Maimonides' test of repentance moves from speech to conduct. The new refusal when temptation recurs shows that the agent's response has changed.<Cite source="maimonides-mishneh-torah-touger" locator="Teshuvah 2:1–2" /> His later account of restored nearness counters the claim that a sinner's record fixes his relationship to God permanently. Neither teaching says the past injury never happened.

The strongest Jewish position, then, is that God mercifully welcomes genuine return within a commanded covenantal life. The Christian question, why repentance is not sufficient, is put to that position, not to a scheme of self-earned innocence: it asks whether a just God's welcome needs the ground Christianity finds in Christ. The setting of ultimate accountability is considered in <QuestionLink question="after-death" worldview="judaism">after death</QuestionLink>.
