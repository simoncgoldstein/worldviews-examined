## The view

The Hebrew Bible treats the regularity of nature as something God has established and pledged. Through Jeremiah God speaks of his faithfulness to Israel "as surely as I have established My covenant with day and night—the laws of heaven and earth."<Cite source="tanakh-jps-gender-sensitive" locator="Jeremiah 33:25" /> The fixed order of creation is the measure of God's reliability, and the expectation that it will continue rests on him.

The rabbis drew a practical lesson. Asked why God does not destroy the idols people worship, the sages answered that people worship the sun, moon and stars, and God will not destroy the world because of fools. "Rather, the world follows its course." Stolen seed still grows when planted; the sinners will answer for it later.<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Avodah Zarah 54b" /> Nature's ordinary course does not bend to each human act, even a wicked one.

Maimonides defended stable natures against the Muslim theologians of the *kalām*. They held that the constant behaviour of things "only follows the direction of habit, just as the king generally rides on horseback," and that fire could just as well cause cold and still be fire. Maimonides answered that this confuses what the imagination can picture with what the intellect knows to be possible; things have real, constant natures.<Cite source="maimonides-guide-friedlander" locator="I.73, tenth proposition" /> Nachmanides placed more weight on God's hidden governance within those natures, as <QuestionLink question="order" worldview="judaism">the account of order</QuestionLink> explains.

None of this is an answer to Hume, whose problem arose centuries later. No classical Jewish source addresses it. What the tradition offers is a theological ground for expecting regularity: the world is reliable because the God who made it is faithful. It does not offer a non-circular argument that the future will resemble the past.

## What this explains well

The account explains why nature can be trusted without being worshipped or treated as independent of God, and why moral disorder does not unravel the physical world.

## Christian response

Here the Christian and Jewish lanes stand on the same ground. Both read Jeremiah 33:25 as tying nature's order to God's covenant faithfulness, and the Talmud's "the world follows its course" closely parallels the Westminster Confession's ordinary providence through second causes.<Cite source="wcf" locator="5.2–3" /> Both therefore offer the same kind of answer to induction, with the same limit: a reason to expect regularity, not a proof that escapes the circle (see <QuestionLink question="induction" worldview="christianity">the Christian answer</QuestionLink>). On this question there is shared ground, not a disagreement.

## Pressure questions

- If the world follows its course even when people sin, how is that course related to God's moral will?
- If constant natures are real, as Maimonides held, what makes them constant: the things themselves, or God's continuing will?

## Deep dive

#### The kalām and the king's habit

Maimonides' tenth proposition presents the *kalām* doctrine of "admissibility": whatever the imagination can picture, the intellect admits as possible. On that view an elephant might be as small as an insect, and the regular behaviour of things is like a king's habit of riding on horseback, which he could break at any time by walking.<Cite source="maimonides-guide-friedlander" locator="I.73, tenth proposition" /> Maimonides' reply is that some things that cannot be imagined are demonstrably real, so imagination is no test of possibility. The intellect, not the imagination, distinguishes the necessary, the possible and the impossible. The same debate, from the Islamic side, appears under <QuestionLink question="induction" worldview="islam">the Islamic answer</QuestionLink>.

#### Covenant and creation

Jeremiah's comparison runs in both directions. God's covenant with Israel is as sure as the laws of heaven and earth, and those laws are themselves a covenant. The psalmist likewise says that God made the heavenly bodies "endure forever, establishing an order that shall never change."<Cite source="tanakh-jps-gender-sensitive" locator="Psalms 148:6" /> Expectation of regularity is therefore, in the biblical idiom, a form of trust.

#### The limits of the material

It would overstate the evidence to present Jewish thought as having solved, or even posed, the problem of induction. The sources show why regularity can be expected and what grounds it, not how an inference from observed to unobserved cases is justified. Later Jewish philosophers who engaged modern philosophy were not researched for this answer.
