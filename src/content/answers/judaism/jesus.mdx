## The view

The Jewish answer is mainly a judgement about criteria, not a denial of history. The Messiah, as Maimonides defines him, is a king from David's line who restores the kingdom, builds the Temple and gathers the exiles. If he succeeds, "he is definitely the Mashiach." If he fails or is killed, "he surely is not the redeemer promised by the Torah."<Cite source="maimonides-mishneh-torah-touger" locator="Kings and Wars 11:4" /> By that test Jesus was not the Messiah. Maimonides names him directly: "Jesus of Nazareth who aspired to be the Mashiach and was executed by the court." He judges Christianity a stumbling block, yet says that in God's plan Christianity and Islam "will only serve to prepare the way for Mashiach's coming," by spreading knowledge of the Messiah, the Torah and the commandments to the nations.<Cite source="maimonides-mishneh-torah-touger" locator="Kings and Wars 11:4" />

The second judgement concerns God, who is one and incorporeal (see <QuestionLink question="ultimate-reality" worldview="judaism">ultimate reality</QuestionLink>). When Nachmanides debated a Christian convert before the king of Aragon in 1263, he called this the heart of the disagreement. The doctrine that the Creator of heaven and earth was born of a woman, grew up and was put to death is, he argued, something reason does not accept, nature does not allow and the prophets never said. The Messiah, he told the king, is simply a king of flesh and blood like himself.<Cite source="nachmanides-vikuach-wikisource" locator="second day" note="Hebrew; our paraphrase" />

The third is exegetical. Jewish interpreters read the texts Christians cite differently. Rashi reads Isaiah's suffering servant as Israel.<Cite source="rashi-tanakh-judaica-press" locator="on Isaiah 53:3" /> Saadia argues that Daniel's "anointed one" who is "cut off" means any anointed priest at the end of the Second Temple period, not one known individual.<Cite source="saadia-emunot-ibn-tibbon" locator="VIII.9" note="Hebrew; our paraphrase" /> And the Torah itself cannot be abrogated (see <QuestionLink question="revelation" worldview="judaism">revelation</QuestionLink>).

None of this requires denying the first-century facts. He was crucified under Pontius Pilate, as Paul, the Gospels and the Roman historian Tacitus attest.<Cite source="tacitus-annals-church-brodribb" locator="15.44" /> The Talmud preserves a different story, of "Jesus the Nazarene" stoned and hanged on Passover eve for sorcery.<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Sanhedrin 43a" /> But that passage is centuries later, and a century ago the Jewish Encyclopedia already judged the Talmud's references to Jesus "for the most part as legendary as those in the apocryphal gospels."<Cite source="jewish-encyclopedia-jesus" locator="Jacobs, In History" /> It is polemic, not counter-evidence.

## What this explains well

The view holds the Messiah to public, historical tests and guards the unity of God, and it explains why the world after Jesus does not look redeemed.

## Christian response

The Christian shares the Scriptures and the hope, and the dispute is largely interpretive: what kind of Messiah did the prophets promise? Christians read the promises as fulfilled in two stages. The Messiah first suffers and is vindicated by resurrection; he "will appear a second time" to complete the work.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Hebrews 9:28" /> The prophets' universal peace is therefore not denied but awaited. That reading stands or falls with the resurrection, which is a historical question (see <QuestionLink question="jesus" worldview="christianity">the Christian answer</QuestionLink>). If God raised a crucified claimant, being killed does not disqualify him.

On divinity, the earliest evidence is itself Jewish. Within about two decades Jewish followers were praying to Jesus and calling him Lord alongside the one God.<Cite source="hurtado-worship-of-christ" locator="Early devotion to Christ" /> The Christian argues that Israel's monotheism, rightly understood, has room for the Son. Judaism answers that it does not. That disagreement is theological, but it should be judged with the first-century evidence in view rather than by later polemic on either side.

## Pressure questions

- If the prophets' messianic promises could be fulfilled in two stages, what in the Hebrew Scriptures rules that out?
- How should the fact that Jesus' earliest worshippers were monotheistic Jews be explained?

## Deep dive

#### Maimonides on Christianity and Islam

Maimonides' account of Jesus was controversial: after the Barcelona disputation, the Mishneh Torah was condemned to be burned because of this chapter's references to Jesus.<Cite source="ej-barcelona-disputation" locator="whole" /> The passage holds two judgements together. Christianity is a stumbling block, fulfilling Daniel's warning that "the vulgar among your people shall exalt themselves... but they shall stumble." Yet God's purposes are beyond human understanding: the nations now discuss the Messiah, the Torah and its commandments, some saying the commandments have lapsed, others that the Messiah has come. When the true Messiah succeeds, Maimonides says, they will realize that they inherited error.<Cite source="maimonides-mishneh-torah-touger" locator="Kings and Wars 11:4" />

#### The Barcelona disputation

In July 1263 the convert Pablo Christiani set out to prove from the Talmud that the Messiah had come, that he was "both human and divine" and atoned for sin, and that the commandments had lapsed. Nachmanides answered that the aggadic passages of the Talmud are not binding, and scholars still disagree whether that stance was tactical or traditional. Two accounts survive, his Hebrew one and a shorter Latin one. He left Spain afterwards and reached the land of Israel in 1267.<Cite source="ej-barcelona-disputation" locator="whole" /> In his account he also argued that the Talmud's sages lived after Jesus yet did not believe in him, that Genesis 49:10 does not require an unbroken kingship, and that since Jesus' time the world has been full of violence, contrary to Isaiah's promise that nations will not learn war.<Cite source="nachmanides-vikuach-wikisource" locator="first and second days" note="Hebrew; our paraphrase" /> The Hebrew text used here is an online transcription whose base edition is not stated, so it is paraphrased rather than quoted.

#### The Talmud's Jesus passage

The baraita in Sanhedrin 43a says a herald went out for forty days before the execution, and the sage Ulla explains the delay by saying Jesus "had close ties with the government." The passage then names five disciples.<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Sanhedrin 43a" /> It conflicts with the earliest sources on the manner of death, crucifixion by Roman authority, and both its identification and date are debated. Maimonides' phrase "executed by the court" follows this rabbinic tradition, not first-century evidence. The medieval *Toledot Yeshu*, which says the body was removed from the tomb, is later polemical folklore with no independent information.<Cite source="jewish-encyclopedia-jesus" locator="Krauss, In Jewish Legend" />

#### Daniel and the date of the Messiah

The Christian theologian Turretin argued that Genesis 49:10 and Daniel 9 required the Messiah to come while the Second Temple stood.<Cite source="turretin-institutio-1847" locator="XIII.1" note="Latin; paraphrased" /> Saadia reads Daniel's seventy weeks as four hundred and ninety years running from the exile through the Second Temple period, and the "anointed one" who is cut off as the anointed priesthood, which ended with the Temple's destruction.<Cite source="saadia-emunot-ibn-tibbon" locator="VIII.9" note="Hebrew; our paraphrase" /> These are rival readings of shared texts, and neither is settled by history alone.
