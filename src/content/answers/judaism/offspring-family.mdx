## The view

Jewish tradition treats having children as a commandment. God blesses humankind to be fertile and increase,<Cite source="tanakh-jps-gender-sensitive" locator="Genesis 1:28" /> and the Mishnah rules that a man may not abstain from procreation unless he already has children.<Cite source="mishnah-kulp" locator="Yevamot 6:6" /> The world itself was made "to be populated," as Isaiah says that God formed the earth to be inhabited.<Cite source="mishnah-kulp" locator="Gittin 4:5" /> One sage says that a man who refrains from procreation "diminishes the Divine Image."<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Yevamot 63b" /> Maimonides codifies the commandment as a positive obligation.<Cite source="maimonides-mishneh-torah-touger" locator="Ishut 15:1–2" /> The sages also preserve an exception: Ben Azzai, who stayed unmarried because his soul yearned for Torah.

Marriage is valued for itself as well. A man without a wife, says the Talmud, is "without joy, without blessing, without goodness."<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Yevamot 62b" /> It is not good for the human to be alone.<Cite source="tanakh-jps-gender-sensitive" locator="Genesis 2:18–24" /> The home transmits the covenant: parents teach Torah to their children,<Cite source="tanakh-jps-gender-sensitive" locator="Deuteronomy 6:7" /> and a father must teach his son Torah and a trade and see to his marriage.<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Kiddushin 29a" />

## What this explains well

The account makes family part of God's purpose for the world. It joins love, children and covenant learning in the home, and it explains the strong communal priority on marriage and continuity.

## Christian response

The Christian shares the creation basis and the view of children as blessing. The difference concerns singleness. Ben Azzai's exception shows that Jewish tradition can honour a life devoted to Torah, but the norm remains obligation. Paul calls singleness a positive gift for undivided devotion,<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="1 Corinthians 7:7, 32–35" /> grounding this in the coming of the kingdom. That is a theological difference, not an inconsistency.

## Pressure questions

- If not procreating diminishes the divine image, how should one understand a childless life?
- Is Ben Azzai's choice an exception the tradition tolerates or a calling it honours?

## Deep dive

#### Disputes in the sources

The Mishnah records disputes. The School of Shammai required two sons and the School of Hillel a son and a daughter. The obligation formally falls on the man, though Rabbi Yohanan ben Beroka held that the verse addresses both.<Cite source="mishnah-kulp" locator="Yevamot 6:6" /> The same Mishnah says that after ten childless years a man may not continue to abstain from procreation and that a divorced wife may remarry. Gittin's statement that the world was made to be populated comes in a ruling about a half-freed slave who could not otherwise marry.<Cite source="mishnah-kulp" locator="Gittin 4:5" /> These texts reflect their legal setting.

#### Ben Azzai's exception

Ben Azzai taught that refusing to procreate is like shedding blood and diminishing the divine image, yet he never married. Challenged, he answered: "my soul yearns for Torah," and "it is possible for the world to be maintained by others."<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Yevamot 63b" /> The tradition preserved his answer as an exception, not a rule.

#### Streams and scope

Orthodox, Conservative and Reform Judaism differ on gender roles, marriage law and related questions. This answer reports classical sources without adjudicating them.
