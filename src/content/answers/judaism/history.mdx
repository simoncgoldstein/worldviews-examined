## The view

Judaism reads history as the story of God's covenant with Israel, from creation and the patriarchs through the exodus, Sinai, the land, exile and return, toward a promised redemption. Maimonides grounds that hope in the Torah itself: "God will bring back your captivity and have mercy upon you. He will again gather you from among the nations."<Cite source="maimonides-mishneh-torah-touger" locator="Kings and Wars 11:1" /> Saadia argues that the long exile is partly punishment and partly trial, and that it cannot go on without end.<Cite source="saadia-emunot-ibn-tibbon" locator="VIII.1" note="Hebrew; our paraphrase" />

Three hopes should be kept distinct. The first is the messianic age, which Maimonides describes soberly. A king from David's line will renew the dynasty, build the Temple, gather the exiles and restore the Torah's observance.<Cite source="maimonides-mishneh-torah-touger" locator="Kings and Wars 11:1" /> He need not work miracles. Rather, "the world will continue according to its pattern," and Isaiah's wolf lying with the lamb is a parable of peace among nations.<Cite source="maimonides-mishneh-torah-touger" locator="Kings and Wars 12:1" /> As the Sages said, the only difference from the present age will be "the emancipation from our subjugation to the gentile kingdoms." Its purpose is freedom to pursue Torah and the knowledge of God.<Cite source="maimonides-mishneh-torah-touger" locator="Repentance 9:2; Kings and Wars 12:2" />

The second hope is the resurrection of the dead, and the third is the World to Come, which Maimonides calls "the ultimate of all reward and the final good." The messianic age belongs to this world; the final good lies beyond it.<Cite source="maimonides-mishneh-torah-touger" locator="Repentance 9:2" /> How these relate is treated under <QuestionLink question="after-death" worldview="judaism">after death</QuestionLink> and <QuestionLink question="final-end" worldview="judaism">final end</QuestionLink>.

Maimonides' account is a major one, not the only one. He himself notes that on the order of the final events "there is a controversy among" the Sages.<Cite source="maimonides-mishneh-torah-touger" locator="Kings and Wars 12:2" /> Other strands expect an apocalyptic war of Gog and Magog and a more dramatic transformation of the world.

## What this explains well

The view ties hope to real history rather than to escape from it, and it gives a public test for messianic claims.

## Christian response

The Christian shares this covenantal history and its prophets. The disagreement is over where history now stands. Christianity holds that the decisive turn has already come: Christ "has appeared once for all at the end of the ages."<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Hebrews 9:26" /> On this reading history now runs between a first coming and a second, and the prophets' promises of universal peace await that second stage. The Jewish reply is that the Messiah's tasks are public and historical, so their non-fulfilment shows that he has not come. That question is argued under <QuestionLink question="jesus" worldview="judaism">Jesus</QuestionLink>. Both sides agree that history is the arena of God's purpose.

## Pressure questions

- If the Messiah is recognized by visible success in history, how should the long delay be understood?
- Could the prophets' messianic promises be fulfilled in more than one stage?

## Deep dive

#### Recognizing the Messiah

Maimonides gives a practical test: a Davidic king who leads Israel in the Torah may be presumed to be the Messiah, and one who also rebuilds the Temple and gathers the exiles is confirmed as such; one who fails or is killed is not.<Cite source="maimonides-mishneh-torah-touger" locator="Kings and Wars 11:4" /> How he applies the test to Jesus is set out under <QuestionLink question="jesus" worldview="judaism">Jesus</QuestionLink>. His example is Rabbi Akiva, who hailed the rebel leader Bar Kozibah as the messianic king and asked him for no signs. When Bar Kozibah was killed, the Sages knew he was not the Messiah.<Cite source="maimonides-mishneh-torah-touger" locator="Kings and Wars 11:3" />

#### Calendars of redemption

The Talmud preserves a teaching that the world lasts six thousand years: "two thousand years are characterized by chaos; two thousand years are characterized by Torah," and two thousand belong to "the period of the coming of the Messiah."<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Sanhedrin 97a" /> Yet the same discussion curses those who calculate the end, because when their date passes they say he will not come at all. The right course is to wait.<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Sanhedrin 97b" /> One sage, Rabbi Hillel, even held that the messianic prophecies were fulfilled in King Hezekiah's day; Rav Yosef answered that later prophets still foretold the Messiah.<Cite source="talmud-bavli-koren" locator="Sanhedrin 99a" /> Maimonides follows the warning: one should not try to fix the time, but await the coming in general terms.<Cite source="maimonides-mishneh-torah-touger" locator="Kings and Wars 12:2" />

#### Saadia's certainty

Saadia argues from the prophets' promises and from God's justice that redemption is certain. He then argues at length that the prophets' promises were not fulfilled in the Second Temple period, so that the redemption they describe is still to come.<Cite source="saadia-emunot-ibn-tibbon" locator="VIII.1; VIII.8" note="Hebrew; our paraphrase" />
