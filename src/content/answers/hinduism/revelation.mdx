## The view

Hindu traditions do not usually picture revelation as a creator speaking to a prophet at a point in history. The central idea is *śruti*, "what is heard": the Veda, including the Upaniṣads, held to be eternal and seen or found by its seers (*ṛṣis*) rather than invented. Śaṅkara cites a hymn in which sacred speech is "found... dwelling in the ṛṣis." When the world is dissolved and renewed at the end of each great cycle, it returns with the same names and forms, so the Veda is never lost.<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra" locator="I.3.29–30 (pp. 211–213)" /> Revelation is therefore not a single event but an eternal word, found again in every cycle of the world.

Beneath it stands *smṛti*, "what is remembered" (the epics, the Gītā, the Purāṇas and the law books), which "depends on something else (viz. Scripture)."<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra" locator="I.3.28 (p. 203)" />

Why regard the Veda as a genuine disclosure? The schools answer differently (see <QuestionLink question="ultimate-authority" worldview="hinduism">ultimate authority</QuestionLink>). For Mīmāṃsā the Veda has no author, so there is no speaker whose faults could make it false. Śaṅkara reports the ground: "the eternity of the Veda is founded on the absence of the remembrance of an agent."<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra" locator="I.3.29 (p. 211)" /> For Nyāya it is reliable because its authors are reliable, and theists such as Udayana argued for an all-knowing author. Vedānta adds that Brahman can be known only from scripture, and Śaṅkara cites the Upaniṣad that the Vedas "have been breathed forth from that great Being."<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra" locator="I.1.3 (pp. 19–20)" /> For Advaita the scripture's knowledge becomes direct realization of Brahman.

A second kind of disclosure is divine descent (*avatāra*). In the Gītā, Kṛṣṇa says: "Whenever there is a decay of religion... then I manifest Myself," and "I am born in every age."<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="4.7–8 (p. 107)" /> Śaṅkara reads this as an appearance: the Lord seems to be born "through my own Maya, but not in reality."<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="4.6 (pp. 106–107)" /> Rāmānuja reads the descents as real but "divine, immaterial and unique."<Cite source="govindacharya-gita-ramanuja" locator="4.8–9 (pp. 141–142)" />

## What this explains well

The account frees revelation from dependence on a fallible human author or a historical accident, and it explains the extraordinary care with which the Veda was preserved.

## Christian response

The Christian agrees that the deepest truths lie beyond unaided perception and must be disclosed. But what shows that the Veda is such a disclosure? History bears on the question. Scholars describe the Ṛg Veda as composed "by a number of bards or bardic families, over a period of several hundred years," and preserved by priestly schools in a transmission that was "completely oral for the entire Vedic period and some considerable time afterwards."<Cite source="jamison-witzel-vedic-hinduism" locator="pp. 2–3, 6" />

That preservation is remarkable, but it shows only that what was composed was kept, not that it was uncomposed. Nor does dating by itself refute the claim that the seers found an eternal word. The issue is narrower: Mīmāṃsā's argument rests on the absence of a remembered author, and historical study supplies authors. Schools that ground the Veda in an all-knowing source face the question every lane faces: what identifies that source's speech?

There is also a contrast in kind. Christianity ties revelation to events that happened once, and Bavinck stresses that Christ's incarnation, death and resurrection cannot be repeated.<Cite source="bavinck-reformed-dogmatics-monergism-1" locator="Vol. 1, §10, PDF p. 376" /> A revelation re-found in every cycle cannot be refuted by history, but for that reason history cannot confirm it either.

## Pressure questions

- If the Vedic hymns bear the marks of composition by poets over centuries, what does the claim that they are authorless or eternal add, and how could it be tested?
- What would show that a particular claimed divine descent was genuine, rather than a story told about a revered figure?

## Deep dive

#### The eternal word and the renewed world

Śaṅkara cites a tradition that the Vedas, hidden at the end of an age, were recovered by the great seers through austerity.<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra" locator="I.3.29 (p. 211)" /> He argues that the world itself comes from the Vedic words. The creator "shaped in the beginning from the words of the Veda" the names and forms of things, he says, quoting Manu.<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra" locator="I.3.28 (pp. 203–204)" /> The words name eternal kinds, not particular individuals, so the periodic dissolution of the world does not threaten them. Each new creation is like waking from sleep: beings return "bearing the same names and forms" as before, in a cycle without beginning.<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra" locator="I.3.30 (pp. 212–215)" /> The Viṣṇu Purāṇa adds that in every third age Viṣṇu, as Vyāsa, divides "the Veda, which is properly but one," into four for the sake of weaker generations.<Cite source="wilson-vishnu-purana" locator="III.3 (p. 272)" />

#### Authorless or breathed forth

If the Veda is "breathed forth" from Brahman, can it also be authorless? Mīmāṃsā says it has no author, and Kumārila argued that a text with no speaker has no source of error.<Cite source="jha-slokavarttika" locator="Sūtra II, vv. 62–68 (pp. 30–31)" /> Śaṅkara's commentary offers two readings of the relevant sūtra: Brahman as the omniscient source of scripture, or scripture as the means by which Brahman is known.<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra" locator="I.1.3 (pp. 19–20)" /> His account of identical names and forms recurring in each world-cycle lets him hold both that the Veda issues from Brahman and that no one ever composed it. This is a difference between schools that Advaita harmonizes, not a contradiction.

#### What history can and cannot show

Jamison and Witzel tentatively place the Vedic period between about 1500 and 500 BCE and distinguish layers in the texts by linguistic development. They note that the Ṛg Veda survives essentially in one extremely well-preserved school, whose word-by-word analysis of the text (the *Padapāṭha*) was itself transmitted.<Cite source="jamison-witzel-vedic-hinduism" locator="pp. 2–6" /> These facts support faithful transmission. They do not decide whether the content was disclosed, which is a question about its source rather than its preservation.
