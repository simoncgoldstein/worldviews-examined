## The view

There is no single Hindu equivalent of Christian forensic guilt. Wrong action bears karmic consequences, but the schools differ over the self, bondage and the Lord. The Gītā diagnoses desire that veils wisdom; its remedy cannot be reduced to feeling less ashamed.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="3.36–43" />

For Śaṅkara, the decisive problem is ignorance of the Self. The Gītā's image of knowledge burning actions is interpreted as rendering them powerless, rather than literally destroying past events. Actions already producing the present body continue until their effects are exhausted; the liberating knowledge concerns those not yet bearing fruit.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="4.36–39, pp. 134–135" /> Ethical discipline prepares for knowledge rather than being made pointless by nonduality.

Rāmānuja offers a more directly personal account. Knowledge and devotion to the Lord remove the divine displeasure associated with sin. His commentary expressly limits non-clinging later sins to those arising from thoughtlessness, rather than deliberate misconduct incompatible with growing meditation.<Cite source="thibaut-ramanuja-sribhashya" locator="IV.1.13–15" /> The Gītā's promise to one taking refuge is interpreted through divine aid and freedom from impediments to the path.<Cite source="govindacharya-gita-ramanuja" locator="18.66, p. 565" /> These are distinct accounts of release; neither says that merely relabeling an injury makes it morally innocent.

## What this explains well

The traditions distinguish the persistence of an action's effects from the possibility of escape from bondage. Śaṅkara's qualification concerning already-fructifying karma prevents a claim that insight instantly changes every circumstance. Rāmānuja joins hope after wrongdoing to changed devotion: the formerly wicked devotee becomes righteous rather than receiving a warrant to continue evil.<Cite source="govindacharya-gita-ramanuja" locator="9.30–31" />

## Christian response

The Christian's question differs by school. Against Advaita, he argues that culpability before God requires more than removing mistaken self-identification. With theistic Vedānta, he asks why remission of the Lord's displeasure is sufficient. He holds that God forgives justly because Christ bore the guilt that is remitted (see <QuestionLink question="guilt" worldview="christianity">the Christian account of guilt</QuestionLink>).<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 3:23–26" /> Those are disagreements over ultimate reality and justice; they do not establish that Hindu practitioners ignore moral consequences or that every school teaches impersonal self-rescue.

## Pressure questions

- For Advaita, what becomes of responsibility for another person's injury at the ultimate level?
- For theistic Vedānta, how does divine release address the wrong as well as its binding consequences?

## Deep dive

#### The fire image has a qualification

Śaṅkara explains the Gītā's fire of knowledge through the potency of actions. Knowledge does not travel back through time and prevent their occurrence. It makes actions unable to produce further binding effects, with the stated exception of those already sustaining the present embodied life.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="4.37, commentary, pp. 134–135" /> That distinction matters for someone who still suffers after insight: continuing bodily life is not by itself evidence that knowledge has failed.

Nor should liberation's transcendence of ordinary agency be turned into a public exemption from ethical training. The diagnosis concerns the beginningless confusion of Self with body and mind, not a convenient theory that every prohibited deed is secretly good.<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra" locator="I.1.1, intro., pp. 3–9" /> Whether that ultimate account adequately preserves personal culpability is a legitimate Christian question, but it needs the distinction between conventional action and ultimate Self to be intelligible.

#### Sin in a personal relation

Rāmānuja reads removal of karmic power through the Lord's pleased or displeased relation to the devotee. His interpretation of knowledge is therefore not Śaṅkara's nondual discovery under a different vocabulary. It concerns knowledge of a supremely dear personal object and divine favor.<Cite source="thibaut-ramanuja-sribhashya" locator="IV.1.13" /> Deliberate evil is explicitly excluded from the claim that later sin does not cling. This keeps the promise from becoming a strategy for preserving misconduct.

The Gītā's refuge language is consequently significant for comparison with Christianity, yet the two systems do not thereby teach the same redemption. God-dependent release and Reformed satisfaction have different accounts of justice and the self. The distinction is pursued in <QuestionLink question="self-salvation" worldview="hinduism">the liberation anchor</QuestionLink>. The continuation of karma and personhood belongs to <QuestionLink question="after-death" worldview="hinduism">after death</QuestionLink>; liberation's final character to <QuestionLink question="final-end" worldview="hinduism">final destiny</QuestionLink>. This answer does not claim that all Hindu expiation rites or popular practices fit one Vedānta formula.
