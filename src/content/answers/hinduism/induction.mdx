## The view

Indian logicians held that inference reaches what we cannot see through general rules that hold without exception. Every inference depends on such a rule, a *vyāpti* or "pervasion," such as "wherever there is smoke there is fire" (see <QuestionLink question="logic-binding" worldview="hinduism">logic</QuestionLink>). How is such a rule known? The materialist Cārvāka school said it cannot be. Its argument survives in a fourteenth-century survey by the Vedāntin Mādhava, who reports it in order to reject it.<Cite source="cowell-gough-sarvadarsanasamgraha" locator="ch. I (pp. 5–9)" />

The Cārvāka case runs like this. Perception reaches only present cases. Inference cannot establish the rule, because it would need another general rule, "and so on," in "an ad infinitum retrogression." Testimony fails similarly. And a true pervasion must hold with no hidden condition, yet the absence of every hidden condition can never be known. So inference is caught in "the thunderbolt-like fallacy of reasoning in a circle." When an inference does succeed, the success is "accidental just like the coincidence of effects observed in the employment of gems, charms, drugs."<Cite source="cowell-gough-sarvadarsanasamgraha" locator="ch. I (pp. 5–9)" />

The Nyāya logicians replied with method. A pervasion is established by observing the sign together with what it signals, knowing of no case where the sign occurs without it, and watching for defeaters such as a counterexample.<Cite source="sep-epistemology-india" locator="§5" /> Hidden conditions must be ruled out: fire produces smoke only when the fuel is wet, so "fire, therefore smoke" fails while "smoke, therefore fire" holds.<Cite source="cowell-gough-sarvadarsanasamgraha" locator="ch. I, translators' note (pp. 5–9)" /> Where two claims conflict, *tarka*, reasoning from the absurd consequences of the alternative, helps settle the matter.<Cite source="sep-epistemology-india" locator="§8" />

The result was not certainty but fallibilism: an inference can turn out to rest on a fallacy, but the very idea of a fallacy presupposes genuine reasons.<Cite source="sep-epistemology-india" locator="§2" />

This debate is an Indian parallel to Hume's problem, not a translation of it. It is framed in its own terms: pervasion, hidden conditions and the means of knowledge.

## What this explains well

The Nyāya response gives a workable method much like empirical science: observe widely, look for exceptions, test hidden conditions. Its fallibilism is honest.

## Christian response

The Christian agrees with Nyāya's method. Its limit is that a method for detecting exceptions does not explain why unobserved cases should conform at all. The Cārvāka's challenge remains: why should a pervasion that has held so far go on holding? Nyāya's theistic wing, through Udayana, holds that the world is ordered by an intelligent maker,<Cite source="cowell-kusumanjali" locator="V.1" /> and the Vedic idea of cosmic order could offer a ground (see <QuestionLink question="order" worldview="hinduism">order</QuestionLink>). But the logicians' reply to the Cārvāka is methodological, not providential. That is explanatory incompleteness, not error: the method is sound, but the ground of the regularity it relies on is left open.

## Pressure questions

- If no counterexample has been observed, what makes it likely that none exists?
- Can the logician answer the materialist without relying on the very general rules in question?

## Deep dive

#### Hidden conditions

The technical term for a hidden condition is *upādhi*. The translators' note gives the standard case: in the faulty inference "the mountain has smoke because it has fire," wet fuel is the condition that must be added, and in a true pervasion there is no such condition.<Cite source="cowell-gough-sarvadarsanasamgraha" locator="ch. I, translators' note (pp. 5–9)" /> The Cārvāka argue that the absence of every possible condition cannot be established without another inference, which brings back the regress.

#### The pragmatic reply

The logicians also answered pragmatically. We could not act without inference, and the sceptic himself relies on general rules whenever he uses words with repeatable meanings, a point SEP attributes to the Nyāya systematizer Gaṅgeśa.<Cite source="sep-epistemology-india" locator="§2" />

#### The Buddhist answer in the same text

Mādhava's survey continues with a Buddhist reply to the Cārvāka: pervasion is known not by mere observation of instances but from relations of identity and cause and effect, with causation established by a fivefold test of perceiving and not perceiving cause and effect.<Cite source="cowell-gough-sarvadarsanasamgraha" locator="ch. II (pp. 12–13)" /> That reply is examined under <QuestionLink question="induction" worldview="buddhism">the Buddhist answer</QuestionLink>.

#### Śaṅkara's opponent

In the *Brahma Sūtra* commentary, an opponent defends reasoning by pointing out that people act on the assumption that past, present and future are uniform. Śaṅkara accepts this for worldly matters but denies that reasoning has the same foundation in matters known only from scripture.<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra" locator="II.1.11 (pp. 314–317)" /> His concern is with the limits of reasoning about the supersensible, not with the problem of induction as such.
