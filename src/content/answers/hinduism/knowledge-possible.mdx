## The view

In Hindu thought we know the world through reliable "means of right knowledge" (*pramāṇas*): above all perception, inference and the word of trustworthy people. The schools made these means a subject of careful study and disagreed about how many there are. The Cārvāka materialists accept perception alone. Buddhists accept perception and inference. The Nyāya school adds comparison and testimony, and Mīmāṃsā and Vedānta add further means.<Cite source="vidyabhusana-nyaya-sutras" locator="1.1.3, with note (pp. 2–3)" />

Nyāya, the school of logic, gives the classic definitions. Perception is knowledge that arises from contact between a sense and its object, and it must be "non-erratic," which rules out the shimmer of summer heat that looks like water. Inference is "preceded by perception," as when we infer fire from smoke. Testimony is "the instructive assertion of a reliable person," who may be a sage, an Ārya or an outsider, provided they know the matter and want to share it.<Cite source="vidyabhusana-nyaya-sutras" locator="1.1.4–7 (pp. 3–4)" /> The world is real, the self is a real knower, and the means of knowledge bring the two into contact.

Error is real but correctable: a cognition stands until something, such as a counterexample, defeats it.<Cite source="sep-epistemology-india" locator="§§5, 8" /> The Mīmāṃsā school makes this a principle. In Kumārila's words, "authoritativeness is inherent in all Means of Right Notion." A cognition is overturned only by a later cognition that contradicts it or by a defect discovered in its source.<Cite source="jha-slokavarttika" locator="Sūtra II, vv. 47–61 (pp. 28–30)" />

Advaita Vedānta accepts all of this, but at one level only. Śaṅkara grants that perception, inference and even scripture work in ordinary life. Yet he says that they "have for their object that which is dependent on Nescience," because they need a knower who identifies the self with body and senses.<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra" locator="Introduction (pp. 3–9)" /> Final knowledge of Brahman is not one more object known by a means of knowledge. It is the realization that ends the division between knower and known, as <QuestionLink question="ultimate-reality" worldview="hinduism">the account of ultimate reality</QuestionLink> explains. An Upaniṣad already ranks even the Vedas as "lower knowledge," beside "the higher knowledge... by which the Indestructible (Brahman) is apprehended."<Cite source="muller-upanishads-2" locator="Muṇḍaka I.1.4–5 (pp. 27–28)" />

The theistic Vedāntins, Rāmānuja and Madhva, reject this two-level picture. For them the ordinary means of knowledge present a world that is ultimately real.<Cite source="iep-madhva" locator="§3" />

## What this explains well

These schools developed one of the world's most careful theories of knowledge, with defined sources, catalogued errors and procedures for correction. Nyāya's realism and its account of testimony fit ordinary experience well. Advaita explains why everyday knowledge works while pointing to a knowledge of another kind.

## Christian response

Nyāya's realism and its account of reliable testimony are close to Christian commitments. The Christian question is what grounds the fit between knower and world. Nyāya theists such as Udayana can appeal to a divine maker of the world;<Cite source="cowell-kusumanjali" locator="V.1" /> for schools without one, the fit is simply given. Advaita faces a sharper problem. If the very duality of knower and known belongs to ignorance, it is hard to see how any means of knowledge, scripture included, can lead out of ignorance. Advaitins reply that scripture removes ignorance without making Brahman an object. That reply is coherent within Advaita, but Rāmānuja's objection<Cite source="iep-ramanuja" locator="Argument from Epistemology" /> exposes a real internal tension, though not a contradiction.

## Pressure questions

- If the distinction between knower and known belongs to ignorance, what makes the realization "I am Brahman" trustworthy rather than one more cognition within ignorance?
- If every cognition is valid until defeated, what grounds that default trust?

## Deep dive

#### Intrinsic or extrinsic validity

The schools disagree about how we know that we know. Mīmāṃsā holds that a cognition carries its own certification: it is accepted unless something defeats it. Nyāya holds that certification is extrinsic: knowing that a cognition is true needs a further cognition, for instance one confirmed by successful action.<Cite source="sep-epistemology-india" locator="§3" /> This dispute matters for authority, because Mīmāṃsā uses intrinsic validity to defend the Veda (see <QuestionLink question="ultimate-authority" worldview="hinduism">the ultimate authority for truth</QuestionLink>).

#### The lamp

If each means of knowledge needed another to validate it, there would be an endless regress. Nyāya answers that a lamp lights up other things and is itself seen: the means of knowledge establish their objects and, in turn, one another.<Cite source="vidyabhusana-nyaya-sutras" locator="2.1.19 (pp. 27–28)" /> Nāgārjuna's Buddhist critique of this reply is described in <QuestionLink question="knowledge-possible" worldview="buddhism">the Buddhist answer</QuestionLink>.

#### Śaṅkara on ordinary knowing

Śaṅkara points out that in practice humans use the means of knowledge much as animals do. A cow runs from a man with a raised stick and walks up to a man holding fresh grass. People who boast of greater intelligence act on their perceptions in the same way.<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra" locator="Introduction (pp. 3–9)" /> His point is not that knowledge is worthless. It is that ordinary knowing belongs to the practical, embodied level, which Brahman-knowledge transcends.

#### Realist Vedānta

Rāmānuja argues that if all cognition involved error, as Advaita implies, the cognition that all cognition errs would itself be in error.<Cite source="iep-ramanuja" locator="Argument from Epistemology" /> He adds that every means of knowledge, even the simplest perception, presents an object with distinguishing features. There is no knowledge of a featureless reality.<Cite source="thibaut-ramanuja-sribhashya" locator="I.1.1 (pp. 40–41)" /> Madhva calls his system the realist viewpoint. Perception and inference must be valid in their own right, and the world they present ultimately real, since even the Vedas reach us only through hearing and inference.<Cite source="iep-madhva" locator="introduction; §3" />

#### The materialist dissent

The Cārvāka school, which accepted perception alone, denied that inference can ever be secure. Its argument and the orthodox replies are taken up under <QuestionLink question="induction" worldview="hinduism">induction</QuestionLink>.
