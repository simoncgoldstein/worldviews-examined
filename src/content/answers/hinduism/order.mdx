## The view

The oldest Hindu scripture already speaks of order at the foundation of the world. A Ṛgveda hymn says that "eternal Law and Truth" were born first, and from them day and night and the sea.<Cite source="griffith-rigveda-4" locator="X.190" /> The word translated "Law" is *ṛta*, the right order of things. It covers the regular course of the heavens, the correct performance of ritual and truthful, right action. Later thought speaks of *dharma* in a similar range of senses. These are not "laws of nature" in the modern sense. They are one order that is natural and moral at once.

The Upaniṣads ground this order in Brahman. "By the command of that Imperishable," the sage Yājñavalkya teaches, "sun and moon stand apart," heaven and earth stand apart, and the seasons and years keep their place.<Cite source="muller-upanishads-2" locator="Bṛhadāraṇyaka III.8.9" /> Brahman is the "inner ruler" within every being.<Cite source="muller-upanishads-2" locator="Bṛhadāraṇyaka III.7" />

The moral side of this order is karma. Beings receive the fruits of their own actions, life after life. The Vedānta commentators hold that the Lord dispenses these fruits, and that his dispensation is "relative to the Karmas" of beings, so he is neither partial nor cruel.<Cite source="subbarau-madhva-brahmasutra" locator="II.1.35, p. 104" />

Śaṅkara argues that order points to intelligence. A non-intelligent first cause, like the primal matter of the Sāṃkhya school, could not account for "the orderly arrangement" of the world.<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra" locator="II.2.1, pp. 363–364" /> Rāmānuja says the Lord's will alone is "the Cause of existence, the Prop of existences, and What establishes an order for existences."<Cite source="govindacharya-gita-ramanuja" locator="9.5–6, pp. 291–292" />

## What this explains well

The view unites the order of nature and the order of morality, so that the same reality accounts for planets and for justice. It explains order by intelligence rather than by blind matter. And karma gives a reason for the inequalities of life without charging God with arbitrariness.

## Christian response

The Christian shares Śaṅkara's argument that blind matter cannot account for the world's order, and Rāmānuja's conviction that a personal will upholds it. The difference concerns karma. In the Vedānta picture the Lord dispenses according to beings' past deeds, through a beginningless series of lives. Reformed Christianity holds that God governs all things, including judgment and mercy, by his own wise and holy will.<Cite source="wcf" locator="5.1" /> For Advaita the order belongs to the empirical level, real until liberating knowledge. These are theological disagreements. The Christian's question is whether karma functions as a law the Lord must follow, or is itself his will.

## Pressure questions

- If the Lord's dispensation always follows karma, is karma a law above him or simply his own will?
- In Advaita, if order belongs to the empirical world, what grounds its reliability?

## Deep dive

#### Ṛta and the Vedic world

The *ṛta* of the Vedic hymns is the pattern by which dawns return, rivers flow and sacrifices succeed. The hymn on creation places "eternal Law and Truth," *ṛta* and *satya*, at the start of cosmic history, born from kindled fervour, before day and night.<Cite source="griffith-rigveda-4" locator="X.190" /> Translators sometimes write "law," but the concept is broader and more religious than a scientific law.

#### Order and the inference to God

The Nyāya philosopher Udayana argued that the joining of atoms at the start of each world-cycle is an action, so it requires "the volition of an intelligent being." The world, he added, is kept from falling by a being whose will supports it, as a bird holds a stick in the air.<Cite source="cowell-kusumanjali" locator="V.1, pp. 64–65" /> The Vedānta schools did not accept that inference proves God. Rāmānuja argued that Brahman is known from scripture alone.<Cite source="thibaut-ramanuja-sribhashya" locator="I.1.3, pp. 161–171" /> Śaṅkara's own argument against the Sāṃkhya is an argument about which cause scripture teaches, not a free-standing proof.<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra" locator="II.2.1, pp. 363–364" />

#### The air in empty space

Rāmānuja illustrates the Lord's support with an image from the Gītā. As the mighty air moves everywhere yet rests in space, so all beings rest in the Lord. The air, he comments, "is so held in the propless vault of space by My power."<Cite source="govindacharya-gita-ramanuja" locator="9.5–6, pp. 291–292" /> For him the order of nature is the continual work of a personal will.
