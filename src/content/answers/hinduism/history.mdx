## The view

Hindu traditions measure time in cycles too vast to picture. In the Bhagavad Gītā, the creator Brahmā's day and night each last "a thousand yugas"; at his dawn all things come forth from the unmanifest, and at nightfall they dissolve into it.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="8.17–18 (pp. 208–209)" /> "All beings... go into My Prakriti at the end of a kalpa. I send them forth again at the beginning."<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="9.7 (p. 220)" /> The process has no first beginning (see <QuestionLink question="something-rather-than-nothing" worldview="hinduism">why there is something</QuestionLink>).

Within each great cycle, history runs downhill through four ages. In the first, the Kṛta, "Dharma is four-footed and entire, and (so is) Truth." In each later age it loses a foot, as theft, falsehood and fraud spread and life shortens, down to the dark Kali age.<Cite source="buhler-manu" locator="I.81–83" />

History is not left to itself. Viṣṇu acts in every age, and at the end of the Kali age he will come as Kalki, destroy the wicked and "reestablish righteousness upon earth"; then "the Krita age shall return."<Cite source="wilson-vishnu-purana" locator="IV.24 (p. 484)" /> Kalki renews the cycle; he does not end it. The Gītā's divine descents, born "in every age" to protect the good, follow the same pattern (see <QuestionLink question="revelation" worldview="hinduism">revelation</QuestionLink>).

History is therefore meaningful and morally ordered, but it has no final consummation; the goal lies outside the round. Śaṅkara reads the Gītā's account of beings born "again and again" as a lesson in detachment from saṃsāra,<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="8.19 (p. 209)" /> and the Lord promises that "on reaching Me... there is no rebirth."<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="8.16 (p. 208)" /> Advaita sees the cycles as the field of ignorance, though real for practical life; the theistic schools see them as the Lord's real governance. Both locate the final good in liberation (see <QuestionLink question="final-end" worldview="hinduism">final end</QuestionLink>).

## What this explains well

The view takes moral decline seriously without despair, since every dark age is followed by renewal, and it keeps divine action present in every age.

## Christian response

The Christian shares the conviction that history is morally governed and that God acts to restore righteousness. The difference is between repetition and a once-for-all act. Augustine pressed a precise point against the ancient cycle theories: if a liberated soul "passes to bliss, and leaves miseries forever, then there happens in time a new thing which time shall not end. Why not, then, the world also?"<Cite source="augustine-city-of-god" locator="XII.13" /> Hindu traditions agree that liberation is final. The Christian asks why a final good should be possible for souls but not for the world, and claims that the world too will be renewed rather than recycled (see <QuestionLink question="history" worldview="christianity">the Christian answer</QuestionLink>).

## Pressure questions

- If every age is restored only to decline again, what do the cycles themselves finally accomplish?
- If liberation is escape from history, does anything done within history have lasting worth beyond an individual's release?

## Deep dive

#### Ages, days and lives of Brahmā

Manu gives the scale. The four ages last four, three, two and one thousand divine years, each with its twilights; twelve thousand divine years make an age of the gods, and a thousand such ages make one day of Brahmā.<Cite source="buhler-manu" locator="I.68–72" /> Each age has its own duties: austerity in the Kṛta, knowledge in the Tretā, sacrifice in the Dvāpara and liberality alone in the Kali.<Cite source="buhler-manu" locator="I.85–86" />

#### Kalki and the return of the Kṛta age

The Viṣṇu Purāṇa describes the end of the Kali age as a time of universal decay. Then a portion of the divine being will be born as Kalki in the village of Sambhala, destroy "all the Mlechchhas and thieves," and restore righteousness. Those living at the end of the age will be awakened, "pellucid as crystal," and become the seeds of a race that follows the laws of the Kṛta age.<Cite source="wilson-vishnu-purana" locator="IV.24 (p. 484)" /> Summing up Viṣṇu's work through the four ages, the same text says that the universal spirit "preserves, creates, and at last destroys, all the world."<Cite source="wilson-vishnu-purana" locator="III.2 (p. 271)" />

#### Not meaningless, not final

Śaṅkara insists that the world of each new cycle repeats the same names and forms, and that saṃsāra is without beginning.<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra" locator="I.3.30 (pp. 212–215)" /> That does not make history illusory for practical purposes: actions bear fruit, dharma declines and is restored, and the Lord's descents are purposeful. What the cycles lack is an end in which history itself is fulfilled. Fulfilment belongs to the soul that leaves them.
