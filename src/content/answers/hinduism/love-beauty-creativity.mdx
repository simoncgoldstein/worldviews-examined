## The view

The organizing principle is that love and beauty find their source in the divine. In the Gītā, Krishna says that whatever is glorious, prosperous or mighty springs from a portion of his splendour.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="10.41" /> The Taittirīya Upaniṣad calls Brahman "flavour" (*rasa*): by tasting it a person becomes blissful.<Cite source="muller-upanishads-2" locator="Taittirīya II.7, pp. 58–59" />

Love's highest form is devotion (*bhakti*). The Lord accepts a leaf, flower, fruit or water offered with love.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="9.26" /> Rāmānuja reads this as impassioned love that the Lord himself enjoys,<Cite source="govindacharya-gita-ramanuja" locator="9.26" /> and teaches that the Lord chooses his lover and generates in him the love by which he is reached.<Cite source="govindacharya-gita-ramanuja" locator="8.14, pp. 272–273" /> Caitanya's Gauḍīya tradition gives that love a range of human forms. The devotee's love for Krishna takes five chief forms, each called a *rasa*: peaceful reverence, service, friendship, a parent's affection and the love of lovers, each containing what the one before it has, so that the last is the fullest.<Cite source="sarkar-chaitanya-charitamrita" locator="ch. XVII, pp. 241–244" />

Human loves are dear for a deeper reason. Yājñavalkya teaches that a husband, wife or son is dear not for their own sake but "for the love of the Self."<Cite source="muller-upanishads-2" locator="Bṛhadāraṇyaka II.4.5, pp. 109–110" /> Advaita reads this as pointing to the one Self in all.

Classical Indian aesthetics gives its own account of art. From Bharata's treatise on drama onward, the purpose of a play, and later of a poem, is to evoke *rasa*, the "taste" of an emotion. Abhinavagupta, the Kashmiri philosopher of about 1000 whose account later writers adopted, held that rasa is neither the hero's feeling nor the actor's. The drama awakens an emotion latent in the responsive spectator and frees it from reference to anyone's private interests, so that he relishes it as no longer merely his own. That is why sorrow or terror on the stage can still be relished.<Cite source="de-sanskrit-poetics-2" locator="pp. 159, 164–169" /> Art, on this view, creates delight by turning emotion into something contemplated rather than suffered.

## What this explains well

The account explains why love and beauty feel like glimpses of something greater, and it gives devotion an intimate, reciprocal character. Rasa theory explains why art can delight even when it portrays grief.

## Christian response

The Christian sees in Rāmānuja's teaching that the Lord initiates love a striking parallel to grace.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="1 John 4:10, 19" /> Gauḍīya devotion, which makes love for a personal Lord the soul's eternal end, stands nearer still; the difference there concerns who the Lord is and how he is known. With Advaita the question is whether love between persons survives when individuality is not ultimate. That is a theological disagreement about ultimate reality.

## Pressure questions

- If a spouse is dear "for the love of the Self," is the spouse loved for their own sake?
- Does Rāmānuja's divinely initiated love leave room for the devotee's free response?

## Deep dive

#### Desire and its limits

The Gītā calls the Lord "desire unopposed to dharma," but Śaṅkara restricts this to desire for what bare bodily sustenance requires.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="7.11, commentary" /> The householder's legitimate pleasure (*kāma*) stands within dharma, yet Advaita treats it as something to be transcended.

#### Two readings of love

For Advaita, love's deepest meaning is recognition: what is loved in another is the one Self, so that love points beyond the distinction of lover and beloved.<Cite source="muller-upanishads-2" locator="Bṛhadāraṇyaka IV.5.6, p. 182" /> For Rāmānuja, love is an eternal relation between the soul and the Lord, in which the Lord delights in his devotee's offering.<Cite source="govindacharya-gita-ramanuja" locator="9.26" /> The difference reaches the question whether love between persons is ultimate.

#### Three uses of rasa

The word *rasa* does three different jobs in this answer. The Taittirīya Upaniṣad's "flavour" names Brahman as the bliss a person tastes; it is a statement about ultimate reality, not a theory of art. The rasa of drama and poetry is aesthetic relish. Bhaṭṭa Nāyaka, an earlier theorist, compared that relish to the bliss of contemplating Brahman, while keeping the aesthetic and the philosophic attitudes distinct.<Cite source="de-sanskrit-poetics-2" locator="pp. 156–159" /> The devotional rasas are a third use. Rūpa Gosvāmin, the follower to whom the Gauḍīya biography has Caitanya teach the five rasas, recast the theory so that the dominant emotion is love of Krishna and the one who relishes it is the devotee, not the literary connoisseur.<Cite source="de-sanskrit-poetics-2" locator="pp. 335–336" /><Cite source="sarkar-chaitanya-charitamrita" locator="ch. XVII, pp. 241–244" /> Later thinkers connect the three, but sharing a word does not make them one doctrine.
