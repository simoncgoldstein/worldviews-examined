## The view

For everyday matters, perception and inference are enough. But what ought to be done (*dharma*) and what ultimate reality is (Brahman) lie beyond them. For these the orthodox schools give final authority to *śruti*, the revealed Veda, including the Upaniṣads. The Bhagavad Gītā says, "the scripture is thy authority in deciding as to what ought to be done and what ought not to be done."<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="16.24 (p. 383)" /> The law book of Manu holds that revelation and tradition must not be called in question, and that a person who scorns them by relying on the art of argument is to be cast out as an unbeliever.<Cite source="buhler-manu" locator="II.10–11 (pp. 30–31)" />

Why should the Veda have this authority? Here the schools divide. For Mīmāṃsā, the Veda has no author at all. Faults in testimony come from the speaker; the Veda has no speaker, so it has no such faults.<Cite source="jha-slokavarttika" locator="Sūtra II, vv. 62–68 (pp. 30–31)" /> For Nyāya, the Veda is authoritative for the opposite reason: "The Veda is reliable like the spell and medical science, because of the reliability of their authors."<Cite source="vidyabhusana-nyaya-sutras" locator="2.1.69 (pp. 42–43)" />

Reason serves scripture where scripture speaks. The Vedānta schools agree that Brahman cannot be known by perception or by independent inference, only from the Upaniṣads.<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra" locator="I.1.2 (pp. 17–18)" /><Cite source="thibaut-ramanuja-sribhashya" locator="I.1.3 (pp. 161–174)" /><Cite source="subbarau-madhva-brahmasutra" locator="I.1.3 (pp. 11–12)" /> Śaṅkara explains why: human thought is "altogether unfettered," and "reasoning which disregards the holy texts and rests on individual opinion only has no proper foundation," since every clever argument is overturned by a cleverer one.<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra" locator="II.1.11 (pp. 314–317)" /> Reasoning is welcome when it supports the texts and helps understand them.

Yet the schools read the same texts in incompatible ways, as non-dual Brahman (Śaṅkara), a personal God with the world as his body (Rāmānuja), or God eternally distinct from souls and matter (Madhva; see <QuestionLink question="ultimate-reality" worldview="hinduism">ultimate reality</QuestionLink>). No school-neutral court decides between them.

Advaita adds a final twist. Even the Vedas are "lower knowledge" compared with the knowledge "by which the Indestructible (Brahman) is apprehended."<Cite source="muller-upanishads-2" locator="Muṇḍaka I.1.4–5 (pp. 27–28)" /> Scripture removes ignorance, and its fruit is direct realization of Brahman, which then needs no further authority.<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra" locator="I.1.2 (pp. 17–18)" /> The theistic Vedāntins keep scripture as the permanent norm.

## What this explains well

The account recognizes that reason cannot settle questions of duty and ultimate reality, where human arguments go round in circles. It gives the tradition a stable textual centre while allowing serious philosophical disagreement about it.

## Christian response

The Christian agrees that reason alone cannot reach God's purposes and that revelation is needed. The Nyāya view, that scripture is reliable because of who stands behind it, is close to the Reformed claim that Scripture's authority rests wholly on God as its author.<Cite source="wcf" locator="1.4" /> Mīmāṃsā's authorless Veda grounds authority in the opposite way, by the absence of any speaker.

Disagreement among the schools refutes none of them, since Christians disagree about Scripture too. The sharper question is Advaita's. If the fruit of scripture is a realization that then needs no further authority, what distinguishes a genuine realization of Brahman from a powerful conviction? Advaita answers that the realization arises from scripture itself and validates itself. The Christian appeal to the Spirit's inward witness faces a similar question. The Reformed answer is that the Spirit adds nothing to a public text that anyone can examine, so the Christian asks whether Advaita's realization keeps a comparable public check. That is a question about how claims are tested, not a charge of incoherence.

## Pressure questions

- If the Veda has no author, what makes its words convey intended meaning, and who stands behind their truth?
- If scripture yields at last to realization, what tests a claimed realization?
- Advaita reads the texts that teach difference as true only from the empirical standpoint; Rāmānuja reads the texts of identity, such as "Thou art that," as naming the Lord who has souls for his body.<Cite source="thibaut-ramanuja-sribhashya" locator="I.1.13, pp. 228–229" /> What principle decides which set of texts governs the reading of the other? Christian appeals to divine accommodation face a similar test.

## Deep dive

#### Authorless or authored

Kumārila argues that, if testimony is valid unless faults are found, and faults belong to speakers, then a Veda without a speaker cannot be faulted.<Cite source="jha-slokavarttika" locator="Sūtra II, vv. 47–68 (pp. 28–31)" /> This depends on Mīmāṃsā's view that words and their meanings are eternal. Nyāya rejects that view. Its commentator explains that the Veda's seers were reliable because they had direct insight into truths, kindness toward living beings and the wish to share what they knew.<Cite source="vidyabhusana-nyaya-sutras" locator="2.1.69 (pp. 42–43)" /> The theist Udayana went further and inferred an omniscient author of the Veda.<Cite source="cowell-kusumanjali" locator="V.1" />

#### What scripture covers

The Gītā and Mīmāṃsā speak of scripture's authority over "what ought to be done."<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="16.23–24 (pp. 382–383)" /> The Vedānta schools extend that authority to knowledge of Brahman. They also differ over the canon. Madhva counts the Mahābhārata and the original Rāmāyaṇa among the authoritative scriptures, alongside the four Vedas.<Cite source="subbarau-madhva-brahmasutra" locator="I.1.3 (pp. 11–12)" />

#### Reasoning in its place

Śaṅkara points out that the philosophers who founded schools by reasoning alone, such as Kapila and Kaṇāda, contradict each other, so reasoning has no secure foundation in matters known only from scripture.<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra" locator="II.1.11 (pp. 314–317)" /> Rāmānuja argues at length that neither perception nor inference can establish the Lord. Brahman is known from scripture alone.<Cite source="thibaut-ramanuja-sribhashya" locator="I.1.3 (pp. 161–174)" />

#### Self-validating knowledge

Mīmāṃsā's principle that cognitions are valid in themselves (see <QuestionLink question="knowledge-possible" worldview="hinduism">why we can know anything</QuestionLink>) is a deliberate form of self-certification: the Veda needs no outside guarantor, because nothing has shown it false. This is the Hindu parallel to the Reformed claim that Scripture is self-attesting, with the difference that one appeals to the absence of a speaker and the other to the presence of God.
