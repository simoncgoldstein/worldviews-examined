## The view

The Upaniṣads speak of Brahman in two ways. Sometimes the language is personal. Brahman "wished, may I be many, may I grow forth," and sent forth all that is.<Cite source="muller-upanishads-2" locator="Taittirīya II.6, p. 58" /> It is the inner ruler of every being,<Cite source="muller-upanishads-2" locator="Bṛhadāraṇyaka III.7" /> "the eternal among eternals, the thinker among thinkers, who, though one, fulfils the desires of many."<Cite source="muller-upanishads-2" locator="Śvetāśvatara VI.13" /> Sometimes the language refuses every description. The Self "is to be described by No, no! He is incomprehensible, for he cannot be comprehended."<Cite source="muller-upanishads-2" locator="Bṛhadāraṇyaka III.9.26, p. 149" />

The three Vedānta schools resolve this differently, and no single yes-or-no answer covers them.

**Advaita** (Śaṅkara) distinguishes Brahman "with qualities" (*saguṇa*) from Brahman "without qualities" (*nirguṇa*). The personal Lord, Īśvara, is omniscient and omnipotent, the cause of the world, and the right object of worship. He is real at the level of ordinary experience.<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra" locator="I.1.2, pp. 15–16" /> The highest truth, though, is Brahman beyond all qualities, and personal distinctions belong to the level that liberating knowledge transcends.<Cite source="sep-shankara" locator="§2.3" />

**Viśiṣṭādvaita** (Rāmānuja) holds that Brahman simply *is* "that highest Person," opposed to all evil, possessing "infinite auspicious qualities, such as knowledge, blessedness," omniscient, omnipotent and "supremely merciful," from whom the world's creation, subsistence and reabsorption proceed.<Cite source="thibaut-ramanuja-sribhashya" locator="I.1.2, pp. 156–161" />

**Dvaita** (Madhva) agrees that the supreme reality is personal: Viṣṇu, the one independent being, distinct from every soul.<Cite source="subbarau-madhva-brahmasutra" locator="I.1.17, pp. 21–22" />

Beyond these three schools the answer is also yes. **Gauḍīya Vaiṣṇavism** reverses Advaita's order: Krishna is "God Himself," and the impersonal Brahman is how he appears to knowledge, a diffuse radiance, as the sun appears as a mass of light.<Cite source="sarkar-chaitanya-charitamrita" locator="ch. XVIII, p. 255" /> **Śaiva Siddhānta**, the Tamil Śaiva theology, gives the same answer with Śiva as the Lord. Its root text, Meykaṇḍār's *Śivajñānabodham* (about 1200), argues that the world, which arises, persists and perishes, must have Hara (Śiva) as its first cause. The Lord is one with souls, different from them, and both at once, and he governs their births according to their deeds. He comes to the ripened soul as its teacher, and knowledge and undying love unite it to his feet.<Cite source="nallaswami-sivajnanabodham" locator="sūtras 1–2, 8, 11" /> As the tradition expounds it, the Lord, souls and their bonds are all without beginning, and grace is the Lord's own eternal power.<Cite source="nallaswami-sivajnanabodham" locator="translator's notes to sūtras 3, 5, 6" />

## What this explains well

The tradition holds together intense personal devotion and a strong sense that the ultimate exceeds every concept. It explains why worship, grace and love of God matter in Hindu life, and why its sages warn against confining God to human categories.

## Christian response

With Rāmānuja and Madhva, and with Gauḍīya and Śaiva Siddhānta theology, the Christian shares the claim that the supreme reality is a personal Lord who knows and loves. The disagreements concern creation and the soul: the world as God's body (Rāmānuja) and souls without beginning (in each of these schools). With Advaita the disagreement is sharper. The Christian holds that personhood belongs to the highest reality, because God is personal in himself as Father, Son and Spirit.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="John 17:24" /> Advaita holds that personal distinctions are finally transcended. The Christian asks a question of explanatory adequacy: can a highest reality beyond knowing, willing and loving ground the knowing and loving persons Advaita itself honours at the conventional level? Advaita answers with its two levels of truth.

## Pressure questions

- In Advaita, if the personal Lord belongs to the level of appearance, is devotion to him finally a stage to be left behind?
- For Rāmānuja and Madhva, if souls are as beginningless as the Lord, what makes him, and not them, the one on whom everything depends?

## Deep dive

#### "No, no"

The phrase *neti neti*, "not thus, not thus," comes from the Bṛhadāraṇyaka. After describing forms of Brahman, the text says: "Next follows the teaching (of Brahman) by No, no!"<Cite source="muller-upanishads-2" locator="Bṛhadāraṇyaka II.3.6, p. 108" /> Advaita reads this as pointing to the qualityless Brahman behind every description. The theistic schools read it as denying that any finite description exhausts the Lord, not as denying his qualities.

#### The Lord who wields māyā

The Śvetāśvatara calls nature *māyā* and the great Lord the *māyin*, the one who wields it.<Cite source="muller-upanishads-2" locator="Śvetāśvatara IV.10, p. 252" /> For Advaita, *māyā* names the power by which the one Brahman appears as many, and the Lord is Brahman as associated with that power. For the theistic schools the verse describes a real Lord with real power over a real nature.

#### Rāmānuja's critique

Rāmānuja's long argument against Advaita turns on qualities. A Brahman of pure, qualityless consciousness, he argued, could not even be the seat of the ignorance Advaita needs to explain the world.<Cite source="thibaut-ramanuja-sribhashya" locator="I.1.1, p. 103" /> For him the qualities are what make Brahman the highest Person. His Gītā commentary describes the Lord as the inner ruler, whose will is the cause and support of all.<Cite source="govindacharya-gita-ramanuja" locator="9.4–6, pp. 290–292" /> How devotion works across the schools is treated in <QuestionLink question="worship" worldview="hinduism">worship</QuestionLink>.
