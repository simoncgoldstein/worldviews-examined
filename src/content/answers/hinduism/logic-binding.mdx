## The view

Indian philosophers developed logic as part of the theory of knowledge. Their question was not "which argument forms are valid?" but "how does an inference give us knowledge of what we cannot perceive?" That is why both Buddhist and Hindu schools count a valid argument with a false premise as a fallacy: it produces no knowledge.<Cite source="sep-epistemology-india" locator="§5" />

The Nyāya school set out a standard five-step form for public argument (see the Deep dive). Its heart is a general rule, such as "whatever is produced is non-eternal, like a pot."<Cite source="vidyabhusana-nyaya-sutras" locator="1.1.32–39 (pp. 10–12)" /> Indian logicians call such a rule *vyāpti*, "pervasion": the sign (being produced, or smoke) is always accompanied by what is to be proved (impermanence, or fire). For the realist Nyāya school, pervasion is a real relation between properties in the world. We discover it; we do not invent it.<Cite source="sep-epistemology-india" locator="§5" /> Logic binds because it follows these real connections.

Reasoning that goes wrong is analysed as a family of fallacies, "apparent reasons" that mimic good signs but mislead.<Cite source="sep-epistemology-india" locator="§2" /> When two claims each seem to have support, a further tool decides between them: *tarka*, suppositional reasoning, "which reveals the character by showing the absurdity of all contrary characters."<Cite source="vidyabhusana-nyaya-sutras" locator="1.1.40 (p. 13)" /><Cite source="sep-epistemology-india" locator="§8" />

Mīmāṃsā adds that every cognition, inference included, is valid until defeated (see <QuestionLink question="knowledge-possible" worldview="hinduism">why we can know anything</QuestionLink>). Advaita accepts inference within ordinary experience, but not as a way of knowing Brahman.

## What this explains well

The approach explains why logic matters: it is how we come to know what we cannot see. Tying logic to real relations explains why good reasoning works, and the fallacies and the reductio method give practical tests for bad reasoning.

## Christian response

The Christian shares Nyāya's realism: logical relations are discovered features of a real world, not conventions. The further question is what grounds that world's intelligibility and the necessity of its pervasions. The theistic Nyāya of Udayana gives an answer close to the Reformed one, an intelligent maker of the world.<Cite source="cowell-kusumanjali" locator="V.1" /> Schools without such a maker take the world's intelligible structure as given. For Advaita a sharper question arises: if inference belongs to the level of ignorance, logic does not reach ultimate reality at all. The Christian holds that logic is grounded in a God who is himself truth, so it binds at every level. This is an explanatory disagreement, not a charge of contradiction.

## Deep dive

#### The five-membered argument

The Nyāya Sūtra's example runs: proposition, sound is non-eternal; reason, because it is produced; example, whatever is produced is non-eternal, like a pot; application, sound is produced; conclusion, therefore sound is non-eternal.<Cite source="vidyabhusana-nyaya-sutras" locator="1.1.32–39 (pp. 10–12)" />

#### Not formal validity

The modern distinction between an argument's form and its content does not map neatly onto Indian logic. As SEP puts it, classical Indian philosophers "are not focused on logic per se, but rather on a psychological process whereby we come to know things indirectly, by way of a sign."<Cite source="sep-epistemology-india" locator="§5" /> An argument is good when the knower actually comes to know the conclusion through it.

#### Three conditions for inference

The Nyāya philosopher Uddyotakara, borrowing from his Buddhist opponent Dignāga, required three things of anyone who infers. The sign must be known to belong to the subject; the pervasion must be remembered; and the knower must bring the two together in an act of "reflection."<Cite source="sep-epistemology-india" locator="§5" /> Logic, on this view, includes the act of a real knower connecting what he knows.

#### Suppositional reasoning

*Tarka* is not itself a means of knowledge but a way of resolving conflicts between apparent sources. The Nyāya Sūtra's example asks whether the soul is eternal. If it were not, it could not enjoy the fruits of its own actions; the absurdity of the alternative supports the thesis.<Cite source="vidyabhusana-nyaya-sutras" locator="1.1.40 (p. 13)" /> The method resembles the reductio arguments of Western logic.

#### Reason and the Veda

Vedānta allows independent reasoning no authority over what only scripture can reveal, as described under <QuestionLink question="ultimate-authority" worldview="hinduism">the ultimate authority for truth</QuestionLink>. Śaṅkara's complaint is that arguments about such matters are endlessly overturned by cleverer ones.<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra" locator="II.1.11 (pp. 314–317)" /> That is a limit on reasoning about what lies beyond perception, not a rejection of logic.
