## The view

The Bhagavad Gītā identifies desire, anger and greed as destructive moral directions. Its account of divine and demonic endowments supplies a normative distinction between forms of conduct, not simply a contrast between pleasure and pain.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="16.1–21" /> Its analysis of the guṇas explains different patterns of embodied motivation, while the intellect itself can mistake adharma for dharma.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="14.6–10; 18.31–32" /> Evil therefore includes distorted judgment as well as uncontrolled impulse.

Śaṅkara's Brahma-sūtra introduction connects the deepest disorder with superimposition: confusing self and what is not self shapes ordinary agency and experience.<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra" locator="intro, pp. 3–9" /> His discussion of inequality appeals to karma rather than divine partiality or cruelty.<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra" locator="II.1.34–36" /> Neither claim should be attributed without qualification to every Hindu school.

Rāmānuja instead treats individual selves as real, with knowledge obscured by beginningless karma. Human volitional effort remains significant within divine permission; his account also describes the compounding of wrongful direction.<Cite source="thibaut-ramanuja-sribhashya" locator="I.1.4, p. 198; II.3.40–41, pp. 556–558" /> Nyāya describes a causal chain from misapprehension through faults and activity to birth and pain, making delusion central to the emergence of other faults.<Cite source="vidyabhusana-nyaya-sutras" locator="1.1.2; 4.1.3–6" /> These explanations share themes without being one metaphysical theory.

The karmic explanation also raises a question about the order within which action bears fruit. Explaining unequal outcomes by previous action can answer a charge of arbitrary distribution within the theory, but it leaves a reader asking what warrants the theory and its governing relation. The school's explanation must therefore be distinguished from evidence that establishes it. The same discipline applies to the Christian appeal to the Fall: a revealed history is a premise to defend, not a fact conceded by every comparator.

## What this explains well

The accounts connect judgment, motivation, conduct and consequences, so evil need not be an unexplained second substance. They explain how apparently rational action can be governed by mistaken identification or disordered desire. They also distinguish the suffering associated with embodied existence from a particular agent's wrongdoing. Preserving the school differences makes the analysis stronger: an explanation through superimposition has different implications from one through a real individual soul's choices under God's permission.

## Christian response

The Christian agrees that evil can exploit good capacities and that judgment may serve desire. Augustine's privation account likewise denies that evil is an independent substance.<Cite source="augustine-enchiridion" locator="11, 13" /> The disagreement concerns the norm and the history: Christianity locates sin in departure from God's law and connects the human condition with Adam's fall.<Cite source="westminster-larger-catechism" locator="Q. 24" /><Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 5:12" /> It asks what warrants karma as an explanation of inequality, and whether ignorance adequately explains knowing wrongdoing. Rāmānuja's account of volition should receive a different objection from Śaṅkara's ultimate account of selfhood. Christianity's own distinction between divine government and guilt also requires defence.

## Pressure questions

- What warrants karma as an explanation of unequal conditions without making victims' suffering evidence of a known prior offence?
- How do ignorance and responsible volition relate when a person recognizes a wrong and still chooses it?

## Deep dive

#### Moral disorder is not merely unpleasantness

The Gītā's divine and demonic endowments distinguish directions of character. Desire, anger and greed can motivate action and destroy discernment; the guṇas also shape how intellect interprets its objects.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="16.1–21; 18.31–32" /> A pleasant experience can therefore accompany a harmful direction, and painful discipline need not be evil in the same sense. <QuestionLink question="know-the-good" worldview="hinduism">Knowledge of the good</QuestionLink> addresses the authority for moral judgment; <QuestionLink question="self-deception" worldview="hinduism">self-deception</QuestionLink> examines judgment that misidentifies its object.

Nyāya's account supplies another explanatory sequence. Misapprehension leads to faults, activity, birth and pain; the classification of faults includes attachment, aversion and delusion, with delusion presented as foundational to the others.<Cite source="vidyabhusana-nyaya-sutras" locator="1.1.2; 4.1.3–6" /> This is not simply the Advaita doctrine restated in different terminology. The chain concerns a distinct school's account of agency and the human problem.

#### Three Vedānta distinctions

Śaṅkara's superimposition account operates at the level of mistaken identification underlying ordinary experience. His karma explanation of inequality separately answers the charge that a creator's unequal distribution makes him partial or cruel.<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra" locator="intro, pp. 3–9; II.1.34–36" /> Those claims must not collapse into the assertion that every experienced injury is negligible because it is not ultimate. The harm and the dispute about ultimate reality concern different levels of the account.

Rāmānuja's real selves and obscured knowledge make the agency question different. His discussion preserves the soul's volitional effort while relating action to divine permission, including a punitive confirmation of a wrongful direction.<Cite source="thibaut-ramanuja-sribhashya" locator="II.3.40–41, pp. 556–558" /> That does not remove the difficult question about God and wrongdoing. It shows why the question must be addressed in the school's own terms rather than by treating all Hindu theism as Advaita.

Madhva gives another account of differentiated souls and divine government. The Brahma-sūtra commentary relates divine impulsion to previous action and effort; its passage distinguishing destinies, including enduring misery, invokes a Padma Purāṇa quotation and is contested.<Cite source="subbarau-madhva-brahmasutra" locator="II.3.42; III.1.21, p. 178" /> This specialist strand limits any claim that every Hindu school offers the same diagnosis or destination. It is not expanded here into a fresh account of liberation.

#### What the explanation permits

Karma can function within these accounts as an explanation of unequal conditions without providing an observer knowledge of a sufferer's particular former action. Assigning a specific prior offence would require evidence that the general theory does not supply. <QuestionLink question="suffering" worldview="hinduism">Suffering</QuestionLink> keeps that limit explicit. <QuestionLink question="fail-the-good" worldview="hinduism">Failure to do the good</QuestionLink> distinguishes desire's strength from responsible effort, and <QuestionLink question="death" worldview="hinduism">death</QuestionLink> develops the material disagreement over selfhood. The anchor identifies these relations without merging their schools or treating every harm as the same kind of evil.

The phrase beginningless karma marks a limit to an origin story.<Cite source="thibaut-ramanuja-sribhashya" locator="I.1.4, p. 198" /> It relates present obscuration to an enduring causal condition rather than identifying one first human transgression. That is a material difference from the Christian account. A reader may still ask how the karmic relation is grounded and whether the explanation answers the ultimate source of disorder. The comparison should preserve that question without pretending that an account must accept Christian historical premises before it can describe moral causation.
