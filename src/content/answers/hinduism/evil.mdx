## The view

The Bhagavad Gītā identifies desire, anger and greed as destructive moral directions. Its account of divine and demonic endowments supplies a normative distinction between forms of conduct, not simply a contrast between pleasure and pain.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="16.1–21" /> Its analysis of the guṇas explains different patterns of embodied motivation, while the intellect itself can mistake adharma for dharma.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="14.6–10; 18.31–32" /> Evil therefore includes distorted judgment as well as uncontrolled impulse.

Śaṅkara's Brahma-sūtra introduction connects the deepest disorder with superimposition: confusing self and what is not self shapes ordinary agency and experience.<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra" locator="intro, pp. 3–9" /> That diagnosis belongs to Advaita, not to every Hindu school.

Rāmānuja instead treats individual selves as real, with knowledge obscured by beginningless karma. Human volitional effort remains significant within divine permission; his account also describes the compounding of wrongful direction.<Cite source="thibaut-ramanuja-sribhashya" locator="I.1.4, p. 198; II.3.40–41, pp. 556–558" /> Nyāya describes a causal chain from misapprehension through faults and activity to birth and pain, making delusion central to the emergence of other faults.<Cite source="vidyabhusana-nyaya-sutras" locator="1.1.2; 4.1.3–6" /> These explanations share themes without being one metaphysical theory.

Karma answers the charge that unequal conditions are arbitrary, and here Śaṅkara and Rāmānuja read the sūtras alike. The Brahma Sūtra rejects the objection that a Lord who makes some beings happy and others wretched is partial or cruel: he gives each its station with regard to its merit and demerit, as rain is the common cause of crops whose differences lie in their seeds. Because the round of rebirth is beginningless, merit and inequality are related like seed and sprout, so there was no first creation in which an innocent soul received an undeserved lot.<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra" locator="II.1.34–36" /><Cite source="thibaut-ramanuja-sribhashya" locator="II.1.34–35, pp. 477–479" /> Śaṅkara and Rāmānuja differ over the setting: for Śaṅkara the teaching about creation concerns the world of name and form produced by ignorance, while for Rāmānuja souls and their deeds form a real, beginningless stream within Brahman's body.<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra" locator="II.1.33" /><Cite source="thibaut-ramanuja-sribhashya" locator="II.1.35, p. 479" />

## What this explains well

The accounts connect judgment, motivation, conduct and consequences, so evil need not be an unexplained second substance. They explain how apparently rational action can be governed by mistaken identification or disordered desire. They also distinguish the suffering associated with embodied existence from a particular agent's wrongdoing. The school differences matter: an explanation through superimposition has different implications from one through a real individual soul's choices under God's permission.

## Christian response

The Christian agrees that evil can exploit good capacities and that judgment may serve desire. Augustine's privation account likewise denies that evil is an independent substance.<Cite source="augustine-enchiridion" locator="11, 13" /> The disagreement concerns the norm and the history: Christianity locates sin in departure from God's law and connects the human condition with Adam's fall.<Cite source="westminster-larger-catechism" locator="Q. 24" /><Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 5:12" /> Vedānta has an answer to the charge of arbitrary distribution, and it argues for it from scripture and from justice: if inequality did not follow earlier deeds, souls would be rewarded and punished for what they had not done.<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra" locator="II.1.36" /> The Christian shares the premise that God is not partial but denies that inequality must therefore be desert. Jesus rejects his disciples' assumption that a man born blind suffered for his own or his parents' sin,<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="John 9:1–3" /> and Christianity places unequal conditions within a fallen world under God's free government rather than within each soul's history of merit. That answer carries its own burden: it must say why a just God permits harms no one deserves (see <QuestionLink question="evil" worldview="christianity">the Christian answer</QuestionLink>), and its history of the Fall has to be defended rather than assumed. Neither a beginningless karmic history nor the Fall can be observed, so the dispute is over which revealed account, and which conception of divine justice, has the better warrant. The further questions bite differently by school. Against Śaṅkara, the Christian asks whether ignorance adequately explains knowing wrongdoing, since Scripture describes people who know God and suppress what they know.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 1:18–21" /> Rāmānuja's real agent, choosing within divine permission, stands closer to the Christian picture, and the question there is how divine government and human guilt relate, a question Christianity must answer too.

## Pressure questions

- What warrants karma as an explanation of unequal conditions without making victims' suffering evidence of a known prior offense?
- How do ignorance and responsible volition relate when a person recognizes a wrong and still chooses it?

## Deep dive

#### Moral disorder is not merely unpleasantness

The Gītā's divine and demonic endowments distinguish directions of character. Desire, anger and greed can motivate action and destroy discernment; the guṇas also shape how intellect interprets its objects.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="16.1–21; 18.31–32" /> A pleasant experience can therefore accompany a harmful direction, and painful discipline need not be evil in the same sense. <QuestionLink question="know-the-good" worldview="hinduism">Knowledge of the good</QuestionLink> addresses the authority for moral judgment; <QuestionLink question="self-deception" worldview="hinduism">self-deception</QuestionLink> examines judgment that misidentifies its object.

Nyāya's account supplies another explanatory sequence. Misapprehension leads to faults, activity, birth and pain; the classification of faults includes attachment, aversion and delusion, with delusion presented as foundational to the others.<Cite source="vidyabhusana-nyaya-sutras" locator="1.1.2; 4.1.3–6" /> This is Nyāya's own account of agency and the human problem, not Advaita in other words.

#### Three Vedānta distinctions

Śaṅkara's superimposition account operates at the level of mistaken identification underlying ordinary experience. His karma explanation of inequality, which Rāmānuja shares, separately answers the charge that a creator's unequal distribution makes him partial or cruel.<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra" locator="intro, pp. 3–9; II.1.34–36" /> Neither makes experienced injury negligible: the harm is real within experience, whatever is said about ultimate reality.

Rāmānuja's real selves and obscured knowledge make the agency question different. His discussion preserves the soul's volitional effort while relating action to divine permission, including a punitive confirmation of a wrongful direction.<Cite source="thibaut-ramanuja-sribhashya" locator="II.3.40–41, pp. 556–558" /> The difficult question about God and wrongdoing remains, but it has to be put in Rāmānuja's terms, not Advaita's.

Madhva gives another account of differentiated souls and divine government. The Brahma-sūtra commentary relates divine impulsion to previous action and effort; its passage distinguishing destinies, including enduring misery, invokes a Padma Purāṇa quotation and is contested.<Cite source="subbarau-madhva-brahmasutra" locator="II.3.42; III.1.21, p. 178" /> Madhva's strand alone shows that Hindu schools do not share one diagnosis or one destination.

#### What the explanation permits

Karma can function within these accounts as an explanation of unequal conditions without providing an observer knowledge of a sufferer's particular former action. Assigning a specific prior offense would require evidence that the general theory does not supply. <QuestionLink question="suffering" worldview="hinduism">Suffering</QuestionLink> develops that limit. <QuestionLink question="fail-the-good" worldview="hinduism">Failure to do the good</QuestionLink> distinguishes desire's strength from responsible effort, and <QuestionLink question="death" worldview="hinduism">death</QuestionLink> develops the disagreement over selfhood.

Beginningless karma also has no origin story.<Cite source="thibaut-ramanuja-sribhashya" locator="I.1.4, p. 198" /> It relates present obscuration to an enduring causal condition rather than to a first human transgression, a material difference from the Christian account. The karmic account can describe moral causation without any such history; the open question is how the karmic relation itself is grounded.
