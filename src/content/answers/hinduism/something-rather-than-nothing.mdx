## The view

The question "why is there something rather than nothing?" assumes that nothing came first. Most Hindu traditions deny that. A teacher in the Chāndogya Upaniṣad rejects the idea that being came from non-being: "How could that which is, be born of that which is not? No, my dear, only that which is, was in the beginning, one only, without a second."<Cite source="muller-upanishads-1" locator="Chāndogya VI.2.1–2, pp. 93–94" /> Being is primordial. The world issues from it, is sustained by it and returns to it, over and over, in cycles that have no first beginning.

Why does the Lord send forth the world at all, if he lacks nothing? The Brahma Sūtra answers that it is "mere sport, such as we see in ordinary life," like a king who plays though he has no unfulfilled desire.<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra" locator="II.1.33, pp. 356–357" /> Rāmānuja gives the same answer: creation is the play (*līlā*) of a Lord whose wishes are already fulfilled.<Cite source="thibaut-ramanuja-sribhashya" locator="II.1.33, p. 477" /> Madhva cites scripture that designing a world is simply the Lord's nature, "for there is nothing to be desired by Him who has all that is desirable."<Cite source="subbarau-madhva-brahmasutra" locator="II.1.34, p. 103" />

For Advaita there is a further qualification. Scripture's account of creation "does not refer to the highest reality; it refers to the apparent world only."<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra" locator="II.1.33, pp. 356–357" /> At the ultimate level nothing was ever really produced.

## What this explains well

The view avoids the puzzle of a first moment before which nothing existed, and it explains creation without making God needy. It also shows humility from the start: a Ṛgveda creation hymn wonders whether even the highest overseer knows how creation came about.

## Christian response

The Christian agrees that nothing comes from nothing, and that God creates out of fullness, not need. Creation "from nothing" does not treat non-being as a source; it means that God alone is the source.<Cite source="bavinck-reformed-dogmatics-monergism-2" locator="Vol. 2, §29.5, PDF p. 493" /> The disagreement is about the world's past and its standing. Scripture teaches a creation with a beginning, made for God's glory,<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Revelation 4:11" /> rather than a beginningless round of emanation and reabsorption.

The Christian also asks a question of explanatory adequacy. In a beginningless series each world-cycle is explained by an earlier one; does the series as a whole need an explanation? Within Hindu thought, the Nyāya philosopher Udayana argued that the world's effects still require an intelligent maker, though he too accepted beginningless cycles and did not argue for a first beginning.

## Pressure questions

- If the cycle of worlds has no beginning, what explains the existence of the whole series, rather than each stage of it?
- If creation is the Lord's play, could he have refrained from it, and would that have been any less perfect?

## Deep dive

#### The Ṛgveda's questioning hymn

The *Nāsadīya* hymn begins, "Then was not non-existent nor existent," and speaks of "That One Thing, breathless, breathed by its own nature." It ends in doubt: the one who oversees creation from the highest heaven "verily knows it, or perhaps he knows not."<Cite source="griffith-rigveda-4" locator="X.129, pp. 367–368" /> The hymn is often read as the beginning of Indian reflection on this question, and it frames it as a mystery rather than a demand for a first event.

#### The argument of Nyāya

The Nyāya school accepted the principle that "entity does not arise from non-entity."<Cite source="vidyabhusana-nyaya-sutras" locator="IV.1.14" /> Its greatest theistic philosopher, Udayana, gave a classic list of grounds for "an everlasting omniscient Being." The earth and similar things have the nature of effects, like a pot, so they must have a maker who wills to make them and knows their material. The first joining of atoms at the beginning of a world-cycle is an action, so it requires the volition of an intelligent being.<Cite source="cowell-kusumanjali" locator="V.1, pp. 64–65" /> The argument concerns the arranging of atoms at the start of each world-cycle. Udayana's God orders and renews worlds; he does not create from nothing.

#### Scripture or inference?

The Vedānta schools rejected this kind of reasoning as a route to Brahman. Rāmānuja argues at length that inference cannot establish one omniscient creator, and that Brahman is known from scripture alone.<Cite source="thibaut-ramanuja-sribhashya" locator="I.1.3, pp. 161–171" /> So Hindu traditions disagree not only about what grounds the world but about how anyone could know it. That bears on the dependence of every lane's answer on revelation.

#### Play and dispensation

The sport analogy raises the problem of suffering: why would a playful Lord make such an unequal world? The Brahma Sūtra's answer, which all three commentators accept in their own ways, is that the Lord's dispensation is relative to the karma of beings, and that the round of rebirth has no beginning.<Cite source="subbarau-madhva-brahmasutra" locator="II.1.35, p. 104" /> That theme belongs to <QuestionLink question="evil" worldview="hinduism">the question of evil</QuestionLink>.
