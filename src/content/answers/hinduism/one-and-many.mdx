## The view

In the beginning, say the Upaniṣads, there was Being, "one only, without a second," and it thought, "may I be many."<Cite source="muller-upanishads-1" locator="Chāndogya VI.2.1–3, pp. 93–94" /> The Lord "makes the one seed manifold."<Cite source="muller-upanishads-2" locator="Śvetāśvatara VI.12" /> The three Vedānta schools accept these texts but differ sharply on what the many are.

**Advaita** (Śaṅkara): the one is ultimately real and the many are its names and forms. Pots are many, but "the truth is that it is clay merely"; so the world's variety is Brahman under name and form.<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra" locator="II.1.14, pp. 320–324" />

**Viśiṣṭādvaita** (Rāmānuja): the one Brahman has a real body made of many souls and things. They are distinct and real, but never separate from him, as a body is inseparable from its soul.<Cite source="thibaut-ramanuja-sribhashya" locator="I.1.1, p. 140" />

**Dvaita** (Madhva): difference is real and eternal. God, souls and matter are irreducibly distinct, and so is each soul from every other.<Cite source="iep-madhva" locator="§3" /> Scripture calls the Lord "all" because he rules all, not because all is he.<Cite source="subbarau-madhva-brahmasutra" locator="I.1.17, pp. 21–22" />

## What this explains well

Each school does justice to a real intuition: that all is finally one (Advaita), that the many live within one life (Rāmānuja), or that real difference is never erased (Madhva).

## Christian response

The Christian holds that the world is a distinct creature made from nothing by the triune God. With Advaita the disagreement is whether plurality is ultimately real; Christianity says created plurality is real and good.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Genesis 1:31" /> With Rāmānuja it is whether the world is God's body or his creature; with Madhva, closest to the Christian view, whether souls and matter were created or are beginningless. These are theological disagreements.

## Pressure questions

- For Advaita, if the many are finally names and forms, who is it that is liberated from them?
- For Madhva, if every soul is eternally distinct and uncreated, what unites them in dependence on one Lord?

## Deep dive

#### Clay, pots and bodies

The clay analogy of the Chāndogya is read by all three schools. Śaṅkara reads it as non-difference of the effect from its cause: only the clay is finally real.<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra" locator="II.1.14, pp. 320–324" /> Rāmānuja reads it as one Brahman in two states, its body of souls and matter subtle before creation and differentiated after, so that the effect is non-different from the cause and yet the many are real.<Cite source="thibaut-ramanuja-sribhashya" locator="I.1.1, p. 140" />

#### All beings in me

The Gītā's paradox, "all beings are in me, and I am not in them," divides the commentators again. Śaṅkara takes it of the unattached Self supporting all without contact.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="9.4–5, pp. 217–218" /> Rāmānuja takes it of the Lord holding all beings as his body by his will, while not depending on them.<Cite source="govindacharya-gita-ramanuja" locator="9.4–6, pp. 290–292" />

#### Madhva's five differences

Madhva's school lists five eternal differences: God and soul, God and matter, soul and soul, soul and matter, and matter and matter.<Cite source="iep-madhva" locator="§3" /> The list is given here through secondary scholarship, because Madhva's own enumeration was not available in a lawful readable edition. His commentary on the Brahma Sūtra supplies the primary support for real difference.
