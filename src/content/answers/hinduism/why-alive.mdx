## The view

Since in Vedānta the self is beginningless, the question is what a human life is for. The classical answer is the aims of life (*puruṣārthas*). *Manusmṛti* weighs whether the chief good is duty (*dharma*), wealth (*artha*) or desire (*kāma*), and concludes that the good is "the aggregate of the three."<Cite source="buhler-manu" locator="II.224" /> Beyond them stands liberation (*mokṣa*), freedom from the cycle of rebirth, and the life stages order the three toward it: only after paying his debts to the sages, the ancestors and the gods should a person set his mind on liberation.<Cite source="buhler-manu" locator="VI.35–37" />

Because the self passes through many births, the human birth has particular value. Śaṅkara calls it rare and difficult to attain, the means to every human aim, and urges that it not be wasted.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="9.33, commentary" /> The Gītā teaches how to live it. Action cannot be abandoned, but it can be done without attachment to its fruits; Janaka and others reached perfection through action, and one should act also for the maintenance of the world (*lokasaṃgraha*).<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="3.19–21" /> Whatever one does can be offered to the Lord.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="9.27–28" />

The schools differ on what liberation is, and so on what life is finally for. For Śaṅkara's Advaita, liberation is knowledge that the Self is Brahman; right action purifies the mind and prepares for that knowledge but does not itself liberate.<Cite source="sep-shankara" locator="§§4.1, 4.3" /> For Rāmānuja, liberation is the manifestation of the soul's own nature, enjoying the Supreme Person while remaining dependent on him.<Cite source="thibaut-ramanuja-sribhashya" locator="IV.4.1–4, 17" />

## What this explains well

The account treats ordinary human goods as legitimate without making them ultimate. Prosperity, pleasure and family have their place within dharma, while their impermanence points beyond them. And the ideal of action without attachment explains how one can work wholeheartedly for others while staying free of anxiety about results.

## Christian response

The Christian recognizes the wisdom of ordering lesser goods to a highest good and of offering every action to God; Paul says the same of eating and drinking.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="1 Corinthians 10:31" /> The disagreement concerns the highest good and its relation to the human creature. For Advaita, purpose finally lies in realizing that individuality was never ultimate, which the Christian thinks leaves the personal life that the aims organize without lasting significance; this is a theological disagreement about ultimate reality. Rāmānuja's account of eternal loving service stands much closer to the Christian end of glorifying and enjoying God. The remaining question with him is whether the soul has a beginning, so that its purpose is given by a maker.

## Pressure questions

- If the self has passed through countless births, why should this one human life matter decisively?
- In Advaita, whose purpose is fulfilled when the individual is found never to have been ultimate?
- Can desire and prosperity be legitimate aims if liberation requires letting them go?

## Deep dive

#### The aims and the stages

*Manusmṛti* records competing views: some say duty and wealth are the good, others pleasure and wealth, others duty alone or wealth alone. Its own decision is that the three together are the good.<Cite source="buhler-manu" locator="II.224" /> The life stages give the aims a sequence. The householder pursues all three, and after seeing his grandchildren may withdraw to the forest; only then is liberation the main concern.<Cite source="buhler-manu" locator="VI.1–2, 35–37" /> Many Hindus treat dharmaśāstra as guidance rather than binding law, and the scheme was written for twice-born men; its details are not shared by all.

#### Action without attachment

The Gītā's teaching on action is its answer to the purpose question for ordinary people. Arjuna is not told to escape his duties but to perform them without attachment, for the world's sake and as an offering.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="3.19–21; 9.27–28" /> For Advaita such action prepares the mind for liberating knowledge; for the theistic schools it is already part of the soul's devotion to the Lord, culminating in surrender.

#### Surrender as the culmination

Rāmānuja's Gītā commentary reads the closing call to abandon all other duties and take refuge in the Lord alone as the culmination of the teaching, with the Lord's promise to free the devotee from all sins.<Cite source="govindacharya-gita-ramanuja" locator="18.66" /> On this reading the purpose of a human life is not finally achievement but refuge in a personal God. Scholars debate how far the later doctrine of surrender is Rāmānuja's own, but the theistic schools agree that the soul's end is relational.<Cite source="iep-ramanuja" locator="§7" />
