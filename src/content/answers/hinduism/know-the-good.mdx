## The view

The Gītā makes scripture authoritative for what ought and ought not to be done, contrasting it with action governed by desire.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="16.23–24" /> The Kaṭha Upaniṣad distinguishes the good from the pleasant: wisdom involves recognizing their difference rather than choosing whatever attracts immediately.<Cite source="muller-upanishads-2" locator="Kaṭha I.2.1–2, p. 8" /> Seeking the good therefore requires discernment, not an assumption that every desire expresses it.

Manu identifies several sources of dharma: Veda, received tradition, the conduct of those who know, and self-satisfaction.<Cite source="buhler-manu" locator="II.6, p. 30; II.10–13, p. 31" /> The last is not personal preference: Manu and its commentators give Veda and tradition the privileged place and limit such discernment to those free of acquisitive desire. Mīmāṃsā makes an especially strong claim: dharma is known through authoritative injunction rather than ordinary perception or inference.<Cite source="jha-slokavarttika" locator="Codanā 16–18" /> That is a claim about how duty is known; people can still notice ordinary consequences.

## What this explains well

These accounts distinguish attraction from authority and moral instruction from mere preference. The Gītā also recognizes that the intellect can misjudge duty or take wrong for right.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="18.31–32" /> Inward discernment is therefore fallible and needs formation. The tradition's sources make room for formation and correction of judgment, while its schools give different accounts of what moral discipline ultimately serves.

## Christian response

The Christian agrees that desire is an unreliable final standard and that moral awareness needs authoritative correction. His further claim grounds obligation in the Creator's law, recognized imperfectly through conscience.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 2:14–15" /> Neither side sets scripture against reason, and neither reduces duty to preference: both recognize interpretive authority and disputed judgments. The disagreement takes a different form with each school. Mīmāṃsā holds that dharma is known only through injunction. Christianity agrees that some duties are known only because God commanded them, such as Israel's ceremonies, but holds that the moral law is also written on the heart, so that Scripture clarifies and corrects a knowledge it did not create.<Cite source="turretin-institutio-1847" locator="XI.1.4, 7" note="Latin; paraphrased" /> With Mīmāṃsā, then, the question is both which word carries authority and whether the core of duty is known apart from it (see <QuestionLink question="know-the-good" worldview="christianity">the Christian answer</QuestionLink>). With Advaita the difference goes deeper. Śaṅkara reads the Gītā's final teaching as transcending dharma and adharma in knowledge of the Self,<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="18.66, comm." /> whereas the Westminster Confession holds that the moral law binds "forever," the justified as well as others, because of "the authority of God the Creator, who gave it."<Cite source="wcf" locator="19.5" /> On the Christian view no realization rises above the moral law, because it expresses the holiness of a personal God. That is a theological disagreement about how dharma relates to ultimate reality.

## Pressure questions

- What warrants an injunction's authority when ordinary observation cannot establish dharma?
- How is apparent inward satisfaction distinguished from desire which has misidentified duty?

## Deep dive

#### Knowing duty and seeking liberation

Mīmāṃsā's claim is specifically about the kind of knowledge required for dharma. Observation may disclose an action's immediate effects without disclosing the authoritative duty expressed in an injunction. It is a claim about moral knowledge, not a rejection of reason in general.<Cite source="jha-slokavarttika" locator="Codanā 16–18" />

Manu's list, read in context, does not license whatever a person happens to approve; the surrounding account gives privileged standing to Veda and tradition and restricts appropriate discernment to those not governed by acquisitive desire.<Cite source="buhler-manu" locator="II.10–13, p. 31" /> How far self-satisfaction reaches is disputed, so it does not map neatly onto a modern autonomous conscience.

Vedānta brings another question into view: moral discipline's relation to liberation. The transcendence of dharma and adharma that Śaṅkara finds in the Gītā's final surrender belongs to realized knowledge of the Self.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="18.66, comm." /> It gives no license to someone who simply prefers to ignore ordinary ethical distinctions, and an ultimate claim cannot be borrowed to excuse conventional wrongdoing.
