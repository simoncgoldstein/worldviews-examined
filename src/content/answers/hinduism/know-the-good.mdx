## The view

The Gītā makes scripture authoritative for what ought and ought not to be done, contrasting it with action governed by desire.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="16.23–24" /> The Kaṭha Upaniṣad distinguishes the good from the pleasant: wisdom involves recognizing their difference rather than choosing whatever attracts immediately.<Cite source="muller-upanishads-2" locator="Kaṭha I.2.1–2, p. 8" /> Seeking the good therefore requires discernment, not an assumption that every desire expresses it.

Manu identifies several sources of dharma: Veda, received tradition, the conduct of those who know, and self-satisfaction.<Cite source="buhler-manu" locator="II.6, p. 30; II.10–13, p. 31" /> The last category needs qualification. The registered edition's commentators limit its use rather than presenting autonomous personal preference as a universal moral authority. Mīmāṃsā makes an especially strong claim: dharma is known through authoritative injunction rather than ordinary perception or inference.<Cite source="jha-slokavarttika" locator="Codanā 16–18" /> This concerns the warrant for duty, not the denial that people can notice ordinary consequences.

## What this explains well

These accounts distinguish attraction from authority and moral instruction from mere preference. The Gītā also recognizes that the intellect can misjudge duty or take wrong for right.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="18.31–32" /> Thus an appeal to inward discernment is not automatically a claim of infallible conscience. The tradition's sources make room for formation and correction of judgment, while its schools give different accounts of what moral discipline ultimately serves.

## Christian response

The Christian agrees that desire is an unreliable final standard and that moral awareness needs authoritative correction. His further claim grounds obligation in the Creator's law, recognized imperfectly through conscience.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 2:14–15" /> The disagreement is not simply scripture versus reason: both lanes recognize interpretive authority and disputed judgments. It concerns which revelation is authoritative and how dharma relates to ultimate reality. Mīmāṃsā's account of injunction poses a different comparison from Vedānta's accounts of the self and liberation. A Christian response must examine those actual grounds, not assume that every Hindu view is preference relativism.

## Pressure questions

- What warrants an injunction's authority when ordinary observation cannot establish dharma?
- How is apparent inward satisfaction distinguished from desire which has misidentified duty?

## Deep dive

#### Knowing duty and seeking liberation

Mīmāṃsā's claim is specifically about the kind of knowledge required for dharma. Observation may disclose an action's immediate effects without disclosing the authoritative duty expressed in an injunction. Treating this as a general rejection of reason would confuse a moral-epistemological claim with a denial of ordinary cognition.<Cite source="jha-slokavarttika" locator="Codanā 16–18" />

The Manu material also needs contextual handling. Its list does not license whatever a person happens to approve; the surrounding account gives privileged standing to Veda and tradition and restricts appropriate discernment to those not governed by acquisitive desire.<Cite source="buhler-manu" locator="II.10–13, p. 31" /> The contested scope of self-satisfaction remains a limit on simple comparisons with modern autonomous conscience.

Vedānta brings another question into view: moral discipline's relation to liberation. Śaṅkara's reading of the Gītā's final surrender includes transcending dharma and adharma in knowledge of Self.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="18.66, comm." /> That is not a licence to treat ordinary ethical distinctions as unreal for any person who prefers to ignore them. The levels and purpose of the account must be distinguished, rather than borrowing an ultimate claim to excuse conventional wrongdoing.
