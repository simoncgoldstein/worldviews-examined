## The view

Across the Vedānta traditions, the human problem is bondage in saṃsāra through desire, ignorance and karma. The Gītā gives the problem a moral psychology: desire and wrath veil wisdom through senses, mind and reason, so recognizing a duty does not guarantee doing it.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="3.36–43" /> The self is not exhausted by the current body, but continuing existence under karma is not yet freedom.<Cite source="muller-upanishads-2" locator="Bṛhadāraṇyaka IV.4.3–5" />

The diagnosis and decisive remedy then diverge. Śaṅkara locates the root in superimposition: Self and body/mind are confused, and mistaken agency sustains bondage.<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra" locator="I.1.1, intro., pp. 3–9" /> Liberating knowledge removes that error. Disciplined action and contemplative training prepare the seeker; they do not manufacture a previously absent nondual Self. The Gītā's fire of knowledge renders actions incapable of producing further binding results, while karma already sustaining the present body continues.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="4.36–39, pp. 134–135" />

For Rāmānuja, the soul is real and distinct, with knowledge obscured by karma. Devotion, knowledge of the Lord and refuge receive divine aid rather than discovering that ultimate individuality was an illusion. His reading of the Gītā's invitation to surrender presents the Lord freeing the devotee from impediments.<Cite source="govindacharya-gita-ramanuja" locator="18.66, p. 565" /> His Brahma Sūtra commentary explains removal of sin's power through the Lord's favor, while excluding deliberate wrongdoing from the non-clinging of later sins.<Cite source="thibaut-ramanuja-sribhashya" locator="IV.1.13–15" />

Madhva also preserves dependent, distinct souls, making Viṣṇu's grace decisive for liberation. His account differs from Rāmānuja's in its hierarchy of souls and their destinies; it must not be treated as a promise that every soul inevitably receives the same end.<Cite source="iep-madhva" locator="§3" /><Cite source="subbarau-madhva-brahmasutra" locator="III.1.21, p. 178" />

Transformation therefore includes disciplined desire and a decisive change in knowledge or divine relation, not simply relief from guilt feelings. Assurance likewise belongs to a school's realization or promise of non-return, rather than a universal Hindu formula. Liberation is Advaita nonduality or, in the theistic schools, enduring God-dependent individual bliss.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="2.12, commentary" /><Cite source="thibaut-ramanuja-sribhashya" locator="IV.4.1–4, 22" /><Cite source="subbarau-madhva-brahmasutra" locator="IV.4.22, pp. 293–294" />

## What this explains well

The remedy reaches the roots of bondage rather than promising endless satisfaction of the desire that produces it. The Gītā's diagnosis explains why moral information alone is weak when desire has entered reason itself. Theistic Vedānta also refuses to make the dependent soul's effort entirely self-sufficient: divine aid is essential to its hope.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="3.38–43" /><Cite source="govindacharya-gita-ramanuja" locator="18.66" /> Internal differences prevent a misleading summary of all Hindu liberation as self-rescue.

## Christian response

The Christian argues that the fundamental disorder is culpable alienation from God, requiring Christ's reconciliation as well as renewed life.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 3:23–26; Ephesians 2:1–10" /> Against Advaita this disputes the priority and ultimate status of misidentification; with theistic Vedānta it disputes the ground of divine release. Knowledge, devotion and divine aid cannot be criticized as though they were one mechanism. The question is whether each remedy answers the real human problem, not whether each reproduces Christian categories.

## Pressure questions

- How can insight or devotion avoid being redirected by the very desire it seeks to overcome?
- Is the final result recognition of nonduality, or the perfected relation of a real distinct soul to God?

## Deep dive

#### Knowledge is not mere information

Śaṅkara's ignorance concerns the Self's misidentification with the changing body and mind, not simply failure to know a moral command.<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra" locator="I.1.1, intro., pp. 3–9" /> Knowing that a particular act is prohibited can therefore coexist with the deeper error. The Christian cannot refute this diagnosis simply by pointing to someone who knows a duty and breaks it. He must explain why rebellion and disordered love should have explanatory priority over the mistaken identity.

The Gītā's discipline also belongs to the remedy. The instruction to govern oneself and the account of wisdom joined to faith and subdued senses are not descriptions of instant insight with no formation.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="6.5–6; 4.38–39" /> Advaita's claim that knowledge is decisive does not make every preparatory practice meaningless. It specifies what removes ignorance, rather than saying every means contributes identical causal work.

#### Divine aid without merging the schools

Rāmānuja's account of the Lord's favor has a different object and structure. Knowledge concerns a supremely dear personal Lord; the soul's own nature is restored rather than ultimate separateness dissolved.<Cite source="thibaut-ramanuja-sribhashya" locator="IV.1.13; IV.4.1–4" /> Devotion and surrender can therefore express dependence, not merely an autonomous effort to produce more favorable karma.

The Gītā's refuge passage is important, but later Śrīvaiṣṇava schemes of surrender should not all be attributed directly to Rāmānuja. The interpretation of his role in those developments is itself discussed by scholarship.<Cite source="iep-ramanuja" locator="§7" /> The anchor uses the checked promise and commentary without silently adding every later account of prapatti. Madhva's dependence on grace likewise should remain distinct from both Advaita and Rāmānuja's particular framework.<Cite source="iep-madhva" locator="§3" />

#### Sin, transformation and permanence

Śaṅkara's qualification about already-fructifying karma prevents a claim that knowledge immediately erases every bodily consequence.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="4.37, commentary, pp. 134–135" /> Rāmānuja's restriction concerning deliberate misconduct similarly prevents turning divine aid into an excuse for persistent wrongdoing.<Cite source="thibaut-ramanuja-sribhashya" locator="IV.1.13" /> Both qualifications matter to whether the proposed transformation matches the diagnosis, though they arise within different accounts.

The final non-return claim is also more than the hope for an improved birth. Rāmānuja grounds it in the Supreme Person's relation to the freed devotee, while Madhva preserves lasting dependent bliss with real distinctions among souls.<Cite source="thibaut-ramanuja-sribhashya" locator="IV.4.22" /><Cite source="subbarau-madhva-brahmasutra" locator="IV.4.22, pp. 293–294" /> The final state and its security cannot be detached from each school's view of the self and Lord.

Christian criticism should therefore proceed separately: whether nondual knowledge adequately addresses personal culpability, and whether theistic divine release requires a satisfaction it does not supply in Christian terms. Those questions depend on the Christian account of justice and personhood, which also requires defense. They do not establish an internal contradiction merely by identifying a different ultimate premise.

An assurance of non-return is not a universal assurance about every currently embodied person's attainment. Rāmānuja's final passage concerns the released devotee and the Lord's relation to that devotee.<Cite source="thibaut-ramanuja-sribhashya" locator="IV.4.22" /> It does not settle every observer's uncertainty about whether another person has attained the relevant knowledge or devotion. Distinguishing permanence of the final condition from knowledge of one's present standing keeps the assurance comparison from importing an unestablished formula.
