## The view

The Vedas, the Upaniṣads, the Bhagavad Gītā and the classical Vedānta commentators contain no teaching about Jesus. Everything Hindus have said about him comes from the modern period, when reformers and teachers read the Gospels and placed Jesus within Hindu frameworks. Their views differ, so there is no single "Hindu view" of Jesus.

Rammohun Roy, the Bengali reformer, published Jesus' moral teachings in 1820 as a "Guide to peace and happiness" and defended that selection in 1823. He revered Jesus as a teacher empowered by God, but argued from God's unity against the Trinity and against Jesus' deity.<Cite source="roy-final-appeal" locator="pp. 3–4; contents, ch. II" /> Miracle stories, he added, would carry no weight in India, where "miracles infinitely more wonderful are related of their gods and saints."<Cite source="roy-final-appeal" locator="p. 17" />

Swami Vivekananda placed Jesus among "the Prophets, the man-Gods, the Incarnations, the embodiments of God," through whom the omnipresent God becomes visible. "If I, as an Oriental, have to worship Jesus of Nazareth, there is only one way left to me, that is, to worship him as God."<Cite source="vivekananda-complete-works-4" locator="Christ, the Messenger" /> But he set history aside: "It does not matter at all whether the New Testament was written within five hundred years of his birth, nor does it matter even, how much of that life is true."<Cite source="vivekananda-complete-works-4" locator="Christ, the Messenger" />

Mohandas Gandhi found that the Sermon on the Mount "went straight to my heart," and he compared it with the Gītā.<Cite source="gandhi-autobiography-desai" locator="I.XX (pp. 63–64)" /> Yet he could not believe that Jesus was "the only incarnate Son of God," or that his blood literally redeemed the world. "I could accept Jesus as a martyr, an embodiment of sacrifice, and a divine teacher, but not as the most perfect man ever born."<Cite source="gandhi-autobiography-desai" locator="II.XV (pp. 126–127)" />

These readings draw on Hindu ideas: that the divine descends "in every age" to restore dharma,<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="4.8 (p. 107)" /> and, for Vivekananda, that great teachers can be worshipped as embodiments of the one God (see <QuestionLink question="revelation" worldview="hinduism">revelation</QuestionLink>).

## What this explains well

The receptions take Jesus' moral teaching and self-giving seriously, and they offer him a reverence that does not depend on joining a church.

## Christian response

These are modern interpretations of the Gospels, not independent ancient evidence about Jesus, and they mostly do not dispute the history. He was crucified under Pontius Pilate, as Paul, the Gospels and the Roman historian Tacitus attest.<Cite source="tacitus-annals-church-brodribb" locator="15.44" /> The disagreement is about meaning, and the question is whether the Hindu frameworks fit the earliest evidence. Within about two decades Jewish followers were praying to Jesus and calling him Lord alongside the one God,<Cite source="hurtado-worship-of-christ" locator="Early devotion to Christ" /> confessing "one God, the Father... and one Lord, Jesus Christ."<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="1 Corinthians 8:6" /> That is a claim of uniqueness within Jewish monotheism, not one manifestation among many. And the earliest preaching rested on a single unrepeatable event, the resurrection, on which faith stands or falls.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="1 Corinthians 15:17" /> Vivekananda's choice to set history aside is candid, but it leaves open whether the Jesus he honours is the one the sources describe.

## Pressure questions

- If Jesus is one incarnation among many, how is that reconciled with his earliest followers' confession of him as the one Lord?
- If history does not matter, as Vivekananda says, what keeps the honoured Jesus tied to the person the sources describe?

## Deep dive

#### Avatāra and incarnation

The Gītā's divine descents recur in every age, whenever dharma declines.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="4.7–8 (p. 107)" /> For Śaṅkara the Lord's birth is only an appearance (see <QuestionLink question="revelation" worldview="hinduism">revelation</QuestionLink>).<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="4.6 (pp. 106–107)" /> Christianity claims something different in kind: that the Word "became flesh," really and once for all.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="John 1:14" /> Calling Jesus an avatar can therefore obscure both traditions. Theistic Vedāntins such as Rāmānuja hold that the Lord's descents are real, which brings them closer to Christian language, but they too treat descent as repeatable.

#### Three reformers, three frameworks

Roy wrote as a Unitarian-minded reformer of the Brahmo movement, not as a representative of classical Vedānta; his argument is with Trinitarian doctrine, and he insists that the precepts of Jesus are a sufficient guide.<Cite source="roy-final-appeal" locator="pp. 3–4" /> Vivekananda, speaking in Los Angeles in 1900, argued that a powerful "nucleus" must lie behind the Gospels even if their details are uncertain, and that great teachers are worshipped as embodiments of God.<Cite source="vivekananda-complete-works-4" locator="Christ, the Messenger" /> Gandhi called Jesus' death on the cross "a great example to the world" while rejecting the belief that his blood literally redeemed it.<Cite source="gandhi-autobiography-desai" locator="II.XV (pp. 126–127)" /> None of the three disputes that Jesus lived and died. Each reinterprets what his life means.
