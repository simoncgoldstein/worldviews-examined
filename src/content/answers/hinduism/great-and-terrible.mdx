## The view

Hindu traditions read the human being as an embodied self (ātman) whose nature is conscious. In theistic Vedānta that self also belongs to God: Rāmānuja reads the Gītā's "portion of Myself" as the real, eternal soul.<Cite source="govindacharya-gita-ramanuja" locator="7.5; 15.7" /> A Vedic text marks the human as "most endowed with knowledge," the creature who "by means of the mortal ... desires the immortal."<Cite source="muller-upanishads-1" locator="Aitareya Āraṇyaka II.3.2.4" /> Wrongdoing arises when that self, bound in a body-mind made of three strands (guṇas), is overrun by desire. Arjuna asks why a person sins "though reluctant, as if constrained by force"; Krishna answers that desire and wrath veil wisdom through the senses, the mind and even reason.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="3.36–40" />

The schools explain the root of that disorder differently. Advaita, especially Śaṅkara, traces the root to a beginningless superimposition of body and mind onto the Self.<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra" locator="I.1.1, intro., pp. 3–4" /> For Rāmānuja the soul's innate knowledge is "contracted" by karma, and the soul stays answerable for its own choices.<Cite source="thibaut-ramanuja-sribhashya" locator="I.1.1, p. 63; II.3.41, p. 557" /> For both, greatness and wickedness are two states of one self: its native light, and that light obscured. Madhva's Dvaita instead spreads them partly across innately different souls. The remedies range from liberating knowledge (Advaita) to devotion and surrender to God (Rāmānuja, Madhva).

## What this explains well

The Gītā names the puzzle of doing what one knows is wrong and gives it a psychology: desire lodges even in reason, so knowing better is not enough, and the wise, Śaṅkara adds, foresee where desire leads and are "ever miserable."<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="3.39, comm." /> The guṇa analysis explains why civilization and domination share a root, since rajas drives both productive work and insatiable craving.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="14.7–10, comm." /> And because the self's worth lies in its nature rather than its record, even one "of flagitiously wicked ways" who turns to God "speedily becomes ... a holy soul."<Cite source="govindacharya-gita-ramanuja" locator="9.30–31, comm." />

## Christian response

The Christian recognizes the Gītā's desire seated even in reason as close to Augustine's mind that "commands itself, and is resisted."<Cite source="augustine-confessions" locator="VIII.9.21" /> Two school-specific points follow.

First, Advaita's root ignorance is not ignorance of duties. It is the Self's misidentification with body and mind, an error with "its cause in wrong knowledge."<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra" locator="I.1.1, intro., pp. 4, 9" /> One can know a prohibition and still break it for a misconceived self, so knowing wrongdoing fits the diagnosis. The Christian disputes the order, not the phenomenon. He traces evil to a will that made itself "a kind of end to itself";<Cite source="augustine-city-of-god" locator="XIV.13" /> love comes first, and the false self-image serves it. Error that yields to discipline and insight favours Advaita's order; resistance born of preferring the flattering self favours the Christian's. The dispute concerns an ultimate premise.

Second, theistic Vedānta has a personal Lord and real guilt. For Rāmānuja, evil deeds consist in "displeasing the Supreme Person,"<Cite source="thibaut-ramanuja-sribhashya" locator="II.2.3, p. 487" /> and the Lord, "demanding no price," frees whoever takes refuge in him.<Cite source="govindacharya-gita-ramanuja" locator="18.66, p. 565" /> The Reformed premise is that justice must be satisfied, not only waived, and that God himself put forward the propitiation: Christ bearing guilt in his people's place (see <QuestionLink question="guilt" worldview="christianity">the Christian account of guilt</QuestionLink>).<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 3:25" /> A Vedāntin can reply that the Lord may justly remit his own displeasure. This is a Christian theological disagreement, not a demonstrated deficiency.

## Pressure questions

- If desire lodges even in reason, what keeps the knowledge or discipline offered as the cure from being steered by the same desire?
- For Advaita: is a wrong done to another person something someone answers for ultimately, or only at the conventional level?
- For Rāmānuja and Madhva: when the Lord removes his displeasure at sin, what makes that removal just as well as merciful?

## Deep dive

#### The shared text: Gītā 3, 14 and 16

The Gītā is common ground for the Vedānta schools. Desire, which turns into wrath when blocked, covers wisdom "as fire is surrounded by smoke, as a mirror by rust," and its seats are "the senses, mind, and reason"; the remedy is to subdue "the self by the self."<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="3.36–43" /> Chapter 14 sets this in a cosmology of three guṇas: sattva brings lucidity, rajas "thirst and attachment," tamas a heedlessness that "covers the judgment."<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="14.6–9, comm." /> Chapter 16 sets "the divine nature" (harmlessness, truth, compassion, forgiveness) beside the "demoniac," and names "lust, wrath, and greed" the "gate to hell."<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="16.1–8, 16.21" /> Agency stays real: "he alone is the friend of himself, he alone is the enemy of himself."<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="6.5–6" />

#### Advaita: superimposition and two levels

Śaṅkara defines superimposition (adhyāsa) as "a natural procedure" by which we merge Self and not-Self and say "That am I," "That is mine."<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra" locator="I.1.1, intro., pp. 3–4" /> Even men "distinguished by superior intelligence" proceed like animals here, and this error is "the cause of all evil" (*anartha*: harm or calamity).<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra" locator="I.1.1, intro., pp. 7–9" /> At the conventional level, karma, agency and moral discipline are fully real. Ultimately only nondual Brahman is real, so Śaṅkara reads the Gītā's "ray of Myself" as "only an imaginary portion,"<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="15.7, comm., pp. 361–362" /> and liberation, by knowledge alone, dissolves the sense of individual agency.<Cite source="sep-shankara" locator="§§4.1, 4.3" /> Yoga and Nyāya likewise put ignorance first: it is the soil of the other afflictions<Cite source="woods-yoga-sutra" locator="YS 2.3–9" /> and the worst of the faults.<Cite source="vidyabhusana-nyaya-sutras" locator="NS 1.1.2; 4.1.6" />

#### Rāmānuja and Madhva: dependent agents

For Rāmānuja, God the inner Ruler acts only by granting "permission (anumati)" to the soul's own "volitional effort," so the fruit "properly belongs to him only." Those resolved on what displeases God are given "a delight in such actions" as their punishment, which explains why wickedness escalates.<Cite source="thibaut-ramanuja-sribhashya" locator="II.3.40–41, pp. 556–558" /> Scholars debate how far the later doctrine of surrender (prapatti) is Rāmānuja's own.<Cite source="iep-ramanuja" locator="§7" /> Madhva holds that God alone is independent; souls are real, eternal and dependent agents, like a carpenter under a master, impelled "according to ... his previous actions and his effort."<Cite source="subbarau-madhva-brahmasutra" locator="II.3.40–42, pp. 145–146" /> His tradition teaches a hierarchy of souls with innate virtues and faults, including some "for ever immersed in absolute misery," a contested and sensitive doctrine.<Cite source="subbarau-madhva-brahmasutra" locator="III.1.21, p. 178" /> Liberation depends on Viṣṇu's grace.<Cite source="iep-madhva" locator="§3" />

#### Dignity across the schools

For Advaita the dignity is the Self itself, the same "in a dog and in a dog-eater" as in a learned Brahmin.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="5.18" /> For Rāmānuja, souls are "fundamentally alike and ... equal" as knowers.<Cite source="iep-ramanuja" locator="§7" /> For Madhva, dignity is real but dependent and graded. The classical record also ranks persons by caste. Śaṅkara argues that śūdras are not qualified for Vedic knowledge of Brahman, though he grants that knowledge they gain otherwise still bears fruit.<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra" locator="I.3.34–38, pp. 223–229" /> The Gītā promises the supreme goal to women, vaiśyas and śūdras who take refuge, though translators construe its phrase rendered "of a sinful birth" differently.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="9.32" />

#### A Christian note on school differences

Rāmānuja's "delight in such actions" given as punishment stands close to Paul, for whom God "gave them up" to their desires;<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 1:24, 28" /> both see escalating sin as judgment, not only habit. The response's first point therefore does not apply to Rāmānuja or Madhva, who trace wrongdoing to a dependent agent's own effort under a personal Lord. With these schools the disagreement concerns the ground of forgiveness, not whether sin is personal.
