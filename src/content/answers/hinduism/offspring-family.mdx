## The view

In classical Hindu teaching family belongs to dharma and the stages of life (*āśramas*). The Taittirīya Upaniṣad's parting instructions to the student include: "Do not cut off the line of children," and treat mother, father, teacher and guest as gods.<Cite source="muller-upanishads-2" locator="Taittirīya I.11.1–3, pp. 52–53" /> *Manusmṛti* calls the householder the most excellent order, because students, forest-dwellers and ascetics all live by his support.<Cite source="buhler-manu" locator="III.77–78" /> Parents are images of the creator Prajāpati and of the earth, and their pains in raising a child cannot be repaid.<Cite source="buhler-manu" locator="II.225–228" />

Offspring also repay a debt. A man owes three debts, to the sages by study, to the ancestors by sons, and to the gods by sacrifice, and only after paying them should he set his mind on liberation.<Cite source="buhler-manu" locator="VI.35–37" /> When he sees his grandchildren he may withdraw to the forest.<Cite source="buhler-manu" locator="VI.1–2" />

Yet renunciation stands above the household. The Bṛhadāraṇyaka says the ancient sages "did not wish for offspring," asking what they would do with children when the Self was their world.<Cite source="muller-upanishads-2" locator="Bṛhadāraṇyaka IV.4.22, pp. 179–180" />

## What this explains well

The account gives family a cosmic and social role: it sustains the generations and every other way of life. It honours parents profoundly. And it holds household duty and renunciation together in one sequence.

## Christian response

The Christian shares the honour of parents and the sense of family as a sacred trust. The difference is that Christianity treats marriage as a creation good in its own right, not a stage to be left behind.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Genesis 2:18–24" /> The Christian also notes that dharmaśāstra's gender provisions are disputed among Hindus themselves.

## Pressure questions

- If the householder supports all other orders, why is renunciation the higher stage?
- What grounds the equal dignity of women where classical texts subordinate them?

## Deep dive

#### Manu's authority and hard provisions

*Manusmṛti* is one dharmaśāstra among several, and its authority varies among Hindus. Its text contains provisions modern Hindu reformers reject. It says a woman "must never be independent," subject first to father, then husband, then sons,<Cite source="buhler-manu" locator="V.147–148" /> and it sets marriage ages that include child brides.<Cite source="buhler-manu" locator="IX.94" /> The same text also says that where women are honoured the gods rejoice, and where they are not, all rites are fruitless.<Cite source="buhler-manu" locator="III.55–59" />

#### Love "for the Self"

Yājñavalkya teaches that a husband, a wife or a son is dear not for their own sake but "for the love of the Self."<Cite source="muller-upanishads-2" locator="Bṛhadāraṇyaka II.4.5, pp. 109–110" /> Advaita reads this as pointing to the one Self in all. Śaṅkara reads even the Gītā's "desire unopposed to dharma" narrowly, as desire for what bare bodily sustenance requires.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="7.11, commentary" /> Household desire is legitimate, but it is not the goal.
