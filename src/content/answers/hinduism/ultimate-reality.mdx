## The view

The Upaniṣads answer this question with one word: Brahman. "In the beginning," a father teaches his son, "there was that only which is, one only, without a second," and that Being thought, "may I be many."<Cite source="muller-upanishads-1" locator="Chāndogya VI.2.1–3, pp. 93–94" /> Brahman is that from which beings are born, by which they live, and into which they return.<Cite source="muller-upanishads-2" locator="Taittirīya III.1, p. 64" /> The Brahma Sūtra makes this its definition: Brahman is that from which the world's origin, subsistence and dissolution proceed.<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra" locator="I.1.2, pp. 15–16" />

The schools of Vedānta agree on that much: ultimate reality is not exhausted by the world we see, the world depends on it at every stage, and the round of rebirth has no first beginning. They disagree sharply about how real the world's plurality is.

**Advaita** (Śaṅkara): Brahman alone is ultimately real. The many relate to Brahman as pots relate to clay; the variety is name and form, while "the truth is that it is clay merely."<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra" locator="II.1.14, pp. 320–324" /> This does not mean the world is simply unreal. Śaṅkara insists that "the entire complex of phenomenal existence is considered as true as long as the knowledge of Brahman being the Self of all has not arisen." Until then, ordinary life goes on undisturbed.<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra" locator="II.1.14, pp. 320–324" />

**Viśiṣṭādvaita** (Rāmānuja): Brahman is the one personal Lord, and all souls and matter are his body. They are real, but they never exist apart from him, as a body never acts apart from its soul.<Cite source="thibaut-ramanuja-sribhashya" locator="I.1.1, p. 140" />

**Dvaita** (Madhva): Viṣṇu alone is independent. Souls and matter are eternally and really different from him, and wholly dependent on him.<Cite source="subbarau-madhva-brahmasutra" locator="I.1.17, pp. 21–22" />

## What this explains well

The tradition holds together the world's dependence and its unity: everything comes from, rests in and returns to one source. It places consciousness at the root of reality rather than at its edges. And Advaita gives a subtle account of how the ordinary world can be fully binding in practice yet not final.

## Christian response

The Christian agrees that the world depends on a reality it does not exhaust; the disagreement is about the relation. For Advaita the world is the appearance of Brahman; for Rāmānuja it is Brahman's body; for Madhva it is distinct but beginningless. Reformed Christianity holds instead that the world is a creature, neither God's appearance nor his body, made from nothing by his free will.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Revelation 4:11" /> That is a theological disagreement grounded in competing revelations.

The Christian also raises a question of explanatory adequacy for Advaita. If Brahman alone is real, where does the ignorance that projects plurality belong? Rāmānuja himself pressed this objection from within Vedānta. The individual soul cannot hold the ignorance, he argued, because the soul exists only through it; and Brahman, being pure knowledge, cannot hold it either.<Cite source="thibaut-ramanuja-sribhashya" locator="I.1.1, p. 103" /> Advaita has replies. Śaṅkara calls the superimposition that constitutes ignorance natural and without beginning, so there was never a self that first existed free of it and then fell into it.<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra" locator="I.1.1, intro., pp. 6, 9" /> He says that the world's causal potentiality, which is of the nature of ignorance and is destroyed by knowledge, has the highest Lord, the world's creator, for its substratum, and that as *māyā* it "cannot be defined either as that which is or that which is not."<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra" locator="I.4.3, p. 243" /> And to the question of who lacks true knowledge he answers, "You yourself who ask this question!" The question, that is, arises within the empirical standpoint, where the individual self is real enough to be ignorant.<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra-2" locator="IV.1.3, p. 340" /> Later Advaitins disputed whether ignorance is located in the individual self or in Brahman.<Cite source="sep-shankara" locator="§3.4" /> These replies meet the charge of outright incoherence, though whether they succeed remains disputed within Vedānta; what the Christian presses is a question of explanation. For Madhva, whose view is closest to the Christian distinction, the question is what grounds the existence of souls and matter if the Lord did not create them.

## Pressure questions

- In Advaita, if ignorance has no beginning and cannot be defined either as being or as non-being, does that explain how the one Brahman appears as many, or mark the point where explanation stops?
- In Dvaita, if souls and matter have always existed, in what sense does their existence, rather than only their activity, depend on the Lord?

## Deep dive

#### Shared images

The Upaniṣads describe the world's relation to Brahman in images that each school can claim. "As the spider sends forth and draws in its thread," says the Muṇḍaka, "thus does everything arise here from the Indestructible."<Cite source="muller-upanishads-2" locator="Muṇḍaka I.1.7" /> The image says that the world is not made from an outside material, and that it returns to its source. It does not settle whether the thread is real in its own right, which is exactly what the schools dispute.

#### Two levels in Advaita

Śaṅkara distinguishes the standpoint of ordinary experience from the standpoint of ultimate truth.<Cite source="sep-shankara" locator="§2.3" /> At the first, the world is real, the Lord (Īśvara) is its omniscient and omnipotent cause, and karma and duty are binding.<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra" locator="I.1.2, pp. 15–16" /> At the second, only Brahman is. Śaṅkara compares the world to a dream, which is taken as real until the dreamer wakes. The point of the comparison is the provisional validity of the dream, not its nothingness.<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra" locator="II.1.14, pp. 320–324" /> The Śvetāśvatara Upaniṣad calls nature *māyā* and the great Lord its wielder, the *māyin*, and Advaita draws heavily on that language.<Cite source="muller-upanishads-2" locator="Śvetāśvatara IV.10, p. 252" />

#### The body of God

Rāmānuja reads the same Chāndogya text differently. Brahman is in its "causal" state when its body of souls and matter is subtle, and in its "effected" state when that body is gross and differentiated. The effect is therefore non-different from the cause, yet souls and matter are not unreal.<Cite source="thibaut-ramanuja-sribhashya" locator="I.1.1, p. 140" /> Souls and matter are related to Brahman as a body to the soul that animates and controls it.

#### Madhva's differences

Madhva's commentary insists that Scripture declares the difference between the Lord and the soul. When a scriptural text calls the Lord "all," Madhva cites another that explains it: the Lord who is not all is called all because he rules all.<Cite source="subbarau-madhva-brahmasutra" locator="I.1.17, pp. 21–22" /> His school's list of five eternal differences is set out in <QuestionLink question="one-and-many" worldview="hinduism">the one and the many</QuestionLink>.
