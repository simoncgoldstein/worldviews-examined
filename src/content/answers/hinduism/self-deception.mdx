## The view

The Gītā distinguishes an intellect which misjudges duty from one so enveloped in darkness that it takes wrong for right.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="18.31–32" /> Desire can also veil wisdom through the senses, mind, and understanding.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="3.38–40" /> Thus one can retain some knowledge while allowing attachment to direct its use. In Śaṅkara's broader account, ordinary identification of Self and not-Self supplies a deep form of misrecognition.<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra" locator="I.1.1, intro., pp. 3–9" /> That is Advaita's account, not every Hindu school's.

## What this explains well

These distinctions explain both acting against recognized wisdom and mistakenly calling the wrong action one's duty. They also make room for the knowledgeable person's awareness that desire is leading him astray.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="3.39, comm." /> Self-deception need not abolish every true judgment before it affects conduct.

## Christian response

The Christian agrees that desire can compromise self-examination; Calvin gives self-love that role.<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="II.1.2" /> Hindu traditions plainly recognize rationalization; the deeper disagreement concerns what is misidentified and what correction ultimately restores. For Śaṅkara the root misidentification is of Self with not-Self; Rāmānuja preserves real dependent selves rather than resolving plurality into one Self.<Cite source="govindacharya-gita-ramanuja" locator="2.12, comm." /> Reformed theology locates the distortion in a creature's resistance to God: what is suppressed is knowledge of the Creator, not the self's identity with Brahman. Similar accounts of biased judgment leave that disagreement about the creature's ultimate identity untouched.

## Deep dive

#### Corrupted discernment and ultimate ignorance

The Gītā's classifications concern the quality of judgment, not simply the quantity of information. Misunderstanding the relation of duty and non-duty differs from consistently reversing their meaning. Both are relevant to a person whose reasons appear convincing to himself.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="18.31–32" />

Śaṅkara's superimposition is a wider diagnosis: the ordinary person attributes features of not-Self to Self and appropriates what is not truly his identity.<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra" locator="I.1.1, intro., pp. 3–9" /> The doctrine is not the same as every everyday lie one tells oneself; it names a background of mistaken identification, which need not be consciously intended.
