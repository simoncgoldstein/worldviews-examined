## The view

The body dies, while the self is not born or killed in the same manner. The Gītā compares bodies to exchanged clothing and describes the embodied self passing through changes and further embodiment.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="2.13, 2.20–22, 2.27" /> The Upaniṣadic account connects desire, intention and action with the condition to come; its caterpillar image depicts a transition to another support.<Cite source="muller-upanishads-2" locator="Bṛhadāraṇyaka IV.4.3–5" /> Rebirth therefore concerns karmically conditioned continuity, rather than an arbitrary second life.

Survival is not itself liberation. Good or bad consequences can sustain further existence within saṃsāra. Freedom concerns release from that bondage, and its meaning depends on the school. Śaṅkara reads the Gītā's apparent plurality of selves as plurality of bodies, not ultimate plurality of Self. Rāmānuja instead defends real enduring individual selves.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="2.12, commentary" /><Cite source="govindacharya-gita-ramanuja" locator="5.16, pp. 182–183" />

Advaita liberation is thus not the infinite continuation of one's present separate personality. Theistic Vedānta retains the liberated soul's distinction in blissful dependence on the Lord. Rāmānuja describes the manifestation of the soul's own nature and non-return; Madhva likewise affirms non-return while retaining differing forms of blessedness.<Cite source="thibaut-ramanuja-sribhashya" locator="IV.4.1–4, 22" /><Cite source="subbarau-madhva-brahmasutra" locator="IV.4.22, pp. 293–294" /> The common word moksha covers real differences about who finally lives and in what relation.

## What this explains well

The account places death within a larger continuity while distinguishing longevity from freedom. It also connects future condition with conduct instead of simply proposing another destination after the current body fails. The final no-return claim explains why rebirth is not itself the highest consolation.<Cite source="thibaut-ramanuja-sribhashya" locator="IV.4.22" /> Because the schools differ over individuality, the comfort each offers differs too.

## Christian response

Christianity places each person's one life before God, and resurrection raises that same person into transformed bodily life rather than into a further series of karmic births.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="1 Corinthians 15:35–49" /><Cite source="wcf" locator="32.2–3" /> Rebirth spreads a moral history across many lives, with continuity carried by the self and its karma rather than by memory or body. Forgetting does not break that continuity: the self that acted is the self that receives the result, as one becomes what one does, and the Vedānta commentators hold that each being's conditions answer to its own prior merit and demerit, not to arbitrary assignment.<Cite source="muller-upanishads-2" locator="Bṛhadāraṇyaka IV.4.5" /><Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra" locator="II.1.34–36" /> Christianity, too, does not make memory the ground of identity. The Christian's question is therefore not whether the sufferer is the same self but what kind of justice this is. In Christian judgment the person gives account for his own deeds before the Judge;<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 14:12; 2 Corinthians 5:10" /> karmic fruition falls on someone who cannot recognize what it answers, so it can neither be owned in repentance nor seen to be just by the one who bears it. The Hindu reply is that fruition is the working out of the self's own action, not a verdict that needs the agent's recognition to be fitting. That is a disagreement about whether desert must be recognizable to be moral justice rather than lawful consequence, not a proof that rebirth breaks personal identity. Against Advaita, the issue also concerns whether personal distinction is ultimate. With theistic Vedānta, the disagreement is over the history and remedy of bondage. Both rebirth and resurrection require warrant, and a Christian preference for resurrection does not by itself refute rebirth.

## Pressure questions

- Bādarāyaṇa holds that the fruits of action come from the Lord, who gives them with a view to the deeds done.<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra-2" locator="III.2.41, p. 183" /> Does the Lord's part add anything to the deed's own desert, such as a verdict or mercy, or does he only see that each deed meets its fruit?
- Does liberation preserve the individual, or remove the misconception of ultimate individuality?

## Deep dive

#### Continuity does not mean unchanged biography

The Gītā's undying self is not a claim that every memory, preference and social role travels unchanged into another body. The Upaniṣadic passage links future condition with desire and action, while its transition image concerns the self's movement to a new form.<Cite source="muller-upanishads-2" locator="Bṛhadāraṇyaka IV.4.3–5" /> It does not describe every psychological feature of the next life.

The Hindu self also differs from Buddhist conditioned rebirth without an enduring ātman. Those accounts can both reject death as the final terminus of ordinary existence while disagreeing fundamentally over continuity's bearer. Calling both "reincarnation" hides that contrast.

#### Liberation and the present body

Śaṅkara distinguishes karma whose results have not yet begun from karma already producing the present body. Knowledge renders the former impotent while the latter runs its course.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="4.37, commentary, pp. 134–135" /> This explains why liberation's knowledge need not mean immediate physical disappearance or immunity from every embodied consequence. It also prevents treating present suffering as sufficient proof that knowledge has not occurred.

Rāmānuja describes the released soul's own nature becoming manifest once karmic obscuration ends; release is not merely a new pleasurable body within the same cycle.<Cite source="thibaut-ramanuja-sribhashya" locator="IV.4.1–4" /> His final non-return passage grounds permanence in the Supreme Person's relation to the devotee, not simply in an individual having accumulated enough favorable births.<Cite source="thibaut-ramanuja-sribhashya" locator="IV.4.22" /> Madhva likewise preserves lasting dependent bliss, with distinctions among liberated souls.<Cite source="subbarau-madhva-brahmasutra" locator="IV.4.22, pp. 293–294" />

The comparisons therefore concern different accounts of identity, dependence and remedy. <QuestionLink question="final-end" worldview="hinduism">Final destiny</QuestionLink> explains the final good by school; <QuestionLink question="self-salvation" worldview="hinduism">the liberation anchor</QuestionLink> explains knowledge, devotion and divine aid. <QuestionLink question="guilt" worldview="hinduism">Guilt</QuestionLink> separates karmic consequences from divine displeasure. No single account of intermediate heavens and hells, or of popular afterlife practice, covers every Hindu tradition.
