## The view

In the Vedānta traditions a human being is an embodied self. The self (*ātman*; *jīva* as individual soul) is conscious, unborn and undying. As it passes through childhood, youth and age in one body, says the Gītā, so it passes to another, casting off worn-out bodies as a person changes clothes.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="2.13, 2.20–22" /> The Kaṭha Upaniṣad says the knowing self is neither born nor killed when the body is killed.<Cite source="muller-upanishads-2" locator="Kaṭha I.2.18–19, p. 11" />

Body and mind belong to *prakṛti*, nature made of three strands (*guṇas*). The conscious self, seated in nature, experiences the strands, and attachment to them causes its births in good and evil wombs.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="13.21" /> Karma carries the self from life to life. Humans do not differ in kind from other beings: the same self is present "in a dog and in a dog-eater" as in a learned Brahmin.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="5.18" /> What distinguishes the human birth is opportunity. A Vedic text calls the human "most endowed with knowledge,"<Cite source="muller-upanishads-1" locator="Aitareya Āraṇyaka II.3.2.4" /> and Śaṅkara calls human birth rare and precious because it is the means to life's aims and to liberation.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="9.33, commentary" />

What the self finally is divides the schools. For Śaṅkara's Advaita the Self is pure consciousness, the same in all bodies.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="2.12, commentary" /> Individual agency arises from a beginningless superimposition of body and mind onto it.<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra" locator="I.1.1, intro., pp. 3–4" /> For Rāmānuja souls are real, eternal and many, "portions" of the Lord, their knowledge only contracted by karma.<Cite source="govindacharya-gita-ramanuja" locator="7.5; 15.7" /><Cite source="thibaut-ramanuja-sribhashya" locator="I.1.1, p. 63" /> For Madhva, souls are eternally distinct from God and from each other, real agents wholly dependent on him.<Cite source="subbarau-madhva-brahmasutra" locator="II.3.40–42, pp. 145–146" />

## What this explains well

The account explains the sense that the one who experiences is not any changing state: the same "I" is present in childhood and age. It treats consciousness as basic, not as something to be explained away. It gives one framework for kinship with animals while marking the human birth as uniquely suited to spiritual effort. And its schools give argued positions on whether individuality is ultimate, a question many traditions leave vague.

## Christian response

The Christian agrees that the person is more than the body's changing states and that consciousness is not an illusion to be explained away. The disagreement is over the origin and standing of the self. Scripture presents the human being as a created, embodied creature with a beginning,<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Genesis 2:7" /> whose future is bodily resurrection rather than release from bodies.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="1 Corinthians 15:35–49" /> The body is not a garment but part of what a human is. These are theological disagreements.

With Advaita, the Christian presses a question of explanatory adequacy. If individuality belongs only to the conventional level, what grounds the personal responsibility and love that the conventional level fully recognizes? Advaita answers with its two levels of truth; the Christian asks whether that preserves the person or postpones the question. With Rāmānuja and Madhva the issue is different: their eternal, dependent souls stand closer to the Christian creature, and the debate concerns whether souls are created or beginningless. The moral side of this anthropology is treated in <QuestionLink question="great-and-terrible" worldview="hinduism">the flagship</QuestionLink>.

## Pressure questions

- If the body is clothing the self leaves behind, does the self keep anything of the life lived in it, such as the love and duties formed there, or only the karma it carries forward?
- In Advaita, who is it that is bound by ignorance and who is liberated, if the Self was never bound?
- If souls are beginningless, what makes each one the particular soul it is?

## Deep dive

#### The shared Gītā picture

The Gītā opens its teaching with the distinction between the embodied self and the body. Bodies end; the embodied one is eternal, indestructible and immeasurable.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="2.13, 2.20–22" /> Chapter 13 develops the distinction as "field" and "knower of the field": nature produces body and mind, and the conscious knower experiences them.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="13.21" /> Krishna calls the living self in the world "a portion of Myself," eternal, which draws the mind and senses to itself.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="15.7" /> All three schools accept these verses and read them differently.

#### Advaita

Śaṅkara reads "portion of Myself" as "only an imaginary portion."<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="15.7, commentary, pp. 361–362" /> The individual soul is the one Self appearing under the limiting conditions of body and mind. At the conventional level, karma, agency and rebirth are fully real, and moral discipline matters. Ultimately, though, liberating knowledge removes the misconception of separateness, and the sense of individual agency is dissolved.<Cite source="sep-shankara" locator="§§4.1, 4.3" />

#### Viśiṣṭādvaita

For Rāmānuja, the Lord is the inner controller of souls, who are real and many and knowers by nature. Karma contracts their knowledge,<Cite source="thibaut-ramanuja-sribhashya" locator="I.1.1, p. 63" /> and in liberation it is manifested in full, though the released soul never acquires God's power to govern the world.<Cite source="thibaut-ramanuja-sribhashya" locator="IV.4.1–4, 17" /> As agents they act by their own effort, which God permits and sustains, so the fruits of action are properly theirs.<Cite source="thibaut-ramanuja-sribhashya" locator="II.3.40–41, pp. 556–558" /> Souls are, in this account, fundamentally alike as knowers.<Cite source="iep-ramanuja" locator="§7" />

#### Dvaita

Madhva holds that God alone is independent. Souls are real, eternal and dependent agents, impelled by God according to their previous actions and effort.<Cite source="subbarau-madhva-brahmasutra" locator="II.3.40–42, pp. 145–146" /> His tradition also teaches that souls differ in innate nature and destiny, a hierarchy that includes souls permanently bound.<Cite source="subbarau-madhva-brahmasutra" locator="III.1.21, p. 178" /> This is a contested doctrine within Hindu thought; dignity here is real but dependent and graded.

#### Body, birth and social rank

Because the self is the same in all bodies, differences of species and birth belong to the body, not the self.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="5.18" /> The classical record nonetheless ranks persons by caste and birth for purposes of ritual qualification, as <QuestionLink question="great-and-terrible" worldview="hinduism">the flagship</QuestionLink> discloses. Many modern Hindus reject those rankings while keeping the anthropology of the embodied self. Sexual difference likewise belongs to the body; its social meaning in the household is treated in <QuestionLink question="offspring-family" worldview="hinduism">the family question</QuestionLink>.
