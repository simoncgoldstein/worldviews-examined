## The view

The Gītā describes pleasure and pain arising through sense-contact and passing away; its instruction is endurance rather than mistaking a present feeling for the whole of reality.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="2.13–14" /> Yoga identifies a wider instability in conditioned existence: change, anxiety, and the effects of prior dispositions make even apparently pleasant conditions vulnerable to pain.<Cite source="woods-yoga-sutra" locator="II.15–16" /> Suffering therefore is not exhausted by the physical sensation of a current injury.

Vedānta accounts also relate unequal conditions to karma. The Brahma Sūtra commentaries answer the charge of divine partiality and cruelty: the Lord, as dispenser, gives beings their conditions according to their prior merit and demerit, not by arbitrary distribution.<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra" locator="II.1.34–36" /><Cite source="thibaut-ramanuja-sribhashya" locator="II.1.34–35, pp. 477–479" /> Pain and pleasure alike are fruits of action that come from the Lord, who alone can requite deeds that pass away as soon as they are done.<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra-2" locator="III.2.38–41, pp. 180–183" /> The framework explains inequality in general. It does not identify the deed behind any particular person's suffering.

## What this explains well

The account links conduct, attachment, and enduring consequences. It also recognizes that pleasure is unstable and that habits shape future vulnerability. Yoga's instruction to avoid future pain makes practical intervention intelligible, rather than merely declaring that the present condition must be welcomed.<Cite source="woods-yoga-sutra" locator="II.16" /> The significance of this account changes by school: Advaita places mistaken identification with body and mind at the center; theistic Vedānta retains the real soul and its dependence.

## Christian response

The Christian agrees that attachment and wrongdoing can increase suffering and that outward comfort is not ultimate security. The disagreement concerns the larger order. In the theistic reading karma is not a blind mechanism, since the Lord dispenses its fruits, and Vedānta defends the account from scripture and from justice: otherwise souls would be requited for what they had not done.<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra" locator="II.1.36" /> The Christian denies that unequal conditions must be desert, and so asks whether that argument holds when no sufferer's earlier deeds are observable and the explanation reaches back without beginning (see <QuestionLink question="evil" worldview="hinduism">the evil anchor</QuestionLink>). Christianity reads creation's disorder through sin and providence, and looks to the deliverance of creation rather than to a history of merit.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 8:19–22" /> The two Vedānta schools also meet the comparison differently. For Advaita, suffering belongs to the self's mistaken identification with body and mind; for Rāmānuja, a real, dependent soul suffers under the Lord's government, a picture closer to the Christian one.<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra" locator="I.1.1, intro., pp. 3–9" /><Cite source="thibaut-ramanuja-sribhashya" locator="II.3.40–41, pp. 556–558" /> Neither the existence of suffering nor an unknown karmic history settles which account has the better warrant.

## Pressure questions

- If the Lord dispenses pain and pleasure according to merit and demerit, is all suffering requital, or could some serve another purpose, such as purification or another's good, without injustice?
- What permits judging the sufferer's responsibility when the relevant history is not known?

## Deep dive

#### Endurance, explanation, and the self

The Gītā's instruction to endure transient pleasure and pain concerns one's response to experience. It is not itself a complete causal account of every illness, accident, or act of violence. Yoga's wider account adds the instability of conditions and the role of afflictions.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="2.14" /><Cite source="woods-yoga-sutra" locator="II.15; 2.3–9" /> Response and causal explanation are different things.

Advaita's account of self-identification also operates at a different level. The ordinary identification of Self with what belongs to body and mind is the background error which Śaṅkara's introductory discussion seeks to expose.<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra" locator="I.1.1, intro., pp. 3–9" /> That analysis does not make an ordinary sufferer's pain unreal: it is real within experience, whatever is said of its ultimate status.

Theistic Vedānta's real dependent selves change the comparison: the soul's agency and divine permission are not dissolved into the same nondual account.<Cite source="thibaut-ramanuja-sribhashya" locator="II.3.40–41, pp. 556–558" /> Not every Hindu position denies a real sufferer.
