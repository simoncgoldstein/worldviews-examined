## The view

The Gītā describes pleasure and pain arising through sense-contact and passing away; its instruction is endurance rather than mistaking a present feeling for the whole of reality.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="2.13–14" /> Yoga identifies a wider instability in conditioned existence: change, anxiety, and the effects of prior dispositions make even apparently pleasant conditions vulnerable to pain.<Cite source="woods-yoga-sutra" locator="II.15–16" /> Suffering therefore is not exhausted by the physical sensation of a current injury.

Vedānta accounts also relate unequal conditions to karma. The Brahma Sūtra commentaries address divine partiality and cruelty by relating differences to beings' prior actions rather than an arbitrary distribution.<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra" locator="II.1.34–36" /> This is a framework for explaining inequality, not available evidence identifying the exact prior deed behind a particular person's suffering. The page does not claim knowledge of such an individual karmic history.

## What this explains well

The account links conduct, attachment, and enduring consequences. It also recognizes that pleasure is unstable and that habits shape future vulnerability. Yoga's instruction to avoid future pain makes practical intervention intelligible, rather than merely declaring that the present condition must be welcomed.<Cite source="woods-yoga-sutra" locator="II.16" /> The significance of this account changes by school: Advaita places mistaken identification with body and mind at the centre; theistic Vedānta retains the real soul and its dependence.

## Christian response

The Christian agrees that attachment and wrongdoing can increase suffering and that outward comfort is not ultimate security. The disagreement concerns the grounds of the larger order. Karma is not a complete explanation until the comparison asks what warrants and governs its connection between actions and conditions. Advaita's superimposition and Rāmānuja's real dependent agent also raise different questions.<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra" locator="I.1.1, intro., pp. 3–9" /><Cite source="thibaut-ramanuja-sribhashya" locator="II.3.40–41, pp. 556–558" /> Christianity instead interprets creation's disorder through sin and divine providence.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 8:19–22" /> That contrast needs argument; neither the existence of suffering nor an unknown karmic history by itself establishes one account.

## Pressure questions

- What grounds the karmic order rather than merely naming the connection it is meant to explain?
- What permits judging the sufferer's responsibility when the relevant history is not known?

## Deep dive

#### Endurance, explanation, and the self

The Gītā's instruction to endure transient pleasure and pain concerns one's response to experience. It is not itself a complete causal account of every illness, accident, or act of violence. Yoga's wider account adds the instability of conditions and the role of afflictions.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="2.14" /><Cite source="woods-yoga-sutra" locator="II.15; 2.3–9" /> Response and causal explanation should not be treated as interchangeable.

Advaita's account of self-identification also operates at a different level. The ordinary identification of Self with what belongs to body and mind is the background error which Śaṅkara's introductory discussion seeks to expose.<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra" locator="I.1.1, intro., pp. 3–9" /> This does not warrant saying that an ordinary sufferer's pain is simply unreal in every relevant sense. The conventional experience and the proposed ultimate analysis must be distinguished.

Theistic Vedānta's real dependent selves change the comparison: the soul's agency and divine permission are not dissolved into the same nondual account.<Cite source="thibaut-ramanuja-sribhashya" locator="II.3.40–41, pp. 556–558" /> A Christian response which assumes all Hindu positions deny a real sufferer would therefore miss an important internal distinction.

Here the sources support a bounded explanation of attachment, instability, and karmic ordering. A general karmic explanation does not identify the particular prior actions of an individual victim.
