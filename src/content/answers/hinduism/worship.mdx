## The view

Hindu worship takes many forms, from temple offerings to inward meditation. The Gītā gives the principle behind them. Krishna says that whoever offers him with devotion a leaf, a flower, a fruit or water, he accepts that offering of love. Whatever one does, eats, offers or gives, and whatever austerity one practises, should be done as an offering to him.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="9.26–27" /> Worship here is not confined to ritual; all of life can become offering.

The Gītā also asks which worshippers are best. Arjuna contrasts those who worship the Lord in his personal form with those who meditate on the Imperishable, the unmanifest absolute. Krishna answers that those who fix their minds on him with faith are the most perfect in yoga; those who worship the Imperishable also reach him, but their path is harder for embodied beings.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="12.1–5" />

The schools read this differently. Śaṅkara ranks devotees of the personal Lord as excellent, but holds that knowers of the Imperishable are one with the Lord.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="12.3–5, commentary, pp. 265–268" /> For Advaita, worship of a personal God is valid and valuable at the conventional level, and it prepares the mind for liberating knowledge. For Rāmānuja, devotion is the soul's highest relation to the Lord. He reads the offering of a leaf as impassioned love that the Lord himself enjoys,<Cite source="govindacharya-gita-ramanuja" locator="9.26" /> and teaches that the Lord chooses his lover and himself generates that love in the devotee.<Cite source="govindacharya-gita-ramanuja" locator="8.14, pp. 272–273" />

## What this explains well

The account explains how devotion can be both humble and intense: a simple offering made with love is accepted. It makes room for many temperaments and stages, from ritual to contemplation. And in Rāmānuja it explains devotion as reciprocal, the Lord's love preceding and evoking the devotee's.

## Christian response

The Christian recognizes in Rāmānuja's teaching a striking parallel to grace: love for God that God himself initiates. Scripture says, "We love because he first loved us."<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="1 John 4:19" />

The disagreements are two. First, Hindu practice ordinarily allows worship of many deities and of God through images, which the Christian reads as the transfer of worship to created forms that the second commandment forbids.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Exodus 20:3–6" /> Many Hindus answer that these are forms through which the one divine is approached; the disagreement is whether God may be worshipped through such forms. Second, in Advaita devotion to a personal Lord is finally transcended in knowledge of the impersonal Self. The Christian holds that God is personal at the deepest level, so worship is never outgrown. Both are theological disagreements.

## Pressure questions

- If, for Advaita, worship of the personal Lord prepares for a knowledge in which worshipper and worshipped are no longer two, is the love it expresses finally real?
- What makes an offering pleasing to the Lord: the devotion of the giver or the Lord's choice of the devotee?
- How does one distinguish worshipping the one divine through an image from worshipping the image?

## Deep dive

#### Two paths in Gītā 12

Gītā 12 is the classic text for comparing devotion and contemplation. Śaṅkara defines worship (*upāsanā*) as approaching an object of meditation and dwelling on it in a continuous current of thought. In his reading, devotees of the personal Lord are excellent yogins, while those devoted to the unmanifest Imperishable are said to be one with the Lord, though on a path harder for those identified with the body.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="12.1–5, pp. 265–268" /> His reading preserves both the value of devotion and the priority of knowledge.

#### Rāmānuja's devotion

Rāmānuja reads the same chapters as a teaching on love. The Lord, accompanied by Śrī, makes himself accessible for worship. The Lord is said to choose his lover and to generate the love by which he is approached.<Cite source="govindacharya-gita-ramanuja" locator="proem; 8.14, pp. 272–273; 9.26" /> This gives Viśiṣṭādvaita a strong doctrine of divine initiative. Scholars debate how far later teaching on surrender (*prapatti*) develops beyond Rāmānuja's own writings.<Cite source="iep-ramanuja" locator="§7" />

#### Images and many deities

Temple worship with consecrated images (*mūrti*), the theology of the Lord's presence in them, and the ritual traditions that govern it are widespread but are not researched here and are described only generally. Many Hindus understand the many deities as forms or powers of one supreme reality, while others worship a particular deity as supreme. Madhva's tradition adds that liberation depends on Viṣṇu's grace.<Cite source="iep-madhva" locator="§3" /> The self who worships, and the schools' differing accounts of it, are described in <QuestionLink question="what-is-man" worldview="hinduism">the human-nature anchor</QuestionLink>.
