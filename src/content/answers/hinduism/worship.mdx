## The view

Hindu worship takes many forms, from temple offerings to inward meditation. The Gītā gives the principle behind them. Krishna says that whoever offers him with devotion a leaf, a flower, a fruit or water, he accepts that offering of love. Whatever one does, eats, offers or gives, and whatever austerity one practises, should be done as an offering to him.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="9.26–27" /> Worship here is not confined to ritual; all of life can become offering.

The Gītā also asks which worshippers are best. Arjuna contrasts those who worship the Lord in his personal form with those who meditate on the Imperishable, the unmanifest absolute. Krishna answers that those who fix their minds on him with faith are the most perfect in yoga; those who worship the Imperishable also reach him, but their path is harder for embodied beings.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="12.1–5" />

The schools read this differently. Śaṅkara ranks devotees of the personal Lord as excellent, but holds that knowers of the Imperishable are one with the Lord.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="12.3–5, commentary, pp. 265–268" /> For Advaita, worship of a personal God is valid and valuable at the conventional level, and it prepares the mind for liberating knowledge. For Rāmānuja, devotion is the soul's highest relation to the Lord. He reads the offering of a leaf as impassioned love that the Lord himself enjoys,<Cite source="govindacharya-gita-ramanuja" locator="9.26" /> and teaches that the Lord chooses his lover and himself generates that love in the devotee.<Cite source="govindacharya-gita-ramanuja" locator="8.14, pp. 272–273" /> The Bhāgavata Purāṇa, the great scripture of Krishna devotion, makes devotion the measure of all religion: the highest duty is the one from which devotion to the Lord arises, devotion that seeks nothing in return and that nothing interrupts.<Cite source="subbarau-bhagavata-1" locator="I.2.6, p. 6" />

Much of this worship is offered before a consecrated image (*mūrti*), and the schools explain it differently. Śaṅkara mentions images only in passing, as an analogy. Where scripture bids one meditate on the sun as Brahman, he says, the thought of the higher reality is laid on the lower object, as one honours a king's charioteer as the king, "analogously as a contemplation on Vishnu is superinduced on his images"; Brahman, the ruler of all, grants the fruit.<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra-2" locator="IV.1.5, pp. 342–345" /> Śrīvaiṣṇava teaching says more about the image itself. For Piḷḷai Lokācārya, in his thirteenth-century summary, the image is one of the Lord's own forms, his "image descent" (*arcāvatāra*). In it the all-powerful Lord takes the name and shape his worshippers choose and, as if dependent on them, becomes visibly present in temples and homes.<Cite source="govindacharya-artha-pancaka" locator="Para-svarūpa I 5, p. 577; Sanskrit version, p. 601" /> To think images inert and powerless is an error against God, and the worshipper is to regard the image as the deity, not as wood, metal, stone or clay (our paraphrase of the Sanskrit).<Cite source="govindacharya-artha-pancaka" locator="Virodhi O 2, p. 589; Sanskrit version, p. 601" />

## What this explains well

The account explains how devotion can be both humble and intense: a simple offering made with love is accepted. It makes room for many temperaments and stages, from ritual to contemplation. In Rāmānuja it explains devotion as reciprocal, the Lord's love preceding and evoking the devotee's. And the theology of the image explains how worship can be bodily and local without, in the worshipper's understanding, being directed at matter.

## Christian response

The Christian recognizes in Rāmānuja's teaching a striking parallel to grace: love for God that God himself initiates. Scripture says, "We love because he first loved us."<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="1 John 4:19" />

The disagreements are two. First, images. The Śrīvaiṣṇava does not mistake stone for God; he holds that the Lord has chosen to be present in the image. The Reformed objection therefore turns on two other questions. Who is worshipped? The first commandment allows no god but the LORD who brought Israel out of Egypt, whom the Christian does not identify with Viṣṇu or any other Hindu deity. How is he worshipped? The second forbids images made to be bowed down to, even of the true God: Israel, having seen no form at Horeb, was to make none,<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Exodus 20:3–6; Deuteronomy 4:15–16" /> and the golden calf was condemned though its feast was proclaimed in the LORD's name.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Exodus 32:4–5" /> Westminster accordingly forbids worshipping an image "or God in it or by it."<Cite source="westminster-larger-catechism" locator="Q. 109" /> The Śrīvaiṣṇava concedes neither premise, holding on his own scriptures' authority that the Lord he worships is supreme and has chosen this descent. That is a theological disagreement grounded in competing revelatory authorities. Second, in Advaita devotion to a personal Lord is finally transcended in knowledge of the impersonal Self. The Christian holds that God is personal at the deepest level, so worship is never outgrown. That too is a theological disagreement.

## Pressure questions

- If, for Advaita, worship of the personal Lord prepares for a knowledge in which worshipper and worshipped are no longer two, is the love it expresses finally real?
- What makes an offering pleasing to the Lord: the devotion of the giver or the Lord's choice of the devotee?
- If worshippers choose the name and form in which the Lord is approached, what shows that he has consented to dwell in that image, rather than that their devotion alone has made it holy?

## Deep dive

#### Two paths in Gītā 12

Gītā 12 is the classic text for comparing devotion and contemplation. Śaṅkara defines worship (*upāsanā*) as approaching an object of meditation and dwelling on it in a continuous current of thought. In his reading, devotees of the personal Lord are excellent yogins, while those devoted to the unmanifest Imperishable are said to be one with the Lord, though on a path harder for those identified with the body.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="12.1–5, pp. 265–268" /> His reading preserves both the value of devotion and the priority of knowledge.

#### Rāmānuja's devotion

Rāmānuja reads the same chapters as a teaching on love. The Lord, accompanied by Śrī, makes himself accessible for worship. The Lord is said to choose his lover and to generate the love by which he is approached.<Cite source="govindacharya-gita-ramanuja" locator="proem; 8.14, pp. 272–273; 9.26" /> This gives Viśiṣṭādvaita a strong doctrine of divine initiative. Scholars debate how far later teaching on surrender (*prapatti*) develops beyond Rāmānuja's own writings.<Cite source="iep-ramanuja" locator="§7" />

#### Images and many deities

In Piḷḷai Lokācārya's scheme the image is the furthest reach of the Lord's accessibility, not a rival to his transcendence: it comes after his transcendent form, his emanations, his descents and his indwelling of souls. The Sanskrit version distinguishes images that manifest themselves from those installed by gods, by sages or by human beings.<Cite source="govindacharya-artha-pancaka" locator="Para-svarūpa I 1–5, p. 576; Sanskrit version, p. 601" /> A Śrīvaiṣṇava verse quoted in the translator's notes compares the Lord's presence in all things to fire latent in wood, and his special presence in the image to fire in a red-hot iron ball.<Cite source="govindacharya-artha-pancaka" locator="Appendix V, p. 594" />

The Bhāgavata adds a warning from within devotion itself. Speaking as the Lord, the sage Kapila says that he dwells in all beings; one who neglects him there and worships only the image throws his offerings into ashes, and image-worship by one who scorns other beings does not please him. Yet the devotee is to go on worshipping until he finds the Lord in his own heart. Vīrarāghava's Viśiṣṭādvaita commentary reads the rebuke as aimed at ignorant worshippers, not at image worship as such.<Cite source="subbarau-bhagavata-1" locator="III.29.21–25, pp. 359–360, with note" /> Image worship is not only Vaiṣṇava: Śaiva Siddhānta's root text directs the freed soul to worship the forms in the temples as Śiva's form.<Cite source="nallaswami-sivajnanabodham" locator="sūtra 12" />

Many Hindus understand the many deities as forms or powers of one supreme reality, while others worship a particular deity as supreme. Madhva's tradition adds that liberation depends on Viṣṇu's grace.<Cite source="iep-madhva" locator="§3" /> The self who worships, and the schools' differing accounts of it, are described in <QuestionLink question="what-is-man" worldview="hinduism">the human-nature anchor</QuestionLink>; devotion that places love of the Lord above liberation itself is described in <QuestionLink question="final-end" worldview="hinduism">the final end</QuestionLink>.
