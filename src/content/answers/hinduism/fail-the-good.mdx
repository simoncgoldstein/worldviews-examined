## The view

Arjuna asks why a person acts wrongly even against his intention. The Gītā answers with desire and anger, born of *rajas*, the quality associated with restless activity. Desire veils knowledge through the senses, mind, and understanding; the response is to govern the senses and overcome it.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="3.36–43" /> Śaṅkara makes the conflict especially sharp: the wise person can recognize desire as an enemy even before suffering its consequences.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="3.39, comm." /> Wrongdoing, then, is not simply ignorance of what is right.

## What this explains well

The account connects attention, attachment, and loss of judgment. The Gītā's sequence runs from dwelling on an object through desire and anger to delusion and ruin.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="2.62–63" /> Rāmānuja adds the force of past habit: desire grows sharper through repetition and turns to anger when blocked.<Cite source="govindacharya-gita-ramanuja" locator="3.37, comm." /> It explains why intending improvement does not immediately undo established appetites.

## Christian response

The Christian recognizes this entanglement of knowledge and desire; Calvin also distinguishes misunderstanding a particular case from knowingly choosing wrong.<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="II.2.23" /> The deeper dispute differs by school. Śaṅkara's ultimate diagnosis is the mistaken identification of Self with not-Self; Rāmānuja keeps a real, dependent agent whose effort matters.<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra" locator="I.1.1, intro., pp. 3–9" /><Cite source="thibaut-ramanuja-sribhashya" locator="II.3.40–41, pp. 556–558" /> If the deepest root is a misidentification, as for Śaṅkara, the final remedy is knowledge. Reformed theology locates the root in a creature's will turned against God, which suppresses what it knows and so is not cured by knowledge alone.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 1:18–21" /> Rāmānuja's dependent agent stands closer to that picture, and the disagreement with him concerns how such an agent is restored (see <QuestionLink question="self-salvation" worldview="hinduism">salvation and liberation</QuestionLink>). The shared moral psychology does not settle these theological premises.

## Pressure questions

- What must be corrected when someone already recognizes desire as harmful but still follows it?

## Deep dive

#### Veiled knowledge is not absent knowledge

The Gītā can call desire a veil without claiming that the wrongdoer lacks every true judgment. Śaṅkara's commentary on 3.39 distinguishes the wise person, who knows desire's danger beforehand, from the ignorant person who treats it as a friend until suffering teaches otherwise.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="3.39, comm." /> The same vocabulary therefore covers more than one relation between knowledge and action. Recognizing a danger does not yet mean that desire has lost its practical influence.

Rāmānuja's emphasis on past habits makes that influence concrete. His reading of 3.37 follows the obstruction facing a person already seeking the path of knowledge, rather than making every lapse evidence that no sincere aspiration exists.<Cite source="govindacharya-gita-ramanuja" locator="3.36–37, comm." /> Elsewhere he affirms the soul's volitional effort under God's permission, retaining responsibility rather than treating the person as an inert instrument.<Cite source="thibaut-ramanuja-sribhashya" locator="II.3.40–41, pp. 556–558" />

These shared accounts of temptation sit alongside a deep disagreement over what the self ultimately is. For that distinction and the different roles of knowledge, devotion, and grace, see <QuestionLink question="great-and-terrible" worldview="hinduism">the flagship anthropology</QuestionLink>.
