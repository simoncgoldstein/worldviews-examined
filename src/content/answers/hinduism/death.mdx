## The view

The Bhagavad Gītā distinguishes bodily death from the self's destruction: the self is not born or slain, while bodies are exchanged and death is certain for what has been born.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="2.20; 2.22; 2.27" /> The Kaṭha Upaniṣad likewise presents the knowing self as undying.<Cite source="muller-upanishads-2" locator="Kaṭha I.2.18–19, p. 11" /> These texts explain mortality through a distinction between the embodied life that ends and a self whose existence does not end with it.

Rebirth is more than continued survival. The Bṛhadāraṇyaka connects the move to another embodied state with desire, action and its fruit.<Cite source="muller-upanishads-2" locator="Bṛhadāraṇyaka IV.4.3–5, pp. 175–176" /> The problem is recurring embodied existence under this condition, so simply acquiring another body is not the final answer. School differences determine what ultimately endures: Śaṅkara reads the Gītā's plural language through one Self, while Rāmānuja defends real, enduring plurality.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="2.12, commentary" /><Cite source="govindacharya-gita-ramanuja" locator="5.16, pp. 182–183" />

## What this explains well

This account distinguishes the loss of a particular embodied life from the destruction of the self, and locates the deeper problem in recurring existence rather than annihilation. That changes what it means to call mortality wrong. It also explains why prolonging life alone cannot resolve the human problem. In the Kaṭha narrative, Naciketas refuses wealth, pleasures and long life as substitutes for an answer about the dead.<Cite source="muller-upanishads-2" locator="Kaṭha I.1.20–29, pp. 5–7" /> Mortality becomes an occasion to ask what is lasting and what sort of existence is actually worth seeking.

## Christian response

The Christian also refuses to equate bodily death with the whole significance of the person, but he values the body differently. The body is not a garment to be exchanged but part of the person, which is why death is an enemy and the hope is bodily resurrection, not release from embodiment.<Cite source="wcf" locator="32.1–2" /><Cite source="bible-esv" locator="1 Corinthians 15:26" /> The disagreement therefore concerns the truth of rebirth, the identity of the self and the worth of embodied life. It takes different forms with the two schools. Against Advaita, the question is whether individual persons are ultimately real. With Rāmānuja, who affirms enduring individuals, it turns on rebirth and on what embodiment means for the final state.

## Pressure questions

- What warrants the distinction between bodily death and an undying self?
- Does the ultimate answer preserve real individual selves or transcend their apparent plurality?

## Deep dive

#### Persistence does not settle identity

The Gītā's image of replacing worn-out clothes can make the body/self distinction intelligible, but an image does not by itself decide the metaphysical character of the self.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="2.22" /> Śaṅkara's explanation of 2.12 specifically denies that the plural wording establishes many ultimate selves. Rāmānuja appeals to the same verse in defending real plurality, not merely plurality caused by temporary limiting conditions.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="2.12, commentary" /><Cite source="govindacharya-gita-ramanuja" locator="5.16, pp. 182–183" /> That disagreement changes the answer to what a person's death ultimately leaves intact.

The Kaṭha's refusal of substitute gifts also qualifies a superficial immortality reading. A long and pleasurable earthly life is precisely what fails to answer Naciketas's question. The issue is knowledge concerning death and the self, not simply extending the same sequence of desires. The Bṛhadāraṇyaka's account then connects the next condition with desire and action; the continuing cycle supplies the setting in which mortality and bondage are related.

The Gītā's instruction concerning grief does not make bereavement trivial for Hindu traditions. Its argument addresses the difference between the perishing body and the enduring self within a particular teaching. Recognizing that distinction still leaves substantial questions about attachment and the value of embodied relationships.
