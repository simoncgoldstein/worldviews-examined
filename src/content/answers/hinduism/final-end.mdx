## The view

The highest end in the Vedānta traditions is liberation from saṃsāra, not simply another favorable life or a temporary heavenly reward. The self's survival of bodily death leaves the bondage question unanswered; desire and action can sustain further births.<Cite source="muller-upanishads-2" locator="Bṛhadāraṇyaka IV.4.3–5" /> The shared word moksha therefore names release, while its precise meaning depends on the account of self and God.

For Advaita, liberating knowledge removes the misconception of ultimate separation: the Self is nondual, and plurality of bodies is not ultimate plurality of Self.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="2.12, commentary" /><Cite source="sep-shankara" locator="§§4.1, 4.3" /> The goal is not an independently existing individual accumulating pleasures forever. Nor is it the destruction of an originally real soul.

Rāmānuja instead describes the soul's own true nature becoming manifest, released from karmic obscuration and enjoying the Supreme Person. Its knowledge and bliss are perfected, while it does not acquire God's universal power to govern the world. The Lord does not return the beloved devotee to bondage.<Cite source="thibaut-ramanuja-sribhashya" locator="IV.4.1–4, 17, 22" />

Madhva preserves real distinction among God and souls, with lasting dependent bliss and differing forms of blessedness among the liberated.<Cite source="subbarau-madhva-brahmasutra" locator="IV.4.22, pp. 293–294" /> His tradition's distinctions of soul destiny also mean that liberation is not the same inevitable outcome for all souls.<Cite source="subbarau-madhva-brahmasutra" locator="III.1.21, p. 178" /> These school differences are essential to the question of man's final end.

## What this explains well

The contrast between survival and freedom is searching: continued existence would not be the final good if the conditions generating bondage remained. Rāmānuja's account adds positive bliss to release from misery, while retaining divine dependence.<Cite source="thibaut-ramanuja-sribhashya" locator="IV.4.1–4, 22" /> The school distinctions make clear that liberation cannot be discussed adequately without asking whether personal individuality is finally real.

## Christian response

Christianity affirms perfected creaturely distinction and embodied communion with God.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="1 John 3:1–3; Revelation 21:1–4" /> With Advaita the contrast concerns ultimate individuality. The Christian holds that the creature's distinctness from God is not a misconception but the condition of love and communion, perfected in glory rather than removed. Theistic Vedānta also preserves the soul's distinction and dependent bliss, so the agreement there is closer, and the comparison concerns the ground and character of that dependence: for the Christian, communion secured by Christ in a renewed bodily creation (see <QuestionLink question="final-end" worldview="christianity">the Christian answer</QuestionLink>). A shared preference for release or bliss does not make these the same final good.

## Pressure questions

- Does liberation disclose the absence of ultimate separateness, or perfect a real distinct person?
- What makes freedom permanent rather than another temporary favorable condition?

## Deep dive

#### Nonduality is not the death of a real soul

Advaita's diagnosis concerns the mistaken identification of Self with the changing body and mind. Removing that error is not supposed to kill an entity that had previously existed with ultimate independence.<Cite source="thibaut-shankara-brahmasutra" locator="I.1.1, intro., pp. 3–9" /> A Christian may reject the nondual premise, but calling the goal annihilation would describe it in categories it disputes. The question is whether distinct personhood is ultimately real, not whether Advaita seeks a longer period of ordinary private enjoyment.

Discipline and the continuing present body also need not contradict knowledge's decisive role. Śaṅkara allows already-fructifying karma to run its course.<Cite source="sastri-gita-shankara" locator="4.37, commentary, pp. 134–135" /> Release from bondage is therefore not a promise that every bodily condition disappears immediately.

#### Positive bliss and dependence

Rāmānuja's account of manifestation is not mere absence of pain. The released soul's own nature is no longer obscured by karma, and its fulfillment concerns the Supreme Person.<Cite source="thibaut-ramanuja-sribhashya" locator="IV.4.1–4" /> The explicit limitation on world-governing power preserves the difference between the Lord and the liberated creature; the non-return passage grounds security in divine favor as well as completed release.<Cite source="thibaut-ramanuja-sribhashya" locator="IV.4.17, 22" />

Madhva's differing forms of blessedness likewise preserve plurality within freedom.<Cite source="subbarau-madhva-brahmasutra" locator="IV.4.22, pp. 293–294" /> His difficult teaching about souls destined for lasting misery is a specifically Dvaita distinction, not a claim about all Hindus.<Cite source="subbarau-madhva-brahmasutra" locator="III.1.21, p. 178" /> Not every school, then, promises every soul the same eventual liberation.
