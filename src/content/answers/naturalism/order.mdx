## The view

Nature behaves regularly: stones fall, planets orbit, chemicals combine in fixed proportions. Naturalists take this regularity as a basic feature of the world, something science discovers and describes. They do not take it as evidence of a mind behind the world.

Hume set the terms of the modern discussion. When we watch one event cause another, he argued, we never observe a hidden power linking them. Events "seem conjoined, but never connected."<Cite source="hume-enquiry" locator="7.26" /> Science can reduce many regularities to a few general principles, but "these ultimate springs and principles are totally shut up from human curiosity and enquiry."<Cite source="hume-enquiry" locator="4.12" />

What, then, are laws of nature? Naturalists give different answers.<Cite source="sep-laws-of-nature" locator="§§2–3, 5" />

- **Humeans** hold that laws are the simplest, most informative summary of everything that actually happens. Laws describe the pattern; they do not make it happen.
- **Non-Humeans** hold that laws reflect real necessities in nature, either relations between properties or the powers things have.
- A minority are **antirealists**, who treat talk of laws as a useful tool rather than a description of reality.

For the order of living things, naturalists point to natural selection, which produces the appearance of design without a designer. Hume's Philo had already suggested that matter in motion might settle into a stable, self-maintaining order.<Cite source="hume-dialogues" locator="8.6" />

## What this explains well

The view fits the actual practice of science, which discovers regularities and does not need to know why they hold. It explains biological order by a well-tested mechanism. And it avoids the picture of laws as commands needing a commander.

## Christian response

The Christian agrees that the order of nature is real and is discovered, not invented. The question is whether naturalism explains that order or simply accepts it. For a Humean, the regularity of the world is the last word: laws summarize the pattern and do not account for it.<Cite source="sep-laws-of-nature" locator="§§2, 8" /> That leaves unexplained why the pattern continues, which every inference from past to future assumes. Non-Humean necessities go further, but they are posits whose own source is unexplained. The Christian holds that the faithfulness of a wise Creator, who speaks of his "fixed order of heaven and earth,"<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Jeremiah 33:25" /> explains the order better. That is a claim of comparative explanatory adequacy, resting on theistic premises, not a charge of contradiction. Responses to cosmic fine-tuning, such as positing a multiverse, raise the same question at a larger scale.

## Pressure questions

- If laws only summarize the whole pattern of what actually happens, what gives us reason, before tomorrow arrives, to expect the part we have seen to continue?
- If laws are real necessities in nature, where do those necessities come from?

## Deep dive

#### Systems, universals and powers

The "best system" view, associated with David Lewis, says a law is any regularity that belongs to the simplest and strongest true summary of the world.<Cite source="sep-laws-of-nature" locator="§2" /> David Armstrong and others argue that laws are relations of necessitation between universal properties.<Cite source="sep-laws-of-nature" locator="§3" /> Dispositional essentialists locate necessity in the powers of things themselves. The debate matters, because only non-Humeans claim that laws *explain* regularities. For a Humean, explaining a regularity by a law is explaining it by a more general regularity.<Cite source="sep-laws-of-nature" locator="§8" />

#### Philo on order and necessity

In Hume's *Dialogues*, Philo compares the order of nature to a curious property of numbers. The digits of any multiple of 9 add up to a multiple of 9. A superficial observer might admire this as chance or design, but a mathematician sees it is necessary. Perhaps, Philo suggests, the order of the universe is necessary in the same way.<Cite source="hume-dialogues" locator="9.10" /> Elsewhere he sketches matter that, by continual motion, falls into a self-sustaining "œconomy" of forms.<Cite source="hume-dialogues" locator="8.6" />

#### Fine-tuning

Physicists have found that some constants and early conditions seem to need narrow values for life to be possible. Naturalist responses vary. Some deny that improbable fine-tuning requires any response, or expect new physics to remove it.<Cite source="sep-fine-tuning" locator="§§2.2–2.3" /> Some argue that observers could only find themselves in a life-permitting universe.<Cite source="sep-fine-tuning" locator="§3.2" /> Others posit many universes with different values.<Cite source="sep-fine-tuning" locator="§4.1" /> The multiverse response is contested even among naturalists.

#### No lawgiver

Carroll warns against reasoning as though "the reliability of such laws implies the existence of a legislative body or a law-enforcement agency." He treats this as a metaphor from everyday life that may not extend to reality as a whole.<Cite source="carroll-something-rather-than-nothing" locator="§5" />
