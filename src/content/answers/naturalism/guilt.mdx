## The view

Naturalism supplies no single doctrine of guilt. A naturalistic realist such as Railton can regard wrongdoing as an objective failure toward others; an anti-realist offers a different account of moral authority. The origin of remorse does not settle that disagreement. Darwin describes an enduring social instinct returning after a stronger temporary impulse has prevailed, producing backward-looking moral distress.<Cite source="railton-moral-realism" locator="pp. 190–203" /><Cite source="darwin-descent" locator="ch. IV, pp. 113–114" />

The practical distinction is between having done wrong and feeling bad. A quiet conscience does not vindicate an injury, while disproportionate self-accusation does not create one. A responsible response seeks an accurate account, acknowledges harm, makes available repair and accepts warranted consequences. Forgiveness concerns how a wrong is subsequently held against a wrongdoer; it need not excuse the act or deny its culpability.<Cite source="sep-forgiveness" locator="§§1–2" />

No divine absolution is required for one person to forgive another. But an offender's wish to move on cannot determine whether the victim forgives, whether a court punishes, or whether trust is restored. These responses have different agents and purposes. Self-forgiveness is itself debated, especially when it seems to claim authority over a wrong done to someone else.<Cite source="sep-forgiveness" locator="§§2.4–3, 5.1" />

## What this explains well

This approach keeps attention on the injured person and on changes the wrongdoer can actually make. It avoids treating prolonged misery as equivalent to moral repair. The distinction between forgiveness and reconciliation also lets one relinquish hostility while retaining boundaries where renewed association would be harmful.<Cite source="sep-forgiveness" locator="§2.5" /> Forgiveness need not be a demand that the vulnerable person restore every relationship.

## Christian response

The Christian grants that secular apology and forgiveness can be real moral achievements. The disagreement concerns guilt's ultimate scope: Scripture treats wrongs against neighbors as also answerable to their Creator, and Christ's redemption addresses that judgment.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 3:23–26" /> That claim speaks to a gap secular forgiveness leaves open. When the direct victim is dead or unreachable, no human party has standing to forgive, and the wrong is never released; on the Christian account every wrong is also against God, who can forgive it. A naturalist who rejects such a tribunal will reject the need for its pardon, and the Christian has to give warrant for it. The comparison is one of adequacy: secular forgiveness is real and possible, but its reach ends where the injured party's does.

## Pressure questions

- When repair is impossible, what would a proportionate release from blame involve?
- Who has standing to forgive a wrong whose direct victim can no longer respond?

## Deep dive

#### Remorse is evidence, not the verdict

Darwin's account helps explain why conscience can accuse after temptation has passed: the social disposition remains when the shorter-lived impulse subsides.<Cite source="darwin-descent" locator="ch. IV, pp. 113–114" /> That history supplies a mechanism, not an automatic test of rightness. Communities can form mistaken standards, and a person may feel no remorse for a real injury. The realist and anti-realist dispute belongs to <QuestionLink question="know-the-good" worldview="naturalism">moral knowledge</QuestionLink>; on either view, acknowledged wrongdoing calls for the responses described above.

Repair and accountability are also distinguishable. Returning stolen property can correct part of a loss without making the theft permissible. Punishment can remain warranted even if a victim forgives, and punishment by itself does not establish that a relationship has healed. Conversely, no general principle says that greater suffering by the offender yields greater restitution to the victim. The moral task is not an exchange of pain for innocence.

#### The authority to release a claim

Contemporary secular accounts differ over whether forgiveness primarily changes emotion, judgment, treatment or interpersonal obligations.<Cite source="sep-forgiveness" locator="§§4.1–4.2" /> They are not one unified naturalist position. Their common challenge is to preserve acknowledgment of wrongdoing while explaining how its continuing hold can appropriately change.

The strongest Christian pressure concerns injuries beyond any human agent's power to repair, not whether human forgiveness can occur without God. A murdered person's relationships and lost future are not returned by the perpetrator's remorse. Mortalism accepts that some moral losses remain finally unrectified; a Christian claims resurrection and judgment promise a larger setting for justice.<Cite source="russell-what-i-believe" locator="ch. 1" /><Cite source="bible-esv" locator="2 Corinthians 5:10" /> That promise requires its own warrant. The contrast is developed in <QuestionLink question="after-death" worldview="naturalism">after death</QuestionLink>, while <QuestionLink question="self-salvation" worldview="naturalism">the human improvement anchor</QuestionLink> considers whether finite repair is the appropriate aim.
