## The view

Naturalism explains the human mixture as what one should expect from a clever, social, partly altruistic animal, wholly part of nature and shaped by biological and cultural evolution. Darwin argued that any animal with strong social instincts would acquire a conscience once its intelligence approached ours.<Cite source="darwin-descent" locator="ch. IV, p. 98" /> Human greatness has the same roots: teaching, imitation and cooperation let each generation keep and improve on what the last discovered, so tools, science, art and institutions accumulate.<Cite source="tennie-ratchet" locator="Abstract" /> Love, loyalty and self-sacrifice are genuine expressions of these instincts.

But the instincts are limited. Sympathy is strongest for one's own group and competes with self-interest and status. In Hume's image, there is "some particle of the dove" in us, "along with the elements of the wolf and serpent."<Cite source="hume-epm" locator="9.4" /> Knowingly doing wrong follows: a strong impulse in the moment overrides more lasting concerns, and remorse comes afterward.<Cite source="darwin-descent" locator="ch. IV, pp. 113–114" /> Naturalists disagree whether morality is a human device for counteracting limited sympathies (Mackie)<Cite source="mackie-ethics" locator="p. 108" /> or a matter of what would be approved if everyone's interests counted equally (Railton);<Cite source="railton-moral-realism" locator="p. 190" /> either way, the remedy is gradual.

## What this explains well

Because greatness and terribleness share one source, the account predicts their particular shape. Generosity is strongest toward kin and friends, heroism and cruelty cluster along group lines, and the loyalty and courage that inspire sacrifice in war are favoured in competition between groups.<Cite source="darwin-descent" locator="ch. V, p. 130" /> Comparison with other apes explains a distinctive human pairing: far less fighting within groups than chimpanzees show, yet coalitions able to intimidate, expel or kill.<Cite source="wrangham-two-types" locator="Resolving the Rousseau–Kropotkin vs. Hobbes–Huxley Debate" /> It gives testable accounts of weakness of will,<Cite source="sep-weakness-will" locator="§3.4.2" /> and treats moral progress as real but fragile: "an uneven secular trend," not a guarantee.<Cite source="railton-moral-realism" locator="pp. 193–194" />

## Christian response

The Christian can grant these mechanisms; nothing in Christian theology depends on denying them. The disagreement lies at two deeper points.

The first concerns moral authority. Railton's "sensible knave" admits that his conduct is unjust and asks, "But what is that to me?"<Cite source="railton-moral-realism" locator="p. 169" /> Railton keeps him obligated but grants that not every rational agent has a reason to comply.<Cite source="railton-moral-realism" locator="p. 201" /> Whether a duty applies, whether one has reason to do it, and whether one is moved thus come apart. The Christian asks what makes a duty that gives its subject no reason authoritative over him, rather than merely true of him. On the Christian view the law expresses the will of a personal God who is himself the good, so that what one ought to do and one's deepest end coincide.<Cite source="westminster-larger-catechism" locator="Q. 1" /> Naturalist realists contest this.

The second concerns equal dignity. Some capacity-based accounts, weighting interests by strength or setting autonomy thresholds, can leave the most severely impaired with lesser standing,<Cite source="sep-cognitive-disability" locator="§3" /> though other accounts seek more inclusive grounds.<Cite source="sep-cognitive-disability" locator="§4.1" /> The Christian locates worth in a status God confers: murder is forbidden "for God made man in his own image."<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Genesis 9:6" /> That commitment is equally substantive. The claim is comparative: a status, unlike a capacity, does not come in degrees.

## Pressure questions

- If the knave is obligated but has no reason to comply, what makes the obligation authoritative over him?
- What secures equal standing for those with the least capacity, such as infants or people with advanced dementia?
- If remorse is a social instinct reasserting itself, what makes it accurate rather than merely strong?

## Deep dive

#### Which naturalism?

Naturalism has no creed. The view represented here holds that human beings are wholly natural organisms and that knowledge of them is continuous with the sciences.<Cite source="sep-naturalism" locator="§1; §2" /> Its account of human nature runs from Hume through Darwin to contemporary evolutionary ethics and moral psychology, and it is shared by naturalists who disagree sharply about morality. Three questions should be kept apart: how moral capacities evolved, what we ought to do, and what moral claims are. Answering the first does not settle the other two.<Cite source="sep-morality-biology" locator="§1.1" />

#### Hume and Darwin: a mixed constitution

For Hume, reason alone cannot move us to act; only a contrary passion can hold back a passion.<Cite source="hume-treatise" locator="2.3.3.4" /> Justice exists because human generosity is "confin'd" and goods are scarce.<Cite source="hume-treatise" locator="3.2.2.18" /> He saw the gap between knowing and doing plainly: "Men often act knowingly against their interest," and strength of mind is the calm passions prevailing over the violent.<Cite source="hume-treatise" locator="2.3.3.10" /> Darwin called the moral sense "the most noble of all the attributes of man,"<Cite source="darwin-descent" locator="ch. IV, p. 97" /> yet noted that social feelings extend "only to those of the same association."<Cite source="darwin-descent" locator="ch. IV, p. 98" /> He argued that "the simplest reason" pushes sympathy outward from tribe to nation, after which only "an artificial barrier" stands in the way of extending it to all peoples.<Cite source="darwin-descent" locator="ch. IV, p. 122" />

#### One source, two faces

Evolutionary "altruism," defined by effects on fitness, differs from genuine psychological care, and evolution can produce the second: love is not selfishness in disguise.<Cite source="sep-altruism-biological" locator="§5" /> Richard Wrangham distinguishes reactive aggression, a response to threat, from proactive aggression, a "purposeful planned attack."<Cite source="wrangham-two-types" locator="introduction" /> Humans are low in the first and high in the second. Cumulative culture shows the same doubleness: the cooperative learning and conformity that make culture's "ratchet" possible also bring sanctions against those who do not conform.<Cite source="tennie-ratchet" locator="§5" />

#### Moral facts and dignity

The same human nature is read in two ways. Mackie held that "there are no objective values,"<Cite source="mackie-ethics" locator="p. 15" /> and that morality feels objective because we project our attitudes and socially instilled demands onto the world.<Cite source="mackie-ethics" locator="pp. 42–43" /> Railton defends naturalistic moral realism, on which rightness is fixed from a social point of view that includes but exceeds any one person's own.<Cite source="railton-moral-realism" locator="p. 201" /> Evolutionary debunkers argue that this history gives realists reason to doubt their moral beliefs; realists reply that much moral belief comes from reflection that evolution did not fix.<Cite source="sep-morality-biology" locator="§4.1" /> Naturalists ground dignity variously: in capacities such as reason-giving agency and sentience, in the demand that everyone's interests count equally, or in Hume's sentiment of humanity extending to all mankind.<Cite source="hume-epm" locator="9.5–9.6" /> The founding text does not always meet that ideal. Darwin calls humanity to animals a late acquisition, "apparently unfelt by savages,"<Cite source="darwin-descent" locator="ch. IV, pp. 121–123" /> a ranking of peoples that present-day naturalists reject.

#### A Christian note: Railton's analogy with logic

Railton compares moral evaluation to logic: a person may have no reason to purge every contradiction from his beliefs, yet logic is not arbitrary, and moral conduct likewise "is rational from an impartial point of view."<Cite source="railton-moral-realism" locator="p. 202" /> The Christian finds the analogy apt, since both standards apply whether or not one wants them, but thinks it moves the authority question rather than settling it: why should the impartial standpoint govern someone who rejects it? The Christian reads the pervasive human concern for justification as the work of the law written on the heart,<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 2:14–15" /> a reading argued from Scripture, not from neutral premises.
