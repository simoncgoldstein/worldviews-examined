## The view

Naturalism explains the human mixture as what one should expect from a clever, social,
partly altruistic animal. Human beings are part of nature<Cite source="russell-what-i-believe" locator="ch. 1" />
and are products of biological and cultural evolution. Darwin argued that any animal with
strong social instincts would acquire a conscience once its intelligence approached ours.<Cite source="darwin-descent" locator="ch. IV, p. 98" />
Human greatness has the same roots. Teaching, imitation and distinctively human forms of
cooperation let each generation keep and improve on what the last discovered. Tools, science,
art and institutions therefore accumulate.<Cite source="tennie-ratchet" locator="Abstract" />
Love, loyalty and self-sacrifice are genuine expressions of our social instincts.

Those instincts are also limited. Sympathy is strongest for one's own group, and it lives
alongside self-interest, fear and the pursuit of status. In Hume's image, there is "some
particle of the dove" in us, "along with the elements of the wolf and serpent."<Cite source="hume-epm" locator="9.4" />
Knowingly doing wrong follows from this constitution. A strong impulse in the moment
overrides more lasting concerns, and remorse comes afterward.<Cite source="darwin-descent" locator="ch. IV, pp. 113–114" />

Naturalists agree that moral capacities have this natural history. They disagree about what
morality is. For Mackie, it is a human device for counteracting limited sympathies.<Cite source="mackie-ethics" locator="p. 108" />
For realists such as Railton, moral facts concern what would be approved if everyone's
interests counted equally.<Cite source="railton-moral-realism" locator="p. 190" /> Either way,
the remedy is gradual: knowledge, education, institutions and a widening of sympathy.<Cite source="russell-what-i-believe" locator="ch. 4" />

## What this explains well

Naturalism derives human greatness and human terribleness from one source, so it predicts
the particular shape they take. Generosity is strongest toward kin and friends, and heroism
and cruelty cluster along group lines. The loyalty and courage that inspire sacrifice in war
are favoured in competition between groups.<Cite source="darwin-descent" locator="ch. V, p. 130" />
Comparison with other apes explains a distinctive human pairing. Humans fight far less within
their groups than chimpanzees do, yet male coalitions can jointly intimidate, expel or kill
an offending individual.<Cite source="wrangham-two-types" locator="Resolving the Rousseau–Kropotkin vs. Hobbes–Huxley Debate" /> The account also explains
why the cooperation that makes cumulative culture possible brings conformity and sanctions
against those who do not conform.<Cite source="tennie-ratchet" locator="§5" /> It gives
testable accounts of weakness of will, such as the pull of immediate rewards over later ones.<Cite source="sep-weakness-will" locator="§3.4.2" />
It treats moral progress as real but fragile: "an uneven secular trend," not a guarantee.<Cite source="railton-moral-realism" locator="pp. 193–194" />

## Christian response

The Christian can grant this account's mechanisms, from Hume's mixed constitution to Darwin's remorse and two kinds of aggression; it has no stake in denying them. Calvin traced all truth to the Spirit, "the only fountain of truth."<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="II.2.15" />

The first disagreement is about explanatory adequacy: moral authority. Railton's "sensible knave" admits that his conduct is unjust and asks, "But what is that to me?"<Cite source="railton-moral-realism" locator="p. 169" /> Railton keeps him obligated, since rightness is fixed from a point of view that includes but exceeds his own.<Cite source="railton-moral-realism" locator="p. 201" /> Knavish desires "cannot license exemption from moral obligation."<Cite source="railton-moral-realism" locator="p. 203" /> What Railton gives up is that every rational agent has a reason to comply.<Cite source="railton-moral-realism" locator="p. 201" /> Three things come apart: whether a duty applies, whether the agent has reason to do it, and whether he is moved. The Christian presses the first gap: what makes a duty that gives its subject no reason authoritative over him, rather than only true of him? On the Christian view the gap closes because the law expresses the will of a personal God who is himself the good. Man's "chief and highest end" is to glorify God and "fully to enjoy him,"<Cite source="westminster-larger-catechism" locator="Q. 1" /> so what one ought to do and one's deepest reason coincide. Naturalist realists contest this.

The second is about grounding equal dignity. Railton defends his criterion by plausibility and explanatory role.<Cite source="railton-moral-realism" locator="pp. 190–191" /> Yet weighting interests by strength can license unequal treatment of people with the most severe impairments, and Kantian autonomy thresholds appear to exclude them.<Cite source="sep-cognitive-disability" locator="§3" /> Other accounts seek more inclusive grounds.<Cite source="sep-cognitive-disability" locator="§4.1" /> The Christian's own commitment is equally substantive: worth is a status God confers, and murder is forbidden "for God made man in his own image."<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Genesis 9:6" /> The claim is comparative: a status, unlike capacities, does not come in degrees.

## Pressure questions

- On your view the knave is obligated even if he has no reason to comply. What makes that obligation authoritative over him, rather than simply true of him?
- If everyone's interests count equally but some people's interests are weaker, what secures the equal standing of those with the least capacity, such as infants or people with advanced dementia?
- If remorse is a lasting social instinct reasserting itself, what makes it accurate rather than merely strong?

## Deep dive

#### Which naturalism?

Naturalism has no creed. The view represented here holds that human beings are wholly natural
organisms, and that knowledge of them is continuous with the sciences.<Cite source="sep-naturalism" locator="§1; §2" />
Its account of human nature runs from Hume through Darwin to contemporary evolutionary ethics
and moral psychology. Avowed atheist philosophers such as Russell and Mackie share it, but so
do thinkers like Darwin, an agnostic, and Hume, whose religious position is disputed. Because
naturalists who disagree sharply about morality share this anthropology, it is kept apart from
metaethics here. Three questions are distinct: how moral capacities evolved, what we ought to
do, and what moral claims are. Answering the first does not settle the other two.<Cite source="sep-morality-biology" locator="§1.1" />

#### Hume: the mixed constitution

Hume's "science of man" supplies the basic picture. Reason alone cannot move us to act or hold
back a passion; only a contrary passion can.<Cite source="hume-treatise" locator="2.3.3.4" />
People rarely love anyone more than themselves. Yet in most people the kind affections, taken
together, outweigh the selfish ones.<Cite source="hume-treatise" locator="3.2.2.5" /> Justice
exists because human generosity is "confin'd" and goods are scarce.<Cite source="hume-treatise" locator="3.2.2.18" />
Its remedy is artifice, a product of judgment and understanding.<Cite source="hume-treatise" locator="3.2.2.9" />
Hume also saw the gap between knowing and doing: "Men often act knowingly against their
interest." What we call strength of mind is the calm passions prevailing over the violent
ones.<Cite source="hume-treatise" locator="2.3.3.10" /> Sympathy is "a very powerful principle
in human nature."<Cite source="hume-treatise" locator="3.3.1.10" /> It becomes impartial moral
judgment when we take up "steady and general points of view."<Cite source="hume-treatise" locator="3.3.1.15" />

#### Darwin: conscience from social instinct

Darwin called the moral sense "the most noble of all the attributes of man." It can lead a
person to risk their life for another.<Cite source="darwin-descent" locator="ch. IV, p. 97" />
He traced it to social instincts joined with memory, reflection and language. He also noted
that these feelings extend "only to those of the same association."<Cite source="darwin-descent" locator="ch. IV, p. 98" />

His account of conscience explains acting against one's own judgment. In the moment, we tend
to follow the stronger impulse, which more often serves our own desires at others' expense.
Afterward the lasting social instinct reasserts itself, bringing remorse, shame and a resolve
to do better. "Conscience looks backwards, and serves as a guide for the future."<Cite source="darwin-descent" locator="ch. IV, pp. 113–114" />

Darwin also argued that "the simplest reason" pushes sympathy outward from tribe to nation.
After that, only "an artificial barrier" stands in the way of extending it to all peoples.<Cite source="darwin-descent" locator="ch. IV, p. 122" />

#### One source, two faces

Contemporary naturalists sharpen Darwin's picture. Kin selection and reciprocity explain how
helping behaviour could evolve. Evolutionary "altruism", defined by its effects on fitness,
differs from genuine psychological care, and evolution can produce the second. Love is not
selfishness in disguise.<Cite source="sep-altruism-biological" locator="§5" />

The anthropologist Richard Wrangham distinguishes two kinds of aggression. Proactive
aggression is a "purposeful planned attack"; reactive aggression is a response to a threat.<Cite source="wrangham-two-types" locator="introduction" />
Humans are low in reactive aggression, which makes them more like bonobos, and high in
proactive aggression, which they share with chimpanzees. On this view, the long debate between
the "Hobbes–Huxley" and "Rousseau–Kropotkin" positions is misconceived, because both are
partly correct.<Cite source="wrangham-two-types" locator="Abstract" />

Cumulative culture shows the same doubleness. Michael Tomasello and colleagues trace culture's
"ratchet effect" to cooperative learning, active teaching and conformity. The same cooperation
also brings normative sanctions against those who do not conform.<Cite source="tennie-ratchet" locator="Abstract" />

The philosopher Philip Kitcher gives the human condition a compact formula. Our propensities
for altruism committed us to social life, and their limits made that life troubled.<Cite source="kitcher-ethical-project-precis" locator="p. 10" />
Ethics began as a remedy for failures of altruism, and early conscience was built partly from
fear of one's peers and of unseen powers.<Cite source="kitcher-ethical-project-precis" locator="p. 4" />
We remain "incomplete psychological altruists."<Cite source="kitcher-ethical-project-precis" locator="p. 18" />
Philosophers of action analyse the resulting failures in several ways: acting against one's
best judgment, breaking one's resolutions, and steeply discounting the future.<Cite source="sep-weakness-will" locator="§2; §3.3; §3.4.2" />

#### Agency and the self

Most naturalists are compatibilists. For Hume, liberty is "a power of acting or not acting,
according to the determinations of the will."<Cite source="hume-enquiry" locator="8.23" />
Daniel Dennett argues that what sets humans apart is the practice of "asking, and giving,
reasons," including asking ourselves.<Cite source="dennett-self-artifact" locator="p. 48" />
The self is a "center of narrative gravity" that keeps track of past and planned intentions.<Cite source="dennett-self-artifact" locator="p. 49" />
That larger self can partly control what happens where decisions are made, and so it bears
moral responsibility.<Cite source="dennett-self-artifact" locator="p. 50" /> Inner moral
conflict reflects an agency that is "only partially coherent."<Cite source="dennett-self-artifact" locator="p. 39" />

#### Realists and anti-realists

The same human nature is read in two ways. J. L. Mackie held that "there are no objective
values."<Cite source="mackie-ethics" locator="p. 15" /> On his account, morality feels objective
because we project our attitudes and socially instilled demands onto the world.<Cite source="mackie-ethics" locator="pp. 42–43" />

Peter Railton defends naturalistic moral realism. He argues that social arrangements which
discount a group's interests generate unrest and feedback that push toward including them.
The result is "an uneven secular trend," not a guarantee.<Cite source="railton-moral-realism" locator="pp. 193–194" />

Evolutionary debunkers argue that this history gives realists reason to doubt their moral
beliefs. Realists reply that much moral belief comes from reflection that evolution did not
fix.<Cite source="sep-morality-biology" locator="§4.1" />

#### Remedy and dignity

Russell is typical in refusing utopian shortcuts. "There is no short cut to the good life,
whether individual or social." The work is to build up "intelligence, selfcontrol, and
sympathy" through gradual training and experiment. He also granted that human nature limits
sympathy.<Cite source="russell-what-i-believe" locator="ch. 4" /> The good life, he wrote, is
"inspired by love and guided by knowledge."<Cite source="russell-what-i-believe" locator="ch. 2" />

Naturalists ground dignity in several ways that do not all agree. Some point to capacities
such as reason-giving agency and sentience. Some point to the realist demand that everyone's
interests count equally. Some appeal to Hume's sentiment of humanity, which extends to all
mankind.<Cite source="hume-epm" locator="9.5–9.6" /> Others locate it in human valuing itself;
as Russell put it, "it is we who create value."<Cite source="russell-what-i-believe" locator="ch. 1" />
Most naturalists treat dignity as derived from these sources or achieved over time, not as
metaphysically basic. Realists hold that it is objective all the same.

The tradition's founding text does not live up to its own ideal. In tracing how sympathy
widens from tribe to nation, Darwin also calls humanity to animals a late moral acquisition,
"apparently unfelt by savages."<Cite source="darwin-descent" locator="ch. IV, pp. 121–123" />
Present-day naturalists reject the ranking of peoples that this passage assumes. Debunking arguments raise a further question that
naturalists still dispute: whether equal dignity is a moral fact or the best product of a
contingent history.

#### A Christian note: Railton's analogy with logic

Railton answers the knave with an analogy. A person may have no reason to purge every contradiction from his beliefs, yet logic is not arbitrary. Likewise moral evaluation is not arbitrary, because moral conduct "is rational from an impartial point of view."<Cite source="railton-moral-realism" locator="p. 202" /> He calls the lack of categorical force a limitation "we can live with."<Cite source="railton-moral-realism" locator="p. 203" /> The Christian finds the analogy apt, since both standards apply whether or not one wants them. But it moves the authority question rather than settling it: why should the impartial standpoint govern someone who rejects it? Railton notes that people have sacrificed even their lives for religions and ideologies that claimed to express "the universal" justificatory standpoint.<Cite source="railton-moral-realism" locator="p. 202" /> The Christian reads that pervasive concern as the work of the law written on the heart.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 2:14–15" /> That reading is argued from Scripture, not demonstrated from neutral premises.
