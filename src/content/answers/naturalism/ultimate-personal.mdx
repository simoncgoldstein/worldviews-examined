## The view

Naturalism does not claim to have discovered an impersonal entity called "Ultimate Reality." Its claim is more modest: when we examine nature at its most basic level, we find fields, particles, laws and initial conditions, and no sign of intentions or plans behind them. So there is no good reason to posit a personal being underlying nature.

Russell stated the position in its classic form. He did not claim to prove there is no God, but he thought gods lie "outside the region of even probable knowledge," so there is no reason to consider them.<Cite source="russell-what-i-believe" locator="ch. 1" /> The physicist Sean Carroll puts the method plainly: whether a creator exists "should be judged on ordinary empirical grounds," by whether it gives a useful account of what we observe, not on a priori arguments.<Cite source="carroll-something-rather-than-nothing" locator="§5" />

On this view, persons are real but not basic. Russell wrote that "Man is the product of causes which had no prevision of the end they were achieving."<Cite source="russell-free-mans-worship" locator="para. 5" /> Minds arise when matter is organized in particular ways: in brains, after billions of years of cosmic and biological history. Most naturalists treat mental properties as real higher-level features, determined by their physical basis.<Cite source="sep-properties-emergent" locator="§§3–4" />

This is a burden-of-evidence stance, not a proof. Naturalists generally hold that the case for a personal ultimate has not been made, rather than that it has been refuted.

## What this explains well

The view fits the cosmic record, in which minds appear late and only where complex organisms evolved, and the close dependence of thinking on the brain. It avoids explaining the world by a being whose nature is harder to understand than the world, and it keeps the question open to evidence.

## Christian response

No laboratory test settles this question; the disagreement is about explanatory adequacy. If the basic level of reality is impersonal, persons, reasons and values have to be built from things that have none of these. The Christian argues that personal explanation, explaining something by a purpose, does not reduce to impersonal causation. A world whose ground is personal, made through a Word that was in the beginning,<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="John 1:1–3" /> fits better the existence of persons who reason, love and act for ends. That is a comparative judgment, not a demonstration, and it depends on taking reasons and purposes as genuinely explanatory. The anthropology and ethics are developed in <QuestionLink question="what-is-man" worldview="naturalism">what is man</QuestionLink> and <QuestionLink question="know-the-good" worldview="naturalism">knowing the good</QuestionLink>.

## Pressure questions

- If the most basic level of reality is impersonal, how do reasons, as distinct from causes, come to govern what we believe?
- If some naturalists make experience a basic feature of matter, what stops mind from belonging to the ground of nature itself?

## Deep dive

#### Russell's two moods

In *What I Believe*, Russell is a cautious empiricist. Mind and matter are not ultimate realities but convenient groupings of events, and "everything interesting is a matter of organization, not of primal substance."<Cite source="russell-what-i-believe" locator="ch. 1" /> In "A Free Man's Worship" he is a moralist, describing a universe "purposeless" and "void of meaning," in which human ideals must find a home.<Cite source="russell-free-mans-worship" locator="para. 5" /> Both texts deny a personal ground. The first does it as an inference about evidence, the second as a stance toward life.

#### Carroll's parity argument

Carroll argues that a creator does not escape the need for something that simply exists. If the creator explains its own existence, one may ask why the universe could not do the same.<Cite source="carroll-something-rather-than-nothing" locator="§5" /> He adds that a self-sufficient universe would be a simpler package than a self-sufficient creator plus a universe. The argument is about parsimony; it assumes that a mind is not a simpler stopping point than a physical state. Graham Oppy's systematic version of this comparison could not be lawfully read for this domain.

#### Emergence and panpsychism

Most naturalists accept weak emergence: mental properties are real and useful to describe, but fixed by physical processes.<Cite source="sep-properties-emergent" locator="§§3–4" /> A minority defend panpsychism, on which some form of experience is a basic feature of matter. Stoljar notes that this creates difficulty for the usual physicalist conception of "the physical."<Cite source="sep-physicalism" locator="§4.4" /> Panpsychism makes mentality more basic, but not personal in the sense of a single mind that knows and wills. No mainstream naturalism posits a personal ground.
