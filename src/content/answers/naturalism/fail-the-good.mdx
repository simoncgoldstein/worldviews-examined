## The view

Recognizing a reason for action need not supply the strongest motive. Hume describes violent passions overcoming calmer, more enduring concerns: a person can knowingly act against his interests.<Cite source="hume-treatise" locator="2.3.3.10" /> Darwin offers a historical evolutionary account of conscience. A vivid immediate impulse can defeat persistent social instincts; afterward, when the impulse fades, those instincts reassert themselves as dissatisfaction and remorse.<Cite source="darwin-descent" locator="ch. IV, pp. 113–114" /> Together these explain how moral awareness and contrary action coexist. They are representative naturalist explanations rather than doctrines every naturalist must hold.

## What this explains well

The account makes temptation's timing intelligible: what dominates at the moment need not be what we endorse afterward. It also separates judging an act wrong from maintaining a resolution to avoid it. Philosophical treatments distinguish acting against one's better judgment from abandoning an earlier intention.<Cite source="sep-weakness-will" locator="§2; §3.3" />

## Christian response

The Christian grants the force of these mechanisms: they describe temptation's timing and remorse's return well. The disagreement concerns what the losing judgment answers to. Christianity reads conscience as awareness of God's law, not merely one motive among others.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 2:14–15" /> A naturalist need not reduce duty to desire either: Railton defends objective natural moral facts while allowing that someone may lack a personal reason to obey them.<Cite source="railton-moral-realism" locator="p. 201" /> Nor does explaining the losing motive excuse the act. Hume and Darwin describe how a person comes to act against his judgment, and a naturalist can still hold him responsible and blameworthy for doing so. The difference lies in what the wrongdoer has failed. On the Christian account, knowingly doing wrong is also the refusal of a personal claim, so the wrongdoer stands guilty before God as well as answerable to those he harms. Whether a naturalist standard can carry authority of that kind over someone whose strongest interests oppose it is the question argued under <QuestionLink question="know-the-good" worldview="naturalism">moral knowledge</QuestionLink>.

## Deep dive

#### Explaining failure and justifying judgment

An account of why someone acts is different from an account of whether he acts rightly. Darwin's persistent social instincts explain the return of remorse, but that alone does not show that every remorseful judgment is correct. Likewise, Hume's calmer passions are not simply dispassionate reason: their comparative calm can make their influence look like reason's rule.<Cite source="hume-treatise" locator="2.3.3.4; 2.3.3.10" /> Neither account says that people possess a single motive which invariably follows their considered judgment.

This leaves room for distinct naturalist accounts of morality. Railton's realist standard considers everyone's interests from a social point of view; the explanation of a selfish impulse does not exempt its bearer from that standard.<Cite source="railton-moral-realism" locator="pp. 190–191; p. 201" /> A naturalist can therefore distinguish what explains wrongdoing from what would justify it. The Christian disputes the ultimate grounding of that standard's authority, not the observation that impulses compete or that habits can change.
