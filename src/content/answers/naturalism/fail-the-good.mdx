## The view

Recognizing a reason for action need not supply the strongest motive. Hume describes violent passions overcoming calmer, more enduring concerns: a person can knowingly act against his interests.<Cite source="hume-treatise" locator="2.3.3.10" /> Darwin offers a historical evolutionary account of conscience. A vivid immediate impulse can defeat persistent social instincts; afterward, when the impulse fades, those instincts reassert themselves as dissatisfaction and remorse.<Cite source="darwin-descent" locator="ch. IV, pp. 113–114" /> These explain how moral awareness and contrary action coexist. They are representative naturalistic mechanisms, not a creed every naturalist must accept.

## What this explains well

The account makes temptation's timing intelligible: what dominates at the moment need not be what we endorse afterward. It also separates judging an act wrong from maintaining a resolution to avoid it. Philosophical treatments distinguish acting against one's better judgment from abandoning an earlier intention.<Cite source="sep-weakness-will" locator="§2; §3.3" />

## Christian response

The Christian grants the force of these mechanisms. The disagreement concerns what the judgment answers to. Christianity interprets conscience as awareness of God's law, not merely a balance among motives.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 2:14–15" /> A naturalist need not reduce duty to desire: Railton defends objective natural moral facts while allowing that someone may lack a personal reason to obey them.<Cite source="railton-moral-realism" locator="p. 201" /> The comparative question is how moral obligation binds the person whose strongest interests oppose it. This page does not establish that a naturalistic grounding is impossible.

## Deep dive

#### Explaining failure and justifying judgment

An account of why someone acts is different from an account of whether he acts rightly. Darwin's persistent social instincts explain the return of remorse, but that alone does not show that every remorseful judgment is correct. Likewise, Hume's calmer passions are not simply dispassionate reason: their comparative calm can make their influence look like reason's rule.<Cite source="hume-treatise" locator="2.3.3.4; 2.3.3.10" /> Neither account says that people possess a single motive which invariably follows their considered judgment.

This leaves room for distinct naturalist accounts of morality. Railton's realist standard considers everyone's interests from a social point of view; the explanation of a selfish impulse does not exempt its bearer from that standard.<Cite source="railton-moral-realism" locator="pp. 190–191; p. 201" /> Thus a naturalist can distinguish what explains wrongdoing from what justifies it. The Christian dispute is with the ultimate grounding of that authority, not with the observation that impulses compete or habits can change.

For the broader evolutionary account and the realism/anti-realism distinction, see <QuestionLink question="great-and-terrible" worldview="naturalism">the flagship anthropology</QuestionLink>. Here the narrow conclusion is that recognizing the better course does not guarantee that its associated motives will prevail.
