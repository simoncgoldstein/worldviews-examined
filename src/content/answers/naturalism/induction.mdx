## The view

The problem comes from Hume, and naturalists take it at full strength. All our reasoning about unobserved matters of fact rests on the assumption "that the future will be conformable to the past."<Cite source="hume-enquiry" locator="4.19" /> That assumption cannot be proved by pure reasoning, because "it implies no contradiction, that the course of nature may change."<Cite source="hume-enquiry" locator="4.18" /> Nor can it be proved from experience, because any argument from past experience to the future already assumes it: such a proof "must be evidently going in a circle, and taking that for granted, which is the very point in question."<Cite source="hume-enquiry" locator="4.19" /> Russell's chicken, fed every day until the day its neck is wrung, makes the point vivid.<Cite source="russell-problems-philosophy" locator="ch. VI" />

Hume did not conclude that we should stop inferring. "Custom, then, is the great guide of human life."<Cite source="hume-enquiry" locator="5.6" /> Later naturalists have offered several kinds of answer.<Cite source="sep-induction-problem" locator="§§3–6" />

- **Accept a benign circle.** Using induction to defend induction is circular in its rule, not its premises; if induction is in fact reliable, its conclusions are justified. Quine added that evolution weeds out bad inducers.<Cite source="sep-epistemology-naturalized" locator="§2" note="Quine 1969c, as quoted by SEP" />
- **Add a principle.** Infer real laws as the best explanation of regularities, or assign starting probabilities and update them.
- **Change the question.** Being reasonable simply *is* proportioning belief to inductive evidence (Strawson), or induction will succeed if any method does (Reichenbach).

The Deep dive gives the full map.

## What this explains well

These answers take Hume seriously, explain why induction has served science and survival so well, and are honest about what each gives up.

## Christian response

The Christian agrees that the problem is real and that none of these answers is a trick. Each, though, pays a recognizable price. The circular answers accept a circle and argue it is harmless. The explanatory and Bayesian answers add a premise that experience does not supply: that laws or natural necessities exist, or that certain starting probabilities are reasonable. The dissolving and pragmatic answers change what "justification" means. The Christian answer pays a price too: it rests on a basic commitment to God's faithfulness. The comparative question is which commitment best explains why regularity holds at all. Humeans who treat laws as summaries of what happens leave the continuation of the pattern without explanation.<Cite source="sep-laws-of-nature" locator="§§2, 8" /> This is explanatory incompleteness, not contradiction (see <QuestionLink question="induction" worldview="christianity">the Christian answer</QuestionLink>).

## Pressure questions

- If laws are only summaries of what has happened, what makes it reasonable, before tomorrow arrives, to expect the summary to hold?
- If laws are real necessities, where do those necessities come from?

## Deep dive

#### The full map

SEP's map of responses to Hume can be grouped by what each does:<Cite source="sep-induction-problem" locator="§§3.2, 3.3, 4.1, 5.2, 5.3, 6" />

1. **Rule-circular or externalist justification** (Van Cleve, Papineau): rule-circularity is not vicious, and if induction is reliable, its conclusions are justified. *Accepts a benign circle.*
2. **Inference to the best explanation** (Armstrong, BonJour, Foster): regularities are best explained by non-Humean laws. *Adds an a priori principle and a robust view of laws.* Critics ask why a law limited to the observed region would not explain the data equally well.
3. **Bayesian reasoning**: *adds* prior probabilities, which do the work Hume questioned.
4. **Strawson's dissolution**: asking for a deductive justification of induction demands, in Strawson's words as SEP quotes them, "that induction shall be shown to be really a kind of deduction." *Redefines justification.*
5. **Reichenbach's pragmatic vindication**: like a physician who says that if any remedy will save the patient, it is an operation. *Redefines justification as vindication.*
6. **Quine's naturalized response**: evolution selects good inducers. *Accepts a circle, since the evidence for evolution is itself inductive*, which critics such as Fumerton reject.<Cite source="sep-epistemology-naturalized" locator="§§2, 3.1" />
7. **Living with scepticism**: Hume's own mitigated scepticism, and later views that accept the conclusion and ask how to live with it. *Accepts fallibility.*

#### Hume's two premises

Hume's argument has two horns. Demonstrative reasoning cannot establish uniformity, because its denial is not contradictory. Probable reasoning cannot, because it presupposes uniformity.<Cite source="hume-enquiry" locator="4.18–19" /> In the *Treatise* he states the presupposition directly: if reason determined us, it would proceed on the principle that unobserved instances resemble observed ones, which cannot itself be proved.<Cite source="hume-treatise" locator="1.3.6.4, 1.3.6.11" /> Infants and even animals learn from experience without any such argument, which shows that the inference is not the work of reasoning.<Cite source="hume-enquiry" locator="4.23" />

#### Russell's verdict

Russell concluded that the inductive principle can be neither proved nor disproved by experience: "we must either accept the inductive principle on the ground of its intrinsic evidence, or forgo all justification of our expectations about the future."<Cite source="russell-problems-philosophy" locator="ch. VI" /> His answer adds an a priori principle, which places him with the responses that add a principle rather than with the sceptics.
