## The view

Asked what finally settles a disputed claim, the naturalist points to a method rather than a voice, since naturalism has no canon, prophet or council. The method combines observation and experiment, logic and mathematics, inference to the best explanation, coherence with the rest of what we know, and checking by other inquirers. Methodological naturalism claims "some kind of general authority for the scientific method," and treats philosophy as continuous with science rather than as a court above it.<Cite source="sep-naturalism" locator="introduction; §2.1" /> This is a deliberate feature of the view, not a gap.

Quine gave the position its sharpest form. Beliefs are not tested one by one. They face experience together, as a connected web, and when a prediction fails we may adjust any part of the web. So "no statement is immune to revision," not even a law of logic, although we revise central beliefs last.<Cite source="sep-quine" locator="§§3.2–3.3" note="Quine 1951, as quoted by SEP" /> Nor is there a "First Philosophy," a standpoint wholly outside science from which science as a whole could be justified or criticized. We improve our knowledge from within, using some parts of science to guide inquiry in others.<Cite source="sep-quine" locator="§4.4" />

Hume reached a modest version of this earlier: after pressing sceptical doubts as far as they go, he recommended a "mitigated" scepticism that keeps inquiry within the narrow capacity of human understanding. "Philosophical decisions," he wrote, "are nothing but the reflections of common life, methodized and corrected."<Cite source="hume-enquiry" locator="12.25" />

Naturalists disagree about how much room this leaves for reasoning that is not empirical. Some treat science as the measure of everything that can be known. Others, like Russell, hold that certain principles, including the principle of induction, must be accepted on their own self-evidence because experience cannot prove them<Cite source="russell-problems-philosophy" locator="ch. VI" /> (see <QuestionLink question="induction" worldview="naturalism">induction</QuestionLink>). In practice, authority is spread across communities of inquiry, and all of it remains provisional.

## What this explains well

The view explains how knowledge can grow and correct itself without anyone owning the truth. It makes dogmatism a fault in principle and matches the success of science, which has advanced by revising cherished beliefs.

## Christian response

The Christian does not fault naturalism for lacking a revealed court it never claimed to have. The question is what warrants the methodological network itself. The claim that only revisable empirical methods yield knowledge cannot be established from outside those methods, and Quine openly accepts that there is no outside standpoint. Critics such as Richard Fumerton call the attempt to answer doubts about our methods with those same methods "blatant, indeed pathetic, circularity."<Cite source="sep-epistemology-naturalized" locator="§3.1" note="Fumerton 1994, as quoted by SEP" />

That alone is not a refutation. Every framework reaches commitments that no higher court certifies, and the Christian's appeal to self-attesting Scripture is deliberate too (see <QuestionLink question="ultimate-authority" worldview="christianity">the Christian answer</QuestionLink>). The comparative question is whether each framework's final commitments are coherent and adequate to what they must explain. The Christian presses three things the network uses but does not obviously ground: the normative force of "follow the evidence," the necessity of logic and the reliability of induction. These are claims of explanatory incompleteness, examined under <QuestionLink question="logic-binding" worldview="naturalism">logic</QuestionLink> and <QuestionLink question="induction" worldview="naturalism">induction</QuestionLink>.

## Pressure questions

- What justifies treating scientific method as the final court, other than the method's own results?
- Kornblith argues that the norm "follow the evidence" binds every agent, since whatever anyone wants, a reliable way of forming true beliefs serves it.<Cite source="sep-epistemology-naturalized" locator="§4.2" /> Is a norm grounded in what serves our goals, however universal, the same as one that binds a rational agent whatever their goals?

## Deep dive

#### Holism and revisability

Quine's argument grew out of his debate with Carnap over the distinction between truths of meaning and truths of fact. Quine denied that the distinction can be drawn sharply. Our whole theory, including mathematics and logic, meets experience as a body. Some sentences are so central that we can hardly imagine giving them up, but even these "might, in principle, be rejected." That is how SEP's authors summarize him before quoting his famous line about revision.<Cite source="sep-quine" locator="§3.3" />

#### Answering the sceptic from within

Critics object that if science is the final court, science cannot answer the sceptic without assuming what is in question. Quine's reply is that sceptical doubts themselves arise within science, for example from knowledge of illusions. In SEP's summary, "if skepticism itself is born of science, we can appeal to science in answering its doubts."<Cite source="sep-epistemology-naturalized" locator="§2" note="SEP's paraphrase of Quine 1975" /> Traditional epistemologists answer that the sceptic needs only the *possibility* of error, not its scientific study.<Cite source="sep-epistemology-naturalized" locator="§3.1" /> Many naturalists accept a mild circularity here and argue that it is benign, because the methods in question are in fact reliable.

#### Science and the a priori

Methodological naturalists see philosophy and science as "engaged in essentially the same enterprise, pursuing similar ends and using similar methods." They still disagree about whether that leaves room for a priori knowledge and for intuitions.<Cite source="sep-naturalism" locator="§§2.1, 2.3–2.4" /> Russell held that some knowledge is a priori: the principle of induction and the laws of logic cannot be proved by experience, yet we know them.<Cite source="russell-problems-philosophy" locator="chs. VI–VII" />

#### The self-certification point

Every lane on this site reaches a final authority that is not certified from outside. Naturalism's version is unusual in being *revisable in principle*: the network can, in theory, overturn any part of itself. Defenders see that as its chief virtue. Critics reply that a court which can revise every law, including its own rules of procedure, needs some account of why its verdicts track truth at all.
