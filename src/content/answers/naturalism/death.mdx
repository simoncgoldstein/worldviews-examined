## The view

A naturalist account explains mortality through bodily processes rather than a divine sentence. Evolutionary theories of aging connect senescence with the declining strength of selection at later ages, allowing harmful late effects to persist. Flatt and Partridge review mutation accumulation, antagonistic pleiotropy and trade-offs in bodily maintenance; aging need not be a program selected to kill organisms.<Cite source="flatt-partridge-evolution-aging" locator="Why does aging evolve?; Key lessons from the evolution of aging" /> Aging, however, is not an explanation of every cause of death.

Russell treats personal death as the end of the living person, while refusing to make finite happiness worthless.<Cite source="russell-what-i-believe" locator="ch. 1" /> Whether death harms its subject remains a separate philosophical question. Epicurus argues that death cannot be experienced by the person whose sensation has ceased.<Cite source="epicurus-letter-menoeceus-hicks" locator="Diogenes Laertius X.124–125" /> A deprivation account instead asks whether dying removes worthwhile life that otherwise could have occurred.<Cite source="sep-death" locator="§3.2" />

## What this explains well

This account separates explaining death from evaluating it. One can understand why bodies decline, deplore an early death, care for the dying and value survivors' grief without positing a cosmic purpose. Deprivation accounts also explain why the timing matters: losing future relationships and projects may be bad even though the dead person never later experiences that loss. The Epicurean challenge usefully asks who is harmed and when, rather than assuming that fear settles the argument.

## Christian response

The Christian agrees that physical mechanisms and timing matter, and finds common ground with the deprivation account: death is bad because it takes away goods a life could still have held. Christianity goes further. It calls death an enemy within a fallen condition, something that ought not to be, and promises that the enemy will be undone in the resurrection.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 5:12; 1 Corinthians 15:26" /> That judgment rests on premises about creation and sin that naturalism does not share, and the fair dispute is whether those premises are warranted (see <QuestionLink question="death" worldview="christianity">the Christian answer</QuestionLink>). What the Christian presses is the difference in outcome: on naturalism the deprivation is final, and the goods death takes are never restored. The feeling that death is wrong cannot by itself establish Christianity, and a mechanism of senescence does not show that death is good.

## Deep dive

#### Ending experience or losing goods?

Epicurus is an ancient source whose argument naturalists can inherit; his philosophy as a whole is not modern scientific naturalism. His argument joins the loss of sensation to the claim that death is nothing for its subject.<Cite source="epicurus-letter-menoeceus-hicks" locator="Diogenes Laertius X.124–125" /> That challenges fear of being dead. It does not imply that dying cannot hurt or that survivors cannot suffer a serious loss.

The deprivation account evaluates the actual life against the worthwhile life that might have continued. Its claim concerns a comparison between possibilities, rather than a painful mental state after death. The survey presents this family of views through Nagel and Feldman, whose positions differ.<Cite source="sep-death" locator="§3.2" /> The associated question about the time of harm shows why the absence of a later sufferer is a genuine challenge, not a simple dismissal of all loss.

Bernard Williams's discussion of immortality raises a further issue: indefinitely extended life might become tedious or undermine the desires that make it worth continuing. That does not make premature death harmless, nor every longer life undesirable.<Cite source="sep-death" locator="§7.2" /> Russell's affirmation of finite happiness likewise gives no reason to abandon efforts to preserve worthwhile life.

The disagreement is therefore internal to the evaluation of mortality. Biology alone does not choose between Epicurean and deprivation accounts. Detailed claims about continued existence belong to <QuestionLink question="after-death" worldview="naturalism">after death</QuestionLink>.
