## The view

Naturalism has no revelation of its own, and it does not usually claim that revelation is impossible. It treats every claim to revelation as testimony: someone reports that God spoke, appeared or acted, and the report is weighed like any other. "A wise man," Hume wrote, "proportions his belief to the evidence."<Cite source="hume-enquiry" locator="10.4" />

The difficulty is that revelation claims usually involve miracles, the kind of event ordinary experience tells against: the dead stay dead in every case we can check. So Hume proposed a test: no testimony establishes a miracle "unless the testimony be of such a kind, that its falsehood would be more miraculous, than the fact, which it endeavours to establish."<Cite source="hume-enquiry" locator="10.13" /> The question is which is more probable, that the witness erred or that nature was overturned.

Hume added reasons to discount religious testimony in particular. People love marvels, and "if the spirit of religion join itself to the love of wonder, there is an end of common sense."<Cite source="hume-enquiry" locator="10.17" /> And the religions contradict one another: "in matters of religion, whatever is different is contrary," so the miracles claimed for each count as evidence against the rest.<Cite source="hume-enquiry" locator="10.24" />

This is not a ban on miracles. Hume granted that uniform worldwide testimony could establish eight days of darkness. What he denied was that a miracle could be proved "so as to be the foundation of a system of religion."<Cite source="hume-enquiry" locator="10.36" /> The argument that miracles are impossible by definition comes from Spinoza and Voltaire, and is "sometimes mistakenly attributed to Hume."<Cite source="sep-miracles" locator="§3.1.1" /> Contemporary naturalists usually reason in terms of probability: a very low prior can in principle be overcome, but only by evidence far likelier on the miracle than on any natural explanation.<Cite source="sep-miracles" locator="§2.2.4" />

Inner experiences of the divine face the same problem. Religious diversity works like "wildly conflicting eyewitness reports," and adequate natural explanations of the experiences leave nothing further to explain.<Cite source="sep-religious-experience" locator="§3" /> The serious naturalist is therefore open in principle and sceptical in practice.

## What this explains well

The view explains why revelation claims are so many and so mutually contradictory, and it applies one standard to testimony, sacred or secular.

## Christian response

The Christian need not say that naturalists are merely biased. First, what prior probability is appropriate? The Stanford Encyclopedia's survey of the debate concludes that "the relevance of background beliefs looms large."<Cite source="sep-miracles" locator="§4.2" /> Hume holds that ascribing a miracle to the Almighty makes it no more probable, because we know God only from nature's usual course.<Cite source="hume-enquiry" locator="10.38" /> That is a substantive premise, not a neutral finding. If God exists and intends to make himself known, an act of revelation is not the least likely event.

Second, can evidence move the prior? Hume's criteria, many educated witnesses in a public setting, are reasonable, and the Christian argues that the earliest resurrection testimony meets them better than most religious claims (see <QuestionLink question="jesus" worldview="naturalism">Jesus</QuestionLink>).

Third, testimony can establish an extraordinary fact without settling its cause: as SEP reports Baden Powell's point, the fact is a matter for testimony and the supernatural cause for judgement.<Cite source="sep-miracles" locator="§4.1" /> The decisive step is metaphysical, and naturalism takes it too.

## Pressure questions

- If uniform testimony could establish eight days of darkness, what evidence would suffice for a claimed resurrection, and could any ancient event meet that standard?
- Does the very low prior against revelation come from evidence, or from the prior conviction that nature is all there is?

## Deep dive

#### Hume's argument in two parts

The first part of Hume's essay is general. Uniform experience establishes the laws of nature, so it provides "a direct and full proof" against any miracle, and only a stronger proof could overturn it.<Cite source="hume-enquiry" locator="10.12" /> The second part is particular. No miracle, he says, has been attested by enough educated, honest witnesses in a public enough place; people love marvels; such reports thrive among "ignorant and barbarous nations"; and rival religions cancel one another.<Cite source="hume-enquiry" locator="10.15–24" /> His sharpest example is a resurrection. If all England's historians agreed that Queen Elizabeth died, was buried and reappeared a month later, he would still believe the death had been feigned.<Cite source="hume-enquiry" locator="10.37" /> Prophecies fall under the same verdict, since "all prophecies are real miracles."<Cite source="hume-enquiry" locator="10.41" />

#### Impossible, improbable, or a matter of evidence

Four positions can be distinguished. Some argue that miracles are impossible by definition. Hume holds that testimony to a religious miracle is always outweighed. Bayesian critics, from George Campbell to John Earman, have called Hume's maxim trivial,<Cite source="sep-miracles" locator="§3.1.2" /> and Earman, with Charles Babbage before him, argues that enough independent witnesses could in principle overcome even a very low prior. Others reply that real testimonies are almost never independent in the required sense.<Cite source="sep-miracles" locator="§2.2.4" /> Hume still has able defenders.<Cite source="sep-miracles" locator="§3.3" /> The fourth position is methodological: many historians follow Hume in treating the supernatural as outside what history can conclude, a stance SEP describes as one side of "a deep conceptual fault line" in biblical studies.<Cite source="sep-miracles" locator="§3.3" />

#### Religious experience as evidence

Philosophers such as William Alston and Alvin Plantinga argue that religious experience can justify belief in the way sense experience does. Critics reply that religious experiences differ from sense perception in ways that weaken the analogy, and press the two defeaters: diversity, and natural explanations such as those offered by Freud and Marx.<Cite source="sep-religious-experience" locator="§3" /> Naturalists usually conclude that experience may explain why people believe without showing that what they believe is true.
