## The view

The ordinary mortalist answer is that the person ceases to live and experience when the life sustaining that person ends. Russell states the position directly and denies that happiness becomes unreal merely because it ends.<Cite source="russell-what-i-believe" locator="ch. 1" /> There is no continuing observer trapped in darkness, missing the living or measuring an infinite interval. Matter and influence can remain while the individual who thought and cared no longer does.

That answer should not be attributed to a single settled naturalist theory of personal identity. Psychological-continuity accounts and organismic accounts disagree over what makes a later being the same individual. They also distinguish continuing to exist from retaining the same character: radical changes in memory or personality do not straightforwardly establish that a different individual has replaced someone.<Cite source="sep-personal-identity" locator="§§1–4, 7" />

Hypothetical copying or preservation raises further identity questions; resemblance is not automatically numerical identity. Such possibilities do not constitute evidence that the dead now continue elsewhere. The representative position here is mortalism without a promised resurrection, rebirth or disembodied soul. Epicurus's influential argument holds that death removes sensation and thus leaves no subject experiencing death as an evil.<Cite source="epicurus-letter-menoeceus-hicks" locator="Diogenes Laertius X.124–125" /> That argument bears on death's harmfulness; it does not by itself settle every dispute about who or what a human person is.

## What this explains well

The view resists turning grief or a wish for justice into evidence of continued existence. It also distinguishes a legacy from a survivor: remembering someone is not the same as that person's remembering anything. Russell's claim that finite happiness remains genuine allows love and accomplishment to have value without requiring them to last forever.<Cite source="russell-what-i-believe" locator="ch. 1" /> An ending need not retrospectively empty a life.

## Christian response

The Christian claims more than the persistence of a memory or social influence. Paul's resurrection hope concerns the person transformed into embodied life, and it stands or falls with Christ's resurrection.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="1 Corinthians 15:12–23, 35–49" /> A naturalist may reject its warrant. The unresolved possibility of suffering and wrong left without final rectification is a cost the mortalist can acknowledge, rather than a contradiction within mortalism.

## Pressure questions

- What would distinguish the continuation of this individual from a convincing successor or copy?
- Does death's finality change the value of finite goods, or only their duration?

## Deep dive

#### Persistence is not personality

The persistence question asks whether someone still exists. A characterization question asks what traits define that person or what kind of person he has become. Olson's distinction matters when severe change is invoked as proof that the old person has already vanished.<Cite source="sep-personal-identity" locator="§§1–2" /> The debate cannot be settled simply by the intuition that someone now behaves unlike his earlier self.

Naturalists can disagree about persistence without thereby affirming a spiritual afterlife. Organismic accounts emphasize the continuation of a living human animal; psychological accounts emphasize appropriate continuity of mental features, with disputes over the physical basis and branching.<Cite source="sep-personal-identity" locator="§§3–4, 7" /> Neither framework supplies an observed postmortem successor merely by defining conditions under which one could exist. This answer therefore does not promise uploading, cryonic revival or another speculative escape from mortality.

#### No experience, and a possible loss

Epicurus argues from death's absence of sensation: while we are present, death is absent; when death arrives, we are absent.<Cite source="epicurus-letter-menoeceus-hicks" locator="Diogenes Laertius X.124–125" /> The deprivationist reply does not invent a subject suffering after death. It says an ending can be bad because the person loses goods he would otherwise have enjoyed.<Cite source="sep-death" locator="§3.2" /> Disagreement over harm is thus compatible with agreement that the dead do not experience an endless deprivation.

The Christian objection about unfinished justice likewise should not be misdescribed. Mortalism need not promise that the world ultimately repairs every loss. A refusal to make that promise can be a sober implication of the account. Christianity offers a rival hope, but the desirability of resurrection is not proof that it occurs.
