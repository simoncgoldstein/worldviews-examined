## The view

Serious naturalists do not deny that Jesus existed. As the critical historian Dale Martin puts it, "reputable historical scholars all admit that Jesus of Nazareth existed."<Cite source="martin-yale-new-testament" locator="L13 ch. 2" /> Martin's own reconstruction is of an apocalyptic Jewish prophet.<Cite source="martin-yale-new-testament" locator="L13 ch. 4" /> He was crucified under Pontius Pilate, as Paul, the Gospels and the Roman historian Tacitus attest.<Cite source="tacitus-annals-church-brodribb" locator="15.44" /> Within a few years of his death his followers were proclaiming that God had raised him. Several of his followers, and Paul, had experiences they understood as appearances of the risen Jesus.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="1 Corinthians 15:3–8" /> Naturalism grants all of this. Theories that Jesus never existed are not its representative view.

What it disputes is the explanation. Bart Ehrman, an agnostic New Testament scholar, offers one natural account. Jesus' followers had believed he was the Messiah, but "no Jew, prior to Christianity, thought that the Messiah was to be crucified." Searching their Scriptures, they found the righteous sufferer whom God vindicates, and concluded that God had exalted Jesus. Believers then "started having visions of him"; some reports, like Paul's, were of actual visions, others, like the appearance to five hundred, were "stories of visions." The stories grew into the Gospels, written "30, 40, 50, 60 years later."<Cite source="craig-ehrman-debate-2006" locator="Ehrman, conclusion" /> Visions of the dead are common, and those who have them believe them; no one need have lied.

Whether his tomb was found empty is genuinely debated. Paul's tradition says he "was buried"; the details of the burial are debated, and Ehrman notes that Paul's word is compatible with a common grave.<Cite source="craig-ehrman-debate-2006" locator="Ehrman, first rebuttal" /> The sceptic Jeffery Jay Lowder thinks it slightly more likely than not that the tomb was empty, but argues that a later removal of the body explains that as well as a resurrection does.<Cite source="lowder-empty-tomb-jhc" locator="n. 3; §2" />

Behind the historical argument lies a judgement about probability. Ehrman does not say miracles are impossible. He says that "historians can only establish what probably happened in the past, and by definition a miracle is the least probable occurrence."<Cite source="craig-ehrman-debate-2006" locator="Ehrman, opening statement" /> Hume put the philosophical point in its classic form: if every English historian reported that Queen Elizabeth had died and returned to rule, he would still believe the death was feigned.<Cite source="hume-enquiry" locator="10.37" />

## What this explains well

The account explains the early and sincere belief without supposing a unique break in nature. It treats the Gospels as other ancient sources are treated, and it judges this miracle report by the standard applied to every other.

## Christian response

The Christian does not claim that visions are impossible or that critics refuse evidence. The question is whether the natural explanations cover the facts together. Grief visions fit individuals; the tradition Paul received also reports appearances to groups, to Paul, a persecutor, and to James, whose family, the Gospels report, had not followed Jesus in his lifetime.<Cite source="ej-jesus" locator="The Name, Birth, and Death Date" /> And within about two decades Jewish followers were praying to Jesus and calling him Lord alongside the one God,<Cite source="hurtado-worship-of-christ" locator="Early devotion to Christ" /> something that exaltation traditions do not by themselves explain.

The decisive issue is the prior. If historians "can only establish what probably happened," and a miracle is by definition improbable, then no evidence could ever count, and the conclusion follows from the method rather than from the sources. The Christian argues that this is a metaphysical judgement, not a historical finding; the Stanford Encyclopedia describes the Humean principle as one side of "a deep conceptual fault line" in biblical studies.<Cite source="sep-miracles" locator="§3.3" />

## Pressure questions

- Which natural explanation accounts for the group appearance claims, Paul and James together, rather than one fact at a time?
- If a historian could never conclude that a miracle occurred, whatever the evidence, is that conclusion historical or metaphysical?

## Deep dive

#### Facts granted and facts disputed

Martin's list of what historians can affirm is short: Jesus existed, was baptized by John and was executed; the charge "King of the Jews" on the cross is unlikely to be a Christian invention, since in the Gospels it is his mockers who use the title. The details of the trials, he says, are not historically recoverable.<Cite source="martin-yale-new-testament" locator="L13 chs. 1, 3–4" /> Lowder accepts that Jesus was buried by Joseph of Arimathea, although Romans normally denied burial to the crucified.<Cite source="lowder-empty-tomb-jhc" locator="§1.1" /> Ehrman doubts the burial story, noting that Paul never names Joseph and that Mark was written "35 or 40 years after the fact."<Cite source="craig-ehrman-debate-2006" locator="Ehrman, first rebuttal" />

#### What an appearance meant

Ehrman argues that for ancient people "an appearance does not need to mean reanimation of the physical body," as with Moses and Elijah at the transfiguration.<Cite source="craig-ehrman-debate-2006" locator="Ehrman, first rebuttal" /> A Jewish scholar a century ago, Kaufmann Kohler, gave a similar account: it was owing to Jesus' "frequent apparitions after his death" to his Galilean followers that belief in his resurrection spread.<Cite source="jewish-encyclopedia-jesus" locator="Kohler, In Theology" />

#### An empty tomb without a miracle

Lowder's reburial hypothesis holds that Joseph laid the body in his tomb temporarily, and that it was later moved to a graveyard for criminals. On his reckoning this has equal explanatory power and greater scope than Craig's hypothesis that the tomb was empty because Jesus rose.<Cite source="lowder-empty-tomb-jhc" locator="§2" /> Lowder does not claim that Mark's story is false, only that it is not shown to be true.<Cite source="lowder-empty-tomb-jhc" locator="introduction" /> Ehrman goes further for the sake of argument. He sketches an improbable scenario of family members removing the body and soldiers burying it in a common grave, which he says he does not believe, but which is still "more probable than a miracle."<Cite source="craig-ehrman-debate-2006" locator="Ehrman, opening statement" />

#### Method and metaphysics

Two claims should be kept apart. One is methodological: history works by probabilities and analogies, so it cannot certify a miracle. The other is metaphysical: nature is a closed system, so no miracle happened. Ehrman explicitly asserts only the first ("I won't say they're impossible").<Cite source="craig-ehrman-debate-2006" locator="Ehrman, opening statement" /> Naturalism adds the second, which sets the prior that makes natural explanations preferable unless the evidence overwhelms it. Hume's analysis is philosophy, not a history of Jesus. Its role is to explain why that prior is so low.<Cite source="hume-enquiry" locator="10.12–13" />
