## The view

Naturalism explains religion as a human phenomenon, keeping apart how religion arises, whether it is true, and what, if anything, deserves reverence.

The founding natural history is Hume's. He separated the question of religion's foundation in reason from that of its origin in human nature. Belief in invisible power is widespread but not universal or uniform, so it "springs not from an original instinct or primary impression of nature," like love of progeny, but from secondary principles.<Cite source="hume-natural-history-religion" locator="N 0.1" /> Its first source was not curiosity about nature but "the ordinary affections of human life," the anxious concern for happiness and the dread of misery and death; agitated by such hopes and fears, people "see the first obscure traces of divinity."<Cite source="hume-natural-history-religion" locator="N 2.4–2.5" /> Hume himself professed belief in an intelligent author of nature, though how sincerely is disputed. Darwin traced belief in spiritual agencies to imagination, wonder, curiosity and dreams, and described religious devotion as a complex feeling of love, submission, fear, reverence and gratitude.<Cite source="darwin-descent" locator="ch. III, pp. 93–96" /> Russell put it bluntly: "it is fear of nature that gives rise to religion."<Cite source="russell-what-i-believe" locator="ch. 1" />

Contemporary cognitive science of religion develops these ideas. Some researchers treat religious belief as a byproduct of ordinary cognitive tendencies, such as readiness to detect agents; others argue that religion was an adaptation, for instance one that promoted cooperation in large groups.<Cite source="sep-religion-science" locator="§1.4" /> Neither shows that religion is false.

Humanists need not abandon reverence. Russell rejected the worship of power and urged humans to "worship at the shrine that his own hands have built," honouring ideals of goodness in an indifferent universe.<Cite source="russell-free-mans-worship" locator="paras. 10, 21" />

## What this explains well

The account explains religion's near-universality and its diversity together, without privileging any one religion. It accounts for features of religion that track human hopes and fears, and it supports testable research.

## Christian response

The Christian agrees that religion has psychological and social roots and that much religion is shaped by fear. Scripture says as much about idols. The disagreement concerns the step from explanation to dismissal. Since a natural origin does not settle truth, naturalism's verdict that religion is mistaken depends on its prior judgment that there is no God; that is an ultimate premise, not a scientific finding. The Christian's own explanation, that a sense of deity is natural to humans because God made them to know him, accounts for the same data from the opposite premise.<Cite source="calvin-institutes-beveridge" locator="I.3.1" /> This is underdetermination, not a contradiction in naturalism.

## Pressure questions

- If religious belief has natural causes, does atheistic belief also need a natural history, and does that history bear on its truth?
- Is Russell's reverence for human ideals a form of worship, or a metaphor for moral seriousness?
- If belief in gods arises partly from a readiness to detect agents, which also produces false alarms, is that a reason to discount belief in God as such, or only beliefs formed where the tendency is known to misfire?

## Deep dive

#### Explanation is not evaluation

The scientific study of religion began with natural histories like Hume's and continued with Durkheim's functionalism, Freud's account of religion as wish-fulfilment and William James's psychology of religious experience.<Cite source="sep-religion-science" locator="§1.4" /> Contemporary researchers mostly bracket the truth question. Byproduct theories treat belief in gods as a side effect of cognitive systems that evolved for other tasks. Adaptationist theories ask whether religion helped groups cooperate, as in the debate over moralizing "big gods." Cultures also combine natural and supernatural explanations of the same events rather than treating them as rivals.<Cite source="sep-religion-science" locator="§1.4" /> The CSR literature is cited here through this survey; no single theory is settled.

#### Hume's polytheism thesis

Hume argued that the first religion was polytheism, and that the mind rose only gradually to the idea of one perfect Being.<Cite source="hume-natural-history-religion" locator="N 1.1–1.5" /> His concluding chapter repeats that the frame of nature reveals design, while describing popular religion as a mixture of good and ill.<Cite source="hume-natural-history-religion" locator="N 15.1–15.4" /> The work is therefore best read as a natural history of popular religion, not as an atheist manifesto.

#### Reverence without God

Russell's 1903 essay asks, "Shall we worship Force, or shall we worship Goodness?" and calls the worship of power a prostrate submission to evil.<Cite source="russell-free-mans-worship" locator="paras. 9–10" /> He wants a temple for the worship of human ideals, foreshadowed in music, architecture, reason and poetry.<Cite source="russell-free-mans-worship" locator="para. 14" /> Later Russell held that values are created by human desire, which makes such "worship" a strong metaphor for commitment rather than devotion to a being.<Cite source="russell-what-i-believe" locator="ch. 1" /> Naturalists differ over whether reverence has any proper object at all.
