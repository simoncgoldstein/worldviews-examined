## The view

The "one and the many" is not a question naturalism sets for itself. It can be answered from what naturalists already hold.

The unity is the unity of one natural world. Everything is part of a single spatiotemporal, causally connected order, and for most naturalists everything is physical or built from the physical.<Cite source="sep-physicalism" locator="§§2, 3.1" />

The plurality is real at many levels. Atoms, cells, organisms, minds and societies are different kinds of things, and the sciences that study them use different concepts. Most naturalists treat higher-level properties as real and explanatorily useful while wholly determined by their physical basis.<Cite source="sep-properties-emergent" locator="§§3–4" /> Russell put it simply: "everything interesting is a matter of organization, not of primal substance."<Cite source="russell-what-i-believe" locator="ch. 1" />

Whether wholes are real over and above their parts is debated. Hume's Cleanthes argued that uniting parts into a "whole" is "an arbitrary act of the mind."<Cite source="hume-dialogues" locator="9.9" /> Others treat organisms and systems as genuine unities.

## What this explains well

The view explains how one world can contain endless variety through composition and organization, and it fits the success of science at many levels.

## Christian response

The Christian agrees that the world is one causal order with many real levels, but asks whether naturalism explains that unity or assumes it. That the same laws hold everywhere is, for the naturalist, simply given. The Christian holds that one wise Creator explains why the world is one and why its many parts fit together.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Colossians 1:16–17" /> That is a comparative claim resting on theistic premises.

## Pressure questions

- What makes the world one order rather than a collection of unrelated regularities?
- If wholes are only groupings made by the mind, are organisms and persons any more unified than a heap?

## Deep dive

#### Reductive and non-reductive

Reductive physicalists hold that higher-level kinds are identical to physical kinds. Non-reductive physicalists hold that they are real and distinct but fixed by the physical.<Cite source="sep-physicalism" locator="§3.1" /> Either way, the one is the physical world and the many are its organizations. A small minority accept strong emergence, in which genuinely new causal powers appear at higher levels.<Cite source="sep-properties-emergent" locator="§§3–4" />

#### Abstract unity

Some unities are not physical at all. Mathematics describes structures shared by many systems, and naturalists disagree over whether numbers are real or useful fictions.<Cite source="sep-naturalism" locator="§1.8" /> Here the naturalist answer is incomplete by its own admission, and the question belongs to Knowledge & Truth.

#### Hume's whole

Cleanthes' remark about the "whole" comes from his argument against a cause of the universe. Once each member of a series is explained, he says, asking for a cause of the whole is like asking for the cause of a collection of twenty particles after explaining each one.<Cite source="hume-dialogues" locator="9.9" /> Applied to the one and the many, it suggests that the world's unity may be a way we group things rather than a further fact.
