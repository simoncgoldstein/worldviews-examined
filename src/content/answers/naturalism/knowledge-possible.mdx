## The view

There is no single naturalist theory of knowledge, but there is a shared picture. Knowing is something organisms do. Eyes, memory and inference are natural capacities, shaped by evolution and by learning, that connect a creature to its surroundings. They are reliable because they had to be. Quine put it bluntly: creatures "inveterately wrong in their inductions have a pathetic but praiseworthy tendency to die out before reproducing more of their kind."<Cite source="sep-epistemology-naturalized" locator="§2" note="Quine 1969c, p. 126, as quoted by SEP" /> Two centuries earlier Hume saw nature securing our most necessary inferences by something like instinct rather than slow argument, "a kind of pre-established harmony between the course of nature and the succession of our ideas."<Cite source="hume-enquiry" locator="5.21–22" />

Individual faculties make mistakes. What makes knowledge possible on a larger scale is correction. Instruments extend the senses, experiments test expectations, and other inquirers check our work. Science is this practice at its most disciplined. Many naturalists argue that its success, from predicting eclipses to designing vaccines, is best explained by its theories being at least approximately true; otherwise the success would be a miracle.<Cite source="sep-scientific-realism" locator="§2.1" /> Others are more cautious and point to successful theories that were later abandoned.<Cite source="sep-scientific-realism" locator="§3.3" />

Quine drew the most radical conclusion. There is no "first philosophy" outside science from which knowledge could be certified in advance.<Cite source="sep-quine" locator="§4.4" /> The study of knowledge itself, he wrote, "falls into place as a chapter of psychology and hence of natural science."<Cite source="sep-epistemology-naturalized" locator="§2" note="Quine 1969b, p. 82, as quoted by SEP" /> Moderate naturalists keep more of traditional epistemology's concern with how we *ought* to reason. They ground that "ought" in what reliably produces true belief, which matters to creatures with goals.<Cite source="sep-epistemology-naturalized" locator="§4.2" />

## What this explains well

It explains why our faculties work well in ordinary conditions and fail in predictable ways, and why knowledge grows through public testing rather than private certainty. It fits the evidence that human beings are continuous with other animals, and needs no faculty beyond those that can be studied.

## Christian response

The Christian agrees that perception, memory and inference are real, generally reliable and corrigible. The question is whether natural selection explains the reliability that knowledge needs. Selection rewards behaviour that helps survival and reproduction. That plausibly tracks truth about food and predators. It is less obvious that it tracks truth about quantum fields, mathematics or the moral law, where survival is not at stake.

Naturalists reply that accurate beliefs are the best explanation of how creatures find food and avoid predators, so selection does favour truth, and some offer theories on which a belief's content does causal work.<Cite source="ndpr-naturalism-defeated" locator="Post 2002" /> The debate is real and unresolved. The Christian holds that a mind made by a knowing God for an intelligible world explains the fit between mind and reality more fully than a process that selects for what works. That is a claim of comparative explanatory adequacy, not a charge of contradiction. Hume's "pre-established harmony" names the fit; the Christian thinks it names a design.

## Pressure questions

- If selection favours useful beliefs, what secures true beliefs in domains where usefulness and truth can come apart?

## Deep dive

#### Darwin's doubt and the reliability debate

Darwin once wrote of "the horrid doubt" whether "the convictions of man's mind, which has been developed from the mind of the lower animals, are of any value or at all trustworthy." The context matters. He raised it about his inward conviction that the universe is not the result of chance, not about perception or science in general.<Cite source="darwin-letter-graham-1881" locator="DCP-LETT-13230" />

The Christian philosopher Alvin Plantinga generalized the worry. He argued that a naturalist who accepts evolution has a reason to distrust all his beliefs. A review of the main exchange sets out the naturalist replies:<Cite source="ndpr-naturalism-defeated" locator="Post 2002" />

- truth-tracking pays, so selection favours it (William Ramsey, Jerry Fodor, Evan Fales);
- some beliefs, including trust in our faculties, are justified without needing such support (Michael Bergmann, Ernest Sosa);
- the step from a doubt about reliability to a defeater for every belief is unwarranted (James Van Cleve and others);
- further evidence about our success makes reliability likely again (Richard Otte);
- Ruth Millikan's account of how the content and truth of beliefs play a causal role, which the reviewer calls the "missing strand."

Plantinga's reply, as reported, is that the naturalist still owes an account of how a belief's content, not just its neural structure, makes a causal difference.

#### Hume's two moods

Hume is often read only as a sceptic. He did argue that our inferences about matters of fact rest on custom, not reason (see <QuestionLink question="induction" worldview="naturalism">induction</QuestionLink>). But he also held that the doubt cannot be lived. "Nature, by an absolute and uncontroulable necessity has determin'd us to judge as well as to breathe and feel."<Cite source="hume-treatise" locator="1.4.1.7" /> His considered position is a mitigated scepticism that confines inquiry to common life and experience.<Cite source="hume-enquiry" locator="12.24–25" />

#### Replacement or cooperation

Quine is usually read as proposing that psychology replace the traditional theory of knowledge. Critics object that this loses the normative question of how we should reason, and that answering the sceptic with science is circular. Richard Fumerton calls such responses "blatant, indeed pathetic, circularity."<Cite source="sep-epistemology-naturalized" locator="§3.1" /> Most naturalists today take a cooperative line: epistemology keeps its normative questions but answers them with help from the sciences.<Cite source="sep-epistemology-naturalized" locator="§§4.2, 5" />
