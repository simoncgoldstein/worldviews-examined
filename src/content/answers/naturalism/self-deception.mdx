## The view

Self-deception can be explained through motivated belief formation. On deflationary accounts surveyed in the existing research, desires, fears, or anxieties bias the handling of evidence without requiring a conscious intention to deceive oneself.<Cite source="sep-self-deception" locator="§3.1" /> Russell gives a morally pointed example: treating others as inferior can rationalize cruelty toward them.<Cite source="russell-what-i-believe" locator="ch. 4" /> Someone may know relevant facts while organizing their interpretation around a conclusion that protects his interests or identity.

## What this explains well

The account explains why self-deception need not feel like lying. Selective scrutiny can produce sincere confidence. It also avoids assuming a fully unified agent who deliberately plants a belief he already knows to be false; Dennett describes agency as only partially coherent.<Cite source="dennett-self-artifact" locator="p. 39; pp. 48–49" /> That is explanatory material, not a claim that every naturalist accepts one theory.

## Christian response

The Christian agrees that motivated attention and belief can explain the mechanism. Christianity adds a moral and theological interpretation: people can resist truth because acknowledging it threatens their chosen allegiance.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 1:18–21" /> A naturalistic process account does not by itself decide whether a particular distortion is culpable, adaptive, or an innocent mistake. Nor does identifying a natural mechanism establish that the distortion is morally harmless. The disagreement is over interpretation and accountability, not whether such processes occur.

## Deep dive

#### Intention and evolutionary advantage are separate questions

Intentionalist theories face two familiar difficulties: how one person can hold incompatible beliefs, and how a conscious intention to deceive could succeed without defeating itself.<Cite source="sep-self-deception" locator="§1" /> A non-intentional account answers by locating the distortion in the processes which form or sustain belief. This does not require saying that people never deliberately avoid evidence; it denies that such an intention is necessary in every case.

A further question is why these capacities exist. Proposed evolutionary accounts treat self-deception as advantageous for positive illusions or deceiving others; alternatives treat it as a by-product of other capacities. The checked survey records disputed evidence rather than an established universal function.<Cite source="sep-self-deception" locator="§6" /> This page therefore does not present an adaptation hypothesis as settled science.

For broader accounts of competing motives, see <QuestionLink question="fail-the-good" worldview="naturalism">moral failure</QuestionLink>. For the naturalist account of human capacities and moral disagreement, see <QuestionLink question="great-and-terrible" worldview="naturalism">the flagship</QuestionLink>. Here the narrow explanation concerns how motivation can distort belief, not a proof that every belief arising through evolved processes is unreliable.
