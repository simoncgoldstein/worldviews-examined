## The view

Self-deception can be explained through motivated belief formation. On deflationary accounts, desires, fears, or anxieties bias the handling of evidence without requiring a conscious intention to deceive oneself.<Cite source="sep-self-deception" locator="§3.1" /> Russell gives a morally pointed example: treating others as inferior can rationalize cruelty toward them.<Cite source="russell-what-i-believe" locator="ch. 4" /> Someone may know the relevant facts while organizing his interpretation of them around a conclusion that protects his interests or identity.

## What this explains well

The account explains why self-deception need not feel like lying. Selective scrutiny can produce sincere confidence. It also avoids assuming a fully unified agent who deliberately plants a belief he already knows to be false; Dennett describes agency as only partially coherent.<Cite source="dennett-self-artifact" locator="p. 39; pp. 48–49" /> Naturalists need not all accept one theory of it.

## Christian response

The Christian agrees that motivated attention and belief can explain the mechanism. Christianity adds a moral and theological interpretation: people can resist truth because acknowledging it threatens their chosen allegiance.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 1:18–21" /> A naturalistic process account does not by itself decide whether a particular distortion is culpable, adaptive, or an innocent mistake. Nor does identifying a natural mechanism establish that the distortion is morally harmless. Each side can also describe the other's belief as motivated: the Christian sees resistance to God, the naturalist may see wishful thinking in faith. Neither description settles the dispute without argument. The disagreement is over interpretation and accountability, not whether such processes occur.

## Deep dive

#### Intention and evolutionary advantage are separate questions

Intentionalist theories face two familiar difficulties: how one person can hold incompatible beliefs, and how a conscious intention to deceive could succeed without defeating itself.<Cite source="sep-self-deception" locator="§1" /> A non-intentional account answers by locating the distortion in the processes which form or sustain belief. This does not require saying that people never deliberately avoid evidence; it denies that such an intention is necessary in every case.

A further question is why these capacities exist. Proposed evolutionary accounts treat self-deception as advantageous for positive illusions or deceiving others; alternatives treat it as a by-product of other capacities. The proposals differ over function and supporting evidence.<Cite source="sep-self-deception" locator="§6" /> An adaptation hypothesis is not yet an established function.

For broader accounts of competing motives, see <QuestionLink question="fail-the-good" worldview="naturalism">moral failure</QuestionLink>. For the naturalist account of human capacities and moral disagreement, see <QuestionLink question="great-and-terrible" worldview="naturalism">the flagship</QuestionLink>. The account explains how motivation can distort belief; it does not show that every belief formed by evolved processes is unreliable.
