## The view

Naturalism has no need for an independent evil substance or a supernatural power to explain harmful conduct. It can investigate the motives, capacities and social conditions through which people injure one another. Hume identifies limited generosity and the scarcity of external goods as conditions calling for the conventions of justice.<Cite source="hume-treatise" locator="3.2.2.18" /> Mackie similarly connects morality's practical function with limited sympathy.<Cite source="mackie-ethics" locator="p. 108" /> On these accounts not every wrong is a calculation of private advantage; the point is that social order cannot rely on unlimited spontaneous benevolence.

Natural explanations also distinguish forms of aggression. Wrangham contrasts reactive aggression with planned, proactive aggression; reduced impulsive violence can coexist with organized destructive action.<Cite source="wrangham-two-types" locator="Abstract; Proactive vs. Reactive Aggression" /> Darwin's account of group-supporting virtues likewise connects cooperation within a group with its success in competition against others.<Cite source="darwin-descent" locator="ch. V, p. 130" /> Capacities valuable in one setting can participate in harm in another.

Whether those harms are objectively wrong is a further question. Railton defends naturalist moral realism, while Mackie rejects objective values.<Cite source="railton-moral-realism" locator="pp. 190–194" /><Cite source="mackie-ethics" locator="pp. 15, 42–43" /> Neither speaks for all naturalists. There is also disagreement about the word evil itself: some retain it for especially serious wrongs, while skeptics question its explanatory value and dangers.<Cite source="sep-concept-evil" locator="§§1–1.2; §3.1" />

Natural harms require a different use of the word. A destructive natural process need not possess an intention which can be blamed. Its consequences can nevertheless be terrible for those affected. In debates about God, "natural evil" names such harms without implying that nature is a wicked agent: it picks out the harms whose existence and distribution rival metaphysical accounts try to explain.<Cite source="hume-dialogues" locator="11.6–11.11" /> The distinction keeps culpability attached to agents while allowing a serious comparison about the world's character.

## What this explains well

This approach allows concrete explanations of harmful conduct without treating cruelty as mysterious simply because it is severe. Planning, group loyalty and institutional coordination can increase destructive power even where uncontrolled rage is restrained. The same account distinguishes explaining a behavior from approving it: a natural cause is not thereby a good one, and understanding a cause leaves room for prevention and criticism. Keeping realism and anti-realism distinct also stops an evolutionary history of conduct from passing for a complete moral theory.

## Christian response

The Christian agrees that limited sympathy, distorted desire and organized aggression can explain particular wrongs, but asks about the standard by which they are condemned. Christianity locates that standard in God's law and treats evil as the corruption of created good.<Cite source="westminster-larger-catechism" locator="Q. 24" /><Cite source="augustine-enchiridion" locator="11, 13" /> Against Mackie, the Christian holds that the condemnation of cruelty is true, not projected. With Railton, he shares the conviction that cruelty is objectively wrong, and the question becomes what gives that verdict authority over the cruel person (see <QuestionLink question="know-the-good" worldview="naturalism">moral knowledge</QuestionLink>). The Christian also claims an explanatory advantage at a narrower point. The naturalist can already say, with Darwin and Wrangham, that capacities valuable in one setting serve harm in another, and a naturalist realist condemns cruelty as wrong, not merely as harmful. The Christian adds that intelligence, loyalty and power were made for an end, so that cruelty is a departure from what they are for, not only a harmful use of them; that is the sense in which he calls evil parasitic on good. What naturalism does not supply is a given end from which the capacity departs. These are disagreements about grounding and explanation; naturalists draw real moral distinctions. Natural suffering creates a further dispute, and this one presses on Christianity: Philo argues that its distribution fits indifference better than perfect benevolence.<Cite source="hume-dialogues" locator="11.13–11.15" />

## Pressure questions

- What makes an extreme wrong evil, and does the additional label improve explanation or judgment?
- How does a causal history of moral responses relate to the truth or authority of those responses?

## Deep dive

#### Causes do not settle the verdict

An explanation of limited sympathy can show why cooperative life needs rules. It does not decide every rule's content or show that whatever a group rewards is right. Likewise, an evolutionary account of cooperation may illuminate how dispositions developed without proving that their present objects are morally justified. Darwin's example of group success is particularly important here: a trait can benefit a community's cohesion while participating in harm beyond it.<Cite source="darwin-descent" locator="ch. V, p. 130" /> Description and evaluation require a connection that must be argued, not assumed.

Wrangham's proactive/reactive distinction corrects another simplification. Human destructiveness is not merely a residue of uncontrolled animal rage. Planned aggression can draw upon patience, intelligence and coordination.<Cite source="wrangham-two-types" locator="Proactive vs. Reactive Aggression; Resolving the Rousseau–Kropotkin vs. Hobbes–Huxley Debate" /> The contrast does not excuse any particular aggressor or diagnose every atrocity; it explains how reduced impulsiveness and extreme organized harm can coexist.

#### Three distinct philosophical disagreements

First, moral realists and anti-realists disagree about moral truth. Railton attempts to connect objective moral assessment with informed interests and an impartial standpoint. Mackie's error theory denies the objective values claimed in ordinary moral judgments, while retaining a practical role for constructed morality.<Cite source="railton-moral-realism" locator="pp. 190–194" /><Cite source="mackie-ethics" locator="pp. 15, 42–43, 108" /> Neither view follows automatically from the observation that behavior has natural causes. <QuestionLink question="know-the-good" worldview="naturalism">Knowledge of the good</QuestionLink> develops that dispute.

Second, evil-skeptics and defenders of evil-talk disagree about the concept's usefulness. Skeptics worry that it imports misleading metaphysics, supplies a pseudo-explanation or encourages dangerous classifications of people. Defenders consider whether it marks wrongs of distinctive seriousness or character.<Cite source="sep-concept-evil" locator="§§1.1–1.2; §3.1" /> Retaining the word need not entail belief in a supernatural evil force; abandoning it need not mean tolerating cruelty.

Third, the argument from the world's suffering concerns theism. Philo concedes that suffering may be compatible with perfect goodness under additional assumptions, while denying that observed nature allows the inference to that goodness.<Cite source="hume-dialogues" locator="11.4–11.5" /> His four circumstances then examine features of the system, not only isolated wicked acts.<Cite source="hume-dialogues" locator="11.6–11.11" /> These are Philo's arguments in Hume's dialogue, not a creed every naturalist holds.

The two challenges run in opposite directions. The Christian presses naturalism on moral grounding; Philo presses Christianity on the evidence for God's goodness, asking what sort of world one would expect from the proposed source. Each has to be answered on its own terms. Naturalism's lack of providential meaning does not make its causal account defective, and the Christian reply to Philo is given in <QuestionLink question="evil" worldview="christianity">the Christian answer</QuestionLink>.
