## The view

Naturalism has no need for an independent evil substance or a supernatural power to explain harmful conduct. It can investigate the motives, capacities and social conditions through which people injure one another. Hume identifies limited generosity and the scarcity of external goods as conditions calling for the conventions of justice.<Cite source="hume-treatise" locator="3.2.2.18" /> Mackie similarly connects morality's practical function with limited sympathy.<Cite source="mackie-ethics" locator="p. 108" /> Such accounts do not make every wrong a calculation of private advantage, but they explain why social order cannot rely on unlimited spontaneous benevolence.

Natural explanations also distinguish forms of aggression. Wrangham contrasts reactive aggression with planned, proactive aggression; reduced impulsive violence can coexist with organized destructive action.<Cite source="wrangham-two-types" locator="Abstract; Proactive vs. Reactive Aggression" /> Darwin's account of group-supporting virtues likewise connects cooperation within a group with its success in competition against others.<Cite source="darwin-descent" locator="ch. V, p. 130" /> Capacities valuable in one setting can participate in harm in another.

Whether those harms are objectively wrong is a further question. Railton defends naturalist moral realism, while Mackie rejects objective values.<Cite source="railton-moral-realism" locator="pp. 190–194" /><Cite source="mackie-ethics" locator="pp. 15, 42–43" /> Neither can represent all naturalists. There is also disagreement about the word evil itself: some retain it for especially serious wrongs, while skeptics question its explanatory value and dangers.<Cite source="sep-concept-evil" locator="§§1–1.2; §3.1" />

Natural harms require a different use of the word. A destructive natural process need not possess an intention which can be blamed. Its consequences can nevertheless be terrible for those affected. Calling this natural evil in a debate about God does not commit the naturalist to nature's wicked agency. It identifies the harms whose existence or distribution the competing metaphysical accounts attempt to explain.<Cite source="hume-dialogues" locator="11.6–11.11" /> The distinction keeps culpability attached to agents while allowing a serious comparison about the world's character.

## What this explains well

This approach allows concrete explanations of harmful conduct without treating cruelty as mysterious simply because it is severe. Planning, group loyalty and institutional coordination can increase destructive power even where uncontrolled rage is restrained. The same account can distinguish explaining a behaviour from approving it. A cause's being natural does not alone establish its goodness, nor does recognizing a cause require abandoning prevention or criticism. The realism/anti-realism distinction also prevents an evolutionary history of conduct from silently becoming a complete moral theory.

## Christian response

The Christian agrees that limited sympathy, distorted desire and organized aggression can explain particular wrongs, but asks about the standard by which they are condemned. Christianity locates that standard in God's law and treats evil as corruption of created good.<Cite source="westminster-larger-catechism" locator="Q. 24" /><Cite source="augustine-enchiridion" locator="11, 13" /> A fair comparison must engage Railton's attempted objective grounding as well as Mackie's rejection of it. It cannot simply charge every naturalist with having no moral distinctions. Natural suffering creates a further dispute: Philo argues that its distribution fits indifference better than perfect benevolence.<Cite source="hume-dialogues" locator="11.13–11.15" />

## Pressure questions

- What makes an extreme wrong evil, and does the additional label improve explanation or judgment?
- How does a causal history of moral responses relate to the truth or authority of those responses?

## Deep dive

#### Causes do not settle the verdict

An explanation of limited sympathy can show why cooperative life needs rules. It does not decide every rule's content or show that whatever a group rewards is right. Likewise, an evolutionary account of cooperation may illuminate how dispositions developed without proving that their present objects are morally justified. Darwin's example of group success is particularly important here: a trait can benefit a community's cohesion while participating in harm beyond it.<Cite source="darwin-descent" locator="ch. V, p. 130" /> Description and evaluation require a connection that must be argued, not assumed.

Wrangham's proactive/reactive distinction prevents another simplification. Human destructiveness is not merely a residue of uncontrolled animal rage. Planned aggression can draw upon patience, intelligence and coordination.<Cite source="wrangham-two-types" locator="Proactive vs. Reactive Aggression; Resolving the Rousseau–Kropotkin vs. Hobbes–Huxley Debate" /> The contrast does not establish that any particular aggressor lacked responsibility, and it is not a comprehensive diagnosis of every atrocity. It supplies a narrower explanation of how reduced impulsiveness and extreme organized harm can coexist.

#### Three distinct philosophical disagreements

First, moral realists and anti-realists disagree about moral truth. Railton attempts to connect objective moral assessment with informed interests and an impartial standpoint. Mackie's error theory denies the objective values claimed in ordinary moral judgments, while retaining a practical role for constructed morality.<Cite source="railton-moral-realism" locator="pp. 190–194" /><Cite source="mackie-ethics" locator="pp. 15, 42–43, 108" /> Neither view follows automatically from the observation that behaviour has natural causes. <QuestionLink question="know-the-good" worldview="naturalism">Knowledge of the good</QuestionLink> develops that dispute.

Second, evil-skeptics and defenders of evil-talk disagree about the concept's usefulness. Skeptics worry that it imports misleading metaphysics, supplies a pseudo-explanation or encourages dangerous classifications of people. Defenders consider whether it marks wrongs of distinctive seriousness or character.<Cite source="sep-concept-evil" locator="§§1.1–1.2; §3.1" /> Retaining the word need not entail belief in a supernatural evil force; abandoning it need not mean tolerating cruelty.

Third, the argument from the world's suffering concerns theism. Philo concedes that suffering may be compatible with perfect goodness under additional assumptions, while denying that observed nature allows the inference to that goodness.<Cite source="hume-dialogues" locator="11.4–11.5" /> His four circumstances then examine features of the system, not only isolated wicked acts.<Cite source="hume-dialogues" locator="11.6–11.11" /> These are arguments voiced by Philo in Hume's dialogue, rather than a single unqualified assertion by every naturalist.

<QuestionLink question="suffering" worldview="naturalism">Suffering</QuestionLink> treats the absence of a cosmic purpose and the limits of particular biological explanations. <QuestionLink question="fail-the-good" worldview="naturalism">Failure to do the good</QuestionLink> and <QuestionLink question="self-deception" worldview="naturalism">self-deception</QuestionLink> concern motivation and belief. Keeping those questions distinct allows naturalism's causal strengths and its moral disagreements to be assessed together.

The argument from suffering also needs a standard of comparison. Philo asks what sort of world one would expect from the proposed source, and disputes the inference from the observed world to perfect benevolence. This is not the claim that naturalism's lack of providential meaning makes its causal account defective. The Christian challenge to naturalism concerns moral grounding; Philo's challenge to Christianity concerns the evidence for divine character. The two questions may interact, but neither can substitute for answering the other.
