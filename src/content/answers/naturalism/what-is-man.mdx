## The view

For naturalism a human being is a living organism, wholly part of nature, related by descent to every other animal. Russell put it plainly: "Man is a part of Nature, not something contrasted with Nature." He added that what we call our thoughts depend on the organization of the brain, and that the soul is not a single persisting entity but a history, a series of events.<Cite source="russell-what-i-believe" locator="ch. 1" /> Darwin closed *The Descent of Man* by saying that man, with all his noble qualities, still bears in his bodily frame the "indelible stamp of his lowly origin."<Cite source="darwin-descent" locator="ch. XXI, p. 619" />

Human distinctiveness is real but natural. Darwin argued that an animal with strong social instincts would acquire a moral sense once its intellect had developed as far as ours.<Cite source="darwin-descent" locator="ch. IV, p. 98" /> Language, teaching and imitation let each generation keep and improve what earlier ones discovered, so tools, science, art and institutions accumulate as in no other species.<Cite source="tennie-ratchet" locator="Abstract" /> The difference from other animals lies in these capacities and their cultural amplification, not in an added soul.

The self, on this view, is real without being a separate thing. Dennett describes it as a "center of narrative gravity": not an inner soul or a single brain module, but the organized point of view of an agent that can ask and give reasons and answer to others for what it does.<Cite source="dennett-self-artifact" locator="pp. 48–49" /> What makes someone the same person over time is disputed. Psychological-continuity theories stress memory and character; animalist theories identify us with the living organism.<Cite source="sep-personal-identity" locator="§§3–4" /> Naturalists also differ on whether mental life can be reduced to physics or is a real higher-level feature of physical systems.<Cite source="sep-naturalism" locator="§1" />

Conscious experience raises a further question. When we see red or feel pain, the brain discriminates and reports, and there is also something it is like to be in that state. Most naturalists are physicalists, who hold that experience is a brain process or is realized in one, chiefly, on David Papineau's account, because physical effects appear to have sufficient physical causes.<Cite source="papineau-rise-physicalism" locator="pp. 3–4, 8–9, 30–33" /> David Chalmers, a critic of that view, argues that explaining what the brain does still leaves a further question: why that activity is accompanied by experience at all. He calls it the hard problem.<Cite source="chalmers-facing-up" locator="§§2–3" /> Naturalists answer it differently. Many physicalists locate the gap in how we think about experience, not in what exists; illusionists hold that introspection presents experience as having qualitative feels it does not really have; and a few make experience, or its precursors, a basic feature of matter.

## What this explains well

The account fits the evidence of continuity: shared anatomy and instincts, gradations of intelligence across species, and the dependence of thought and experience on the brain, where injury or chemistry can change memory, personality and awareness. It explains human uniqueness without special pleading, through social learning and cumulative culture. It treats the self as something that develops, can fragment and can be rebuilt, which matches ordinary experience of growth, illness and ageing. And it keeps claims about human nature answerable to ongoing scientific enquiry.

## Christian response

The Christian grants the continuity with animals and the dependence of mind on the brain; Scripture itself says humans are formed from dust.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Genesis 2:7" /> The disagreement lies elsewhere. Dennett's self is defined by the practice of asking and giving reasons and answering for what one does; whether minds shaped by selection can trust those reasons as true, and not only as useful, is pressed under <QuestionLink question="knowledge-possible" worldview="naturalism">why we can know anything</QuestionLink>.

A second question concerns worth. If what is distinctive about humans is a set of capacities that vary in degree, equal dignity needs another ground; naturalists supply several, as <QuestionLink question="great-and-terrible" worldview="naturalism">the account of man's greatness and misery</QuestionLink> shows. The Christian locates that ground in God's image, a premise drawn from revelation that the naturalist does not share.

A third question concerns consciousness. Naturalists dispute whether physical explanation accounts for there being a subject of experience, and each answer has a cost: a gap assigned to our concepts, an introspection that misleads, or experiential matter that must combine into one person. If a conscious God made embodied souls, mind does not arise from what wholly lacks it, so subjects of experience are less surprising; panpsychists seek a similar advantage without God. That is a challenge of explanatory adequacy resting on theistic premises, not a demonstrated contradiction, and it proves no soul. The Christian has no neurological theory of consciousness either, and Bavinck calls the union of soul and body incomprehensible.<Cite source="bavinck-reformed-dogmatics-monergism-2" locator="Vol. 2, §33.9, PDF pp. 665–666" note="automated English translation; paraphrased" />

## Pressure questions

- If the self is a center of narrative gravity, what makes it the same self that is responsible for yesterday's promise?
- Papineau holds that pain simply is a brain process, as water is H₂O, and that the sense of something left out is an intuition of dualism.<Cite source="papineau-explanatory-gap" locator="pp. 6, 11–13" /> If so, what produces that intuition, and why does it attach to experience but not to other identities that cannot be derived from physics either?<Cite source="papineau-explanatory-gap" locator="pp. 9, 14–19" />

## Deep dive

#### Which naturalism?

Naturalism holds that reality contains only natural entities and that knowledge of human beings is continuous with the sciences; it does not by itself settle every question in the philosophy of mind.<Cite source="sep-naturalism" locator="§§1–2" /> Reductive naturalists expect mental states to be explained by physical ones; nonreductive naturalists regard beliefs, reasons and persons as real higher-level features of physical systems. Russell's own view was subtler than materialism: he thought mind and matter alike were convenient terms rather than ultimate realities, and that physics too deals in histories of events, not enduring substances.<Cite source="russell-what-i-believe" locator="ch. 1" /> Atheism does not entail physicalism, and the view represented here is the mainstream evolutionary naturalism that most philosophical naturalists share.

#### Degrees of difference

Darwin treated human mental and moral powers as developments of capacities found in other animals, strengthened by intelligence and habit. The social instincts, including parental and filial affection, were the starting point for conscience.<Cite source="darwin-descent" locator="ch. IV, p. 98" /> He also traced the sense of beauty and the beginnings of religious belief to faculties shared, in simpler form, with animals.<Cite source="darwin-descent" locator="ch. III, pp. 92–96" /> The distinctiveness most naturalists now emphasize is cumulative culture: humans transmit innovations faithfully enough that each generation can build on the last.<Cite source="tennie-ratchet" locator="Abstract; §5" /> Sex and reproduction belong to the same history. Darwin judged general promiscuity in early human society improbable and gave sexual selection a large role in shaping human bodies and behaviour.<Cite source="darwin-descent" locator="ch. XX, pp. 590–591" /> These are explanations of origin; they do not by themselves settle questions of value.

#### Self, agency and persistence

Dennett's agent adds to animal action a capacity to represent and evaluate its own reasons. That lets humans redesign themselves, and it is why they can be held responsible.<Cite source="dennett-self-artifact" locator="p. 48" /> The self that results is only partially coherent, which helps explain inner conflict.<Cite source="dennett-self-artifact" locator="p. 39" /> On persistence, psychological theories make memory and character central, while animalism says we are organisms that can survive the loss of both. These views also distinguish persisting from keeping the same character, so dramatic change does not automatically mean a different individual.<Cite source="sep-personal-identity" locator="§§1–4, 7" /> Most naturalists hold that the person ends when the organism dies, as <QuestionLink question="after-death" worldview="naturalism">the after-death question</QuestionLink> explains.

#### The hard problem

Four questions need to be kept apart: which brain processes accompany experience (neuroscience's question); why any processing is accompanied by experience at all (the hard problem); whether experience is physical, fundamental or something else (the mind–body problem); and what makes someone the same person over time (treated above). Chalmers calls report, attention and wakefulness "easy" problems because they are functions, which cognitive science can in principle explain; experience is not defined by any function. His argument is conceptual, not a laboratory finding, and his answer, "naturalistic dualism", adds experience to the world's basic properties: a dualism of properties, not of souls.<Cite source="chalmers-facing-up" locator="§§2–3, 5–6" /> Neither neuroscience's successes nor arguments from what can be conceived settle the metaphysical question alone.

#### Three replies

Physicalists treat mind–brain identities as a posteriori discoveries, like water = H₂O. On the common view the felt gap arises because we think of experiences directly, by what they are like, so they cannot be derived a priori from physics; the gap is in our concepts, not in reality, and few accept Chalmers' step to the falsity of physicalism. Papineau rejects even this account: identities leave nothing unexplained, and the felt gap is an intuition of dualism that even materialists cannot shake.<Cite source="papineau-explanatory-gap" locator="pp. 7–8, 11–13" />

Illusionists, a minority, hold that experiences have no phenomenal properties, physical or nonphysical: introspection misrepresents them as having qualitative feels. They do not deny conscious experience or an inner life, only that these consist in phenomenal properties.<Cite source="frankish-illusionism" locator="introduction; §§1.1–1.2, 1.6–1.7" /> Dennett, the best-known illusionist, says the slogan "consciousness is an illusion" misreads him; what he denies is an inner medium of qualia.<Cite source="dennett-consciousness-seem" locator="§§1–3" />

Panpsychists make simple experience a basic feature of matter; Galen Strawson argues that any physicalism realistic about experience requires this, since experience cannot emerge from what wholly lacks it.<Cite source="sep-panpsychism" locator="§3.1" /> Panprotopsychists posit precursors of experience instead. Russellian monists, after Russell's *Analysis of Matter* (1927), may take either form: physics gives only matter's structure, and its intrinsic nature grounds consciousness.<Cite source="chalmers-panpsychism-panprotopsychism" locator="pp. 15–16, 18" /><Cite source="sep-panpsychism" locator="§2.5" /> Each faces a combination problem: how tiny subjects could make one unified subject, or how properties that are not experiences could add up to experience.<Cite source="chalmers-panpsychism-panprotopsychism" locator="pp. 22–25" /> Chalmers himself is "by no means confident" that panpsychism is true,<Cite source="chalmers-panpsychism-panprotopsychism" locator="p. 2" /> and it is not naturalist as such: Spinoza and Leibniz held forms of it within accounts of God.<Cite source="sep-panpsychism" locator="§1" />

#### Place in reality

There is no ultimate reality above nature to which humans relate as creatures, and no assigned cosmic role. Russell calls man the product of causes that had no prevision of the end they were achieving.<Cite source="russell-free-mans-worship" locator="para. 5" /> What humans are does not dictate what they are for; whether life still has meaning, and where it comes from, is taken up in <QuestionLink question="why-alive" worldview="naturalism">the purpose question</QuestionLink>.

#### Which humans?

Darwin's text ranks peoples in ways contemporary naturalists reject;<Cite source="darwin-descent" locator="ch. IV, pp. 121–123" /> the anthropology above is the one that survives that correction. Naturalists ground dignity in various ways: in reason-giving agency, in the moral demand that all interests count equally, or in a widely shared sentiment of humanity.<Cite source="railton-moral-realism" locator="p. 190" /><Cite source="hume-epm" locator="9.5–9.6" /> No single grounding is accepted by all.
