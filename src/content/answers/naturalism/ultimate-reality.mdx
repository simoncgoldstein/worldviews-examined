## The view

Naturalism's basic claim is negative before it is positive: there is no God, no spirits and no supernatural realm standing behind or above the world. Reality is the natural order of space, time, matter and energy, the order the sciences investigate.<Cite source="sep-naturalism" locator="§1.1" /> Naturalists do not usually hold this as a dogma. They hold it as the conclusion that best fits the evidence. Russell put the attitude plainly: he could not prove there is no God, but gods lie "outside the region of even probable knowledge," so there is no reason to posit them.<Cite source="russell-what-i-believe" locator="ch. 1" />

There is no naturalist creed about what nature is made of. Most contemporary naturalists are *physicalists*: they hold that everything that exists is physical, or is constituted by, realized by or grounded in the physical.<Cite source="sep-physicalism" locator="§§1–2" /> Life, minds and societies are real. They are not extra ingredients; they are complex organizations of physical things. Russell himself was not a materialist. He thought mind and matter were both convenient constructions out of events, and that "everything interesting is a matter of organization, not of primal substance."<Cite source="russell-what-i-believe" locator="ch. 1" /> Other naturalists allow genuinely new properties to emerge at higher levels.<Cite source="sep-properties-emergent" locator="§§3–4" />

## What this explains well

Naturalism fits the history of inquiry, in which phenomena once credited to spirits have repeatedly turned out to have natural causes. It is economical: it posits one order rather than two. It explains why minds depend so closely on brains, as <QuestionLink question="what-is-man" worldview="naturalism">the account of human nature</QuestionLink> describes. And it allows open questions to stay open instead of filling them with a supernatural answer.

## Christian response

The Christian agrees that the natural world is real, orderly and open to investigation; creation doctrine expects exactly that. The disagreement is over whether nature is the whole of reality. That claim is not a result of physics. It is a philosophical judgment about what the results of physics require. Naturalists themselves distinguish the scientific method from the ontological thesis.<Cite source="sep-naturalism" locator="§2" />

The Christian presses two points. First, the naturalist takes the existence and basic character of the physical order as a starting point. That is a stopping place, not an explanation, and the Christian holds that a self-existent personal God is a better one. That comparison depends on theistic premises, and the Christian should say so. Second, naturalists disagree among themselves about numbers and logical truths, which do not obviously sit in space and time.<Cite source="sep-naturalism" locator="§1.8" /> This is not a contradiction. It is an unfinished account at the point where reasoning itself is grounded, a theme for Knowledge & Truth.

## Pressure questions

- If the natural order is all there is, is its existence simply a brute starting point? If so, what makes that a better stopping place than a self-existent God?
- Are numbers and logical laws real? If they are, in what sense is nature all there is? If they are useful fictions, why is mathematics indispensable to science?

## Deep dive

#### Naturalism is broader than physicalism

The two words are often used interchangeably, but they are not the same. Naturalism denies the supernatural. Physicalism is a particular positive thesis about what the natural is. Stoljar's survey distinguishes physicalism defined by the physical sciences from physicalism defined by supervenience or grounding, and reductive from non-reductive forms.<Cite source="sep-physicalism" locator="§§2, 3.1" /> A non-reductive physicalist holds that psychology and biology describe real kinds that cannot be translated into physics, though everything about them is fixed by the physical. Some naturalists sit outside physicalism altogether. Panpsychists make experience a basic feature of matter, and that sits awkwardly with the usual idea of "the physical."<Cite source="sep-physicalism" locator="§4.4" />

#### Russell's neutral monism

Russell begins *What I Believe* with the claim that man is part of nature, and that his thoughts and movements follow the same laws as stars and atoms. He then denies that either mind or matter is ultimate. Electrons and souls alike are "logical fictions," each really a series of events.<Cite source="russell-what-i-believe" locator="ch. 1" /> This is naturalism without materialism. The basic stuff is neither mental nor material, and the familiar categories are built from it.

#### Emergence

O'Connor distinguishes weak from strong emergence. In weak emergence, higher-level properties are real and explanatorily useful but wholly determined by physical processes. In strong emergence, genuinely new causal powers appear at higher levels.<Cite source="sep-properties-emergent" locator="§§3–4" /> Most naturalists accept the first. The second is a minority view, because it sits in tension with the causal closure of the physical, which is a main motive for physicalism.<Cite source="sep-naturalism" locator="§1.1" />

#### The abstract-objects problem

Mathematics is the hardest case. Some naturalists accept numbers as real because science cannot do without them (an argument associated with Quine and Putnam). Others treat mathematical talk as a useful fiction (Field). A third group says that necessary truths trivially supervene on anything, so they pose no threat.<Cite source="sep-naturalism" locator="§1.8" /><Cite source="sep-physicalism" locator="§5.5" /> These are live options, not a settled doctrine.
