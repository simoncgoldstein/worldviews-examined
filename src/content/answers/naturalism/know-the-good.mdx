## The view

On naturalist accounts, moral awareness grows out of ordinary human capacities rather than a separate faculty requiring revelation. Darwin proposes that social instincts joined to sufficiently developed intelligence produce a moral sense: persistent concern for others can become a standard against which conduct is remembered and judged.<Cite source="darwin-descent" locator="ch. IV, pp. 97–98, 113–114" /> Hume emphasizes sympathy and the correction of narrow perspective through a more general point of view.<Cite source="hume-treatise" locator="3.3.1.15" />

That genealogy does not settle whether its judgments are true. Railton defends naturalistic moral realism: moral assessment considers interests from a social point of view and can be corrected through feedback.<Cite source="railton-moral-realism" locator="pp. 190–194" /> Mackie's error theory denies objective values and explains their apparent objectivity through projection; moral practices can still serve the task of counteracting limited sympathy.<Cite source="mackie-ethics" locator="p. 15; pp. 42–43; p. 108" /> Error theory is one naturalist option among others, not the definition of naturalism.

## What this explains well

The approach explains why moral awareness is socially patterned yet capable of revision. Our sympathy need not remain confined to its earliest circle; reflection can widen concern beyond a group.<Cite source="darwin-descent" locator="ch. IV, pp. 122–123" /> It also separates the origin of a moral capacity from the justification of its verdicts. Naturalists can therefore disagree about realism while sharing the same accounts of learning, cooperation, and motivational conflict: the empirical picture does not wait on the metaethical verdict.

## Christian response

The Christian grants the explanatory force of social learning and sympathy; conscience can have a natural history. He reads the recognition of obligation as conscience bearing witness to God's law.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 2:14–15" /> The real dispute is over what makes a moral standard authoritative, and the two naturalist positions meet it at different places. Mackie denies the objective values the Christian affirms, so the disagreement with him concerns moral realism itself. Railton affirms objective moral facts, so the question for him is narrower.

Hume's "sensible knave," as Railton develops him, admits that his conduct is unjust and asks, "But what is that to me?"<Cite source="railton-moral-realism" locator="p. 169" /> Railton keeps him obligated. The knave's desires cannot exempt him, and the obligation has general grounds, as the demands of logic do. What Railton concedes is that the obligation is not categorical: it need not give every rational agent a reason of his own to comply.<Cite source="railton-moral-realism" locator="pp. 201–203" /> The Christian presses at exactly that concession. On Railton's account the knave is truly bound, yet he may have no reason to care that he is. On the Christian account the one whose claim binds him is also the Creator to whom he belongs, before whom he must give account, and in whom his own good lies, so the obligation gives every person a reason, not only a standard he falls short of.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="James 4:12" /><Cite source="westminster-shorter-catechism" locator="Q. 1" /> Railton can reply that this may be a limitation any account must live with, and that the knave can ask of God's claim too what it is to him. The Christian answer is that the question then concerns not a standpoint the knave may decline but the one on whom his existence and his happiness depend (see <QuestionLink question="know-the-good" worldview="christianity">the Christian answer</QuestionLink>). That is an argument about which account better explains moral authority, not a proof that naturalist realism is incoherent or that naturalists cannot condemn cruelty. A history of conscience by itself neither establishes nor discredits either view.

## Pressure questions

- What distinguishes moral learning which tracks truth from changes which merely make a group more successful or agreeable?
- If an obligation can bind someone who has no reason of his own to comply, as Railton allows, what is lost when moral demands are not reasons for everyone they bind?

## Deep dive

#### Origin, truth, and reliability

Three questions often travel together but should be kept apart: how capacities evolved, which actions are right, and what moral claims mean or refer to. The discussion of morality and biology explicitly distinguishes these inquiries.<Cite source="sep-morality-biology" locator="§1.1" /> An evolutionary explanation of a belief can be relevant to its reliability without automatically settling its content.

This is where debunking arguments enter. If selection favored dispositions for fitness rather than truth, a realist needs an account of why those dispositions nevertheless support trustworthy moral beliefs. Replies appeal, among other things, to reflection which is not fixed directly by the initial evolutionary shaping. The debate remains unsettled.<Cite source="sep-morality-biology" locator="§4.1" />

Railton's distinction between being obligated and having a personal reason to conform preserves objectivity while leaving the relation between obligation and motivation contested.<Cite source="railton-moral-realism" locator="p. 201" /> Mackie's objection works at a different level: he rejects the objective values he thinks ordinary moral claims presuppose. Neither position follows from Darwin's account of conscience alone.

The Christian appeal to God is likewise a claim about ultimate grounding that has to be argued for; finding a biological mechanism incomplete in detail would not establish it. The fuller comparison of naturalist anthropology and moral alternatives is in <QuestionLink question="great-and-terrible" worldview="naturalism">the flagship</QuestionLink>. <QuestionLink question="fail-the-good" worldview="naturalism">Moral failure</QuestionLink> treats the separate question of why recognized reasons lose to other motives.
