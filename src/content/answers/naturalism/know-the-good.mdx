## The view

Darwin proposes that social instincts joined to sufficiently developed intelligence produce a moral sense: persistent concern for others can become a standard against which conduct is remembered and judged.<Cite source="darwin-descent" locator="ch. IV, pp. 97–98, 113–114" /> Hume emphasizes sympathy and the correction of narrow perspective through a more general point of view.<Cite source="hume-treatise" locator="3.3.1.15" /> On these representative accounts, moral awareness grows out of human capacities rather than a separate faculty requiring revelation.

That genealogy does not settle whether its judgments are true. Railton defends naturalistic moral realism: moral assessment considers interests from a social point of view and can be corrected through feedback.<Cite source="railton-moral-realism" locator="pp. 190–194" /> Mackie's error theory denies objective values and explains their apparent objectivity through projection; moral practices can still serve the task of counteracting limited sympathy.<Cite source="mackie-ethics" locator="p. 15; pp. 42–43; p. 108" /> His position is an important alternative, not the definition of naturalism.

## What this explains well

The approach explains why moral awareness is socially patterned yet capable of revision. Our sympathy need not remain confined to its earliest circle; reflection can widen concern beyond a group.<Cite source="darwin-descent" locator="ch. IV, pp. 122–123" /> It also leaves room to distinguish the origin of a moral capacity from the philosophical justification of its verdicts. Naturalists can disagree about realism while sharing accounts of learning, cooperation, and motivational conflict. That disagreement must be represented rather than treated as an automatic refutation.

## Christian response

The Christian grants the explanatory force of social learning and sympathy. He interprets the recognition of obligation as conscience bearing witness to God's law.<Cite source="bible-esv" locator="Romans 2:14–15" /> The dispute is over what makes a standard authoritative, not whether human moral cognition has a history. Railton's realism answers that dispute differently from Mackie's error theory; criticism of one cannot simply be applied to the other. An evolutionary account alone neither establishes nor discredits moral truth. The Christian comparison must ask whether the proposed grounding sustains obligation rather than infer that naturalism cannot condemn cruelty.

## Pressure questions

- What distinguishes moral learning which tracks truth from changes which merely make a group more successful or agreeable?
- How does the proposed standard bind someone whose interests oppose it?

## Deep dive

#### Origin, truth, and reliability

Three questions often travel together but should be kept apart: how capacities evolved, which actions are right, and what moral claims mean or refer to. The checked discussion of morality and biology explicitly distinguishes these inquiries.<Cite source="sep-morality-biology" locator="§1.1" /> An evolutionary explanation of a belief can be relevant to its reliability without automatically settling its content.

This is where debunking arguments enter. If selection favoured dispositions for fitness rather than truth, a realist needs an account of why those dispositions nevertheless support trustworthy moral beliefs. Replies appeal, among other things, to reflection which is not fixed directly by the initial evolutionary shaping. The repository survey records the debate rather than certifying a settled outcome.<Cite source="sep-morality-biology" locator="§4.1" /> No new empirical conclusion about the accuracy of moral cognition is claimed here.

Railton's account supplies an instructive distinction: a person can be obligated under an impartial standard even when he lacks a personal reason to conform to it.<Cite source="railton-moral-realism" locator="p. 201" /> That preserves objectivity while leaving the relation between obligation and motivation contested. Mackie's objection is different: he rejects the objective values he thinks ordinary moral claims presuppose. Neither position follows just from Darwin's account of conscience.

The Christian's appeal to God is likewise a claim about ultimate grounding which needs defence; it is not established by finding a biological mechanism incomplete in detail.
